# Prebiotic Synthesis of Glycine: A Cross-Framework Evidence Review

Glycine can be made by more prebiotic routes than any other amino acid, and every major framework produces it. The question is not whether glycine can form abiotically but which route was geochemically realistic, and that is still contested. Two routes are best supported experimentally. The first is cyanide/aldehyde (Strecker-type) chemistry, which runs through aminoacetonitrile and is evidenced by spark discharge, HCN oligomers, cyanosulfidic chemistry, hydrothermal Strecker runs and meteorites. The second is reductive amination of glyoxylate over Fe- or Ni-bearing minerals. Neither route has an undisputed answer to where its feedstock comes from.

## TL;DR
- **Glycine is the most robust product of prebiotic chemistry in every framework.** It is the dominant or reference amino acid in Miller-type spark discharges, HCN-oligomer hydrolysates, hydrothermal Strecker runs, impact-shock runs, and in samples from Murchison-type meteorites, Ryugu, Bennu and comets. Bennu is the clearest case: glycine was its most abundant amino acid, at 44 nmol g⁻¹.\[1\]
- **The two leading chemical routes start from different feedstocks.**
  - Strecker/cyanosulfidic routes use HCN + H₂CO + NH₃ via aminoacetonitrile, and need no catalyst.
  - Reductive amination of glyoxylate (HC(O)COO⁻) + NH₃ needs a reductant: Fe²⁺, Fe(II)/Fe(III) hydroxides, ferroan brucite, FeS→Fe⁰, or H₂ over Ni/meteoritic metal.
  - Kaur et al. (2024, *Chem*) ran the second route at 22 °C, pH 8, with NH₄⁺ as low as 6 mM.\[2\]
  - Each camp's central critique is the other's strength. Cyanide routes have no clean geological source of concentrated HCN and are poorly congruent with ketoacid-based metabolism. Glyoxylate routes have no demonstrated, efficient prebiotic source of glyoxylate.
- **For network design:** treat aminoacetonitrile and glyoxylate/glycine imine as the two hub intermediates. Model the Strecker branch as a competition between amino- and hydroxy-acid products, set by [NH₃]/[HCN]. Treat the high-temperature (>200 °C) hydrothermal, atmospheric neutral-gas, and delivery-only scenarios as contested or yield-limited rather than established.

## Details

### 1. Atmospheric/spark-discharge synthesis (Miller–Urey and reanalyses)

**Chemistry.** The discharge makes HCN and aldehydes in the gas phase. These dissolve, and glycine forms in the aqueous trap by Strecker chemistry: H₂CO + NH₃ + HCN → aminoacetonitrile → glycinamide → glycine.\[9\] No catalyst is needed.

**Evidence.**
- Miller reported glycine yields of 2.1%, 1.8% and 0.4%, based on carbon added as methane, for his three apparatus.\[3\] These are summarized in Johnson, Cleaves, Dworkin, Glavin, Lazcano & Bada (2008, *Science* 322:404).
- Johnson et al. reanalyzed Miller's archived vials with modern methods. They found 22 amino acids and five amines in the "volcanic" (steam-injected) apparatus. That apparatus produced a wider variety than the classic design, at comparable or higher yields.\[10\]\[11\]
- Parker et al. (2011, *PNAS*) reanalyzed a 1958 Miller run with H₂S, CH₄, NH₃ and CO₂. It contained 23 amino acids and 4 amines, including 7 organosulfur compounds.\[12\] In both reanalyses glycine and alanine were made at much higher concentrations than any other proteinogenic amino acid.\[13\]

**Neutral atmospheres.**
- Cleaves, Chalmers, Lazcano, Miller & Bada (2008, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres* 38:105–115) argued that earlier low yields from CO₂/N₂ were partly artifacts. In their view, nitrite and nitrate made in the discharge oxidized the amino acids or their precursors.\[4\] With carbonate buffering and an oxidation inhibitor (ascorbate), they reported much higher yields.\[14\]
- **Critique:** Kuwahara et al. (2012, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*, "A cautionary note") used isotope labeling. They concluded that ascorbate "contributes amino acid contaminants" by reacting with discharge-derived nitrogen species and formate, and that "amino acids are not efficiently formed from a CO₂-rich atmosphere under the conditions studied."\[4\]
- Takeuchi et al. (2020, *Scientific Reports*) also note that Plankensteiner et al.'s neutral-gas synthesis depended on oxidation of a Cu electrode, which is not available in natural lightning.\[15\]

**Assessment.** The chemistry is not in doubt. What is contested is the geochemistry: most current models favor a weakly reducing to neutral N₂–CO₂ Hadean atmosphere, where HCN output collapses. Supporters answer that local reducing settings, such as volcanic plumes rich in reduced gases (Johnson et al. 2008) or atmospheres after impacts, could reproduce the reducing chemistry.\[11\]

### 2. Aqueous Strecker synthesis (aminoacetonitrile route)

**Steps.**
1. H₂CO + NH₃ ⇌ methanimine (CH₂=NH).
2. CH₂=NH + HCN ⇌ aminoacetonitrile (NH₂CH₂CN). This competes with H₂CO + HCN ⇌ glycolonitrile (HOCH₂CN).
3. Irreversible hydrolysis of aminoacetonitrile to glycinamide and then glycine. Glycolonitrile hydrolyzes to glycolate.

**Conditions and kinetics.** Miller and co-workers measured the equilibrium constants for forming glycine nitrile and the kinetics of its hydrolysis ("The Strecker Synthesis in the Primitive Ocean," in *Origin of Life*). They showed that the amino-acid/hydroxy-acid split can be calculated from pH, temperature and NH₃ concentration.\[16\] In practice the amino-acid branch is favored at mildly alkaline pH (~8–9), with NH₃ comparable to or above HCN.

**Catalyst.** None is required. The steps run uncatalyzed in water.

**Critiques.**
- It requires simultaneous, non-trivial steady-state concentrations of HCN, H₂CO and NH₃.
- Formaldehyde is also consumed by the formose reaction and by cyanohydrin formation.
- Glycolate competes as a by-product.
- Kaur et al. (2024) argue that "cyanide is never directly used in metabolism and is toxic" and that "its relevance to metabolic origins is questionable."\[2\] This is a congruence argument from the metabolism-first camp, not a chemical refutation.

### 3. Bucherer–Bergs/hydantoin variant

Adding CO₂ (or bicarbonate) traps the aminonitrile as a carbamate, which cyclizes to a hydantoin. Hydrolysis of the hydantoin releases the amino acid.

- Pulletikurti, Yadav, Springsteen & Krishnamurthy (2022, *Nature Chemistry* 14:1142–1150) showed that α-ketoacids react with cyanide and ammonia sources by the Bucherer–Bergs pathway to give α-amino acids, and also dihydroorotate and orotate.\[17\]
- As Kaur et al. (2024) summarize, the conditions were hydantoin formation at 20 °C followed by decarboxylative hydrolysis at 80 °C.\[2\]
- Hydantoins, including 5,5-dimethylhydantoin and 5-carboxymethylidene hydantoin, are also released when HCN oligomers are hydrolyzed (Ferris et al. 1974). Hydantoin hydrolyzes to glycine under acidic conditions.\[18\]\[19\]
- **Significance:** this is the most explicit attempt to link cyanide chemistry with ketoacid (metabolic) substrates. **Critique:** it remains cyanide-dependent.

### 4. HCN oligomer hydrolysis

- Ferris, Joshi, Edelson & Lawless (1978, *Journal of Molecular Evolution* 11:293–311) showed that dilute (0.1 M) HCN oligomerizes at pH 9.2. Hydrolysis of the oligomers releases purines, pyrimidines and amino acids.\[20\]
- Ferris, Wos, Nooner & Oró (1974, *J. Mol. Evol.*) identified glycine, aspartic acid and diaminosuccinic acid as the major amino acids.\[21\]
- In a review, Ruiz-Bermejo, Zorzano & Osuna-Esteban (2013, *Life* 3:421) found that glycine and aspartic acid were "the only amino acids that can be identified under all conditions assayed (twenty three sets of experimental conditions)." They also found that "glycine is the amino acid that is always obtained with the greatest yield."\[22\]
- Oró & Kamat (1961, *Nature* 190:442) first reported amino acid synthesis from HCN. UV irradiation of dilute HCN (Abelson) also gives glycine.\[22\]
- **Critiques:**
  - It needs HCN at ≥0.01–0.1 M. That probably requires eutectic freezing or evaporative concentration.
  - The oligomers are intractable mixtures.
  - Hydrazinolysis released only 10% of the amino acids released by acid hydrolysis, and no peptide bonds were found (Ferris et al., *J. Mol. Evol.*).\[23\] This argues against "HCN polymers as proto-proteins" (Matthews), not against glycine release.

### 5. Cyanosulfidic/systems chemistry (Sutherland)

- Patel, Percivalle, Ritson, Duffy & Sutherland (2015, *Nature Chemistry* 7:301–307) showed that precursors of ribonucleotides, amino acids and lipids can all come from reductive homologation of HCN.\[24\]
- The reductant is H₂S, with Cu(I)/Cu(II) photoredox cycling under UV, and phosphate is present.\[25\]
- Glycine forms when glycolonitrile is exposed to ammonia to give aminoacetonitrile, which is then hydrolyzed. Glycolonitrile is the HCN/formaldehyde cyanohydrin and a branch point toward glycolaldehyde.
- The proposed setting is surface streams and pools on a post-impact landscape. Ferrocyanide salts store cyanide, and different steps happen in different pools ("flow chemistry").\[26\]
- **Critiques:**
  - It needs sequential delivery of reagents and a high-UV, cyanide-rich surface environment.
  - It needs sulfide as the reductant from the start, which may belong to a later geochemical stage.\[27\]
  - It shares the cyanide-congruence objection.
- **Supporters' answer:** it is the only route shown to deliver glycine together with nucleotide and lipid precursors from a single feedstock family.

### 6. Reductive amination of glyoxylate (metal/mineral-mediated)

**Mechanism.** Glyoxylate + NH₃ ⇌ hemiaminal (α-hydroxyglycine) ⇌ glycine imine (HN=CH–COO⁻), which a metal, mineral or H₂/metal surface then reduces to glycine. Direct reduction gives glycolate instead.

- Paczelt et al. (2023, *Angewandte Chemie Int. Ed.*) isolated glycine imine for the first time. They showed that in water it hydrolyzes back to hydroxyglycine and hydrated glyoxylic acid. With FeSO₄ or at higher concentration it gives glycine, but "the NMR yield… was approximately 2–3%."\[5\]
- Mayer & Moran (2022, *Angew. Chem.*) measured inherent reactivities with BH₃CN⁻. Glyoxylate was by far the most reactive biological keto acid toward reductive amination (α-ketoglutarate < oxaloacetate ≈ pyruvate ≪ glyoxylate).\[28\] This makes glycine the kinetically easiest amino acid to obtain on this route.

**Experimental systems:**
- **Fe²⁺/Fe⁰ (Muchowska, Varma & Moran 2019, *Nature* 569:104–107).**
  - In this Fe²⁺-promoted network, pyruvate and glyoxylate build up 9 of the 11 Krebs-cycle intermediates.\[29\]
  - Adding hydroxylamine and metallic iron gave glycine, alanine, aspartate and glutamate. Reductive amination was confirmed by exposing glyoxylate, pyruvate, oxaloacetate and α-ketoglutarate to the same conditions.\[29\]\[30\]
  - The authors describe pyruvate and glyoxylate as "two products of abiotic CO₂ reduction."\[29\]
- **Iron oxyhydroxides/green rust (Barge et al.).**
  - Barge, Flores, Baum, VanderVelde & Russell (2019, *PNAS* 116:4828–4833) studied pyruvate → alanine, not glycine. Alanine was maximal (~70%) at pH 10, Fe(II):Fe(III) = 1:1 and 70 °C. Purely ferrous hydroxide gave only lactate, and ferric hydroxide gave no reaction.\[31\]
  - The glycine data are in Barge et al. (2020, *JGR Planets* 125, e2020JE006423). Conditions were 2.5 mM glyoxylate, 375 mM NH₄Cl, 70 °C and pH 7–10.\[32\]
  - At ~50% Fe(II), glycine and glycolate formed in roughly equal amounts.\[6\]
  - Without minerals, glyoxylate produced glycine slowly at NH₄Cl ≥ ~10 mM.\[6\]
  - In the same paper's run with 33% Fe(II) minerals at 70 °C, pH 10, for 1 week, "glycine and alanine can still be produced … even at much lower [NH4Cl] concentrations (5 mM)," which was the lowest concentration tested. The primary text gives no glycine percent yield at that concentration.
- **Ferroan brucite (Chimiak, Hara, Sessions & Templeton 2024, *PNAS* 121:e2408248121).**
  - Conditions: ferroan brucite (Fe₀.₃₃Mg₀.₆₇(OH)₂), 100 mM glyoxylate, 100 mM NH₄Cl or KNO₃, ~20 °C, ~1 atm, pH 10 start.\[7\]\[33\]
  - Glycine yields at 6 weeks were 52 ± 4% to 69 ± 15% with ammonia and 0.1–3.1% with nitrate.\[7\] These are relative to the starting reagents. The brucite's Fe(II) was actually the limiting reagent: it could support at most 31.25 mM amino acid in the ammonia system and 6.25 mM glycine in the nitrate system (Chimiak et al. 2024).
  - Up to 97% of the glycine in the nitrate systems was bound to the mineral and appeared only after acid digestion.\[34\]
  - By-products: glycolate, tartrate, 3-hydroxyaspartate and iminodiacetic acid.\[35\]
  - Significance: nitrate reduction and amination are coupled on a serpentinization mineral, which partly addresses where reduced nitrogen comes from.\[35\]
- **H₂ over Ni/meteoritic metal (Kaur et al. 2024, *Chem* 10:1528–1540).**
  - Conditions: H₂ at 5 bar, 22 °C, pH 8, NH₄⁺ at 6–150 mM.\[2\]\[36\]
  - Catalysts: Ni/SiO₂–Al₂O₃ (10 mol% Ni), Pd/C (1 mol%) and ground iron meteorites. Together they converted glyoxylate and six biological ketoacids into the corresponding amino acids.\[2\]\[37\]
  - Ni and meteorite catalysts gave 6–84% combined yields in a roughly 1:1 amino:hydroxy acid ratio. Pd gave 52–88% with more than 5:1 selectivity for the amino acid.\[2\]
  - There was no reduction without a metal catalyst.\[2\]
  - The authors note that earlier Fe-based work used 375 mM to 1.5 M ammonia, 62–250 times higher than their 6 mM.\[2\]
- **FeS electroreduction (Kitadai et al. 2019, *Science Advances* 5:eaav7848).**\[38\]
  - Electroreduction of FeS to Fe⁰ at about −0.55 V vs SHE could have happened at alkaline vent–seawater interfaces.\[39\] The Fe⁰ formed promotes reductive amination of α-keto acids.
  - The authors note that FeS itself promotes amination only "slightly," at pH 8–10.\[39\]
- **Cyanide as reductant.** Yadav, Pulletikurti, Yerabolu & Krishnamurthy (2022, *Nature Chemistry* 14:170–178) showed that cyanide can act as the reductant in a protometabolic reductive glyoxylate pathway.\[40\]\[41\]

**Where glyoxylate comes from (the key bottleneck):**
- Proposed sources include abiotic CO₂ reduction, though C₂ glyoxylate from CO₂ is far less established than formate, acetate or pyruvate.
- Eschenmoser's "glyoxylate scenario" derives it from HCN.
- Transamination of serine and glycine by the Krishnamurthy group gives pyruvate and glyoxylate, but this uses glycine as an input.\[42\]
- Varma et al. (2018, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*) showed native iron reducing CO₂ to acetate and pyruvate. Preiner et al. (2020) showed greigite, magnetite and awaruite (Ni₃Fe) fixing CO₂ with H₂ at 100 °C under alkaline conditions.\[2\] Neither established an efficient glyoxylate supply.
- **The glyoxylate route is therefore chemically clean but upstream-incomplete.**

### 7. Transamination

Glycine can also form nonenzymatically by transamination from another amino acid to glyoxylate. Pyridoxal/pyridoxamine systems speed this up. Kaur et al. report that pyridoxal improves nickel-catalyzed reductive amination. Schlikker et al. (2025, *FEBS Journal* 292:3041–3055) then made pyridoxamine from pyridoxal "by reaction with NH3 (as little as 5 mM) and H2 (5 bar) on Ni⁰ as catalyst at pH 11 and 80 °C within hours," under serpentinizing conditions. **Critique:** transamination redistributes nitrogen rather than fixing it. Net nitrogen still has to enter by reductive amination or Strecker chemistry.\[43\]

### 8. Hydrothermal / Fischer–Tropsch-type / serpentinization routes

- **Hydrothermal Strecker.** Hennet, Holm & Engel (1992, *Naturwissenschaften* 79:361–365) reacted HCN, CH₂O and NH₄Cl at 150 °C and 10 bar under a CO₂/H₂ (3:1) gas phase, with redox buffered by pyrite–pyrrhotite–magnetite.\[44\] Glycine reached 9.3–11.6 mM after 25–54 h, roughly 10 times alanine. These values come from Andersson & Holm 2000 (*Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere* 30:9–23, Table I), because the primary paper was not accessible.\[8\]
- **Huber & Wächtershäuser (2006, *Science* 314:630–632).** They reported CO-dependent formation of an ordered series of α-hydroxy and α-amino acids, including glycine and alanine, at 80–120 °C. Catalysts were Ni or Ni,Fe precipitates with carbonyl, cyano and methylthio ligands, with Ca(OH)₂ or Mg(OH)₂ as buffer.\[45\]
  - **Published critique:** Bada, Fegley, Miller, Lazcano, Cleaves, Hazen & Chalmers (2007, *Science* 315:937 letter) argued that the chemistry needs "a steady-state concentration of 0.1 to 0.2 M KCN at 100 °C" with "no such robust sources" known. They called 75 bar CO "implausible" by outgassing models, and argued the products are just cyanide/formaldehyde chemistry.\[46\]
  - Wächtershäuser and Huber replied in the same issue.\[46\]
  - Kitadai and co-workers similarly note that the scenario "required a high pressure of CO (1 bar or more)."\[47\]
- **Stability critique.** Bada, Miller & Zhao (1995, *Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere* 25:111–118) showed amino acids are "irreversibly destroyed by heating at 240 °C." They argued that quasi-equilibrium thermodynamic calculations, which had predicted favorable amino acid synthesis in vents (Amend & Shock 1998), "give misleading descriptions."\[48\]\[49\] Aubrey, Cleaves & Bada (2009, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres* 39:91–108) extended this and concluded that high-temperature vents are more likely sinks than sources of amino acids.\[50\]
- **Natural-sample evidence.** Ménez et al. (2018, *Nature* 564:59–63) found abiotic aromatic amino acids, chiefly tryptophan, in serpentinized harzburgite 173 m below the Atlantis Massif seafloor. They proposed Friedel–Crafts synthesis catalyzed by Fe-rich saponite. **No glycine was reported**, and the mechanism is stated with hedging ("may have been formed").\[51\]
- **Recent critique.** Tutolo (2026, *PNAS* 123(36):e2613108123, "Rethinking the origin of life at seafloor hydrothermal vents") argues that ancient Lost City-type vents would have been virtually sulfide-free. His conference abstract of the same title says that "because peridotite contains little sulfur and the ancient oceans were sulfate-poor, the elements required to produce sulfide minerals in alkaline vent chimneys would have been scarce." He also argues that complex organic synthesis there would be kinetically frustrated. Both points weaken sulfide-dependent vent scenarios.
- **Net:** moderate-temperature (20–100 °C) alkaline, serpentinizing settings are much better supported for glyoxylate amination than >200 °C black-smoker settings are for any glycine route.

### 9. Impact-shock synthesis

- Furukawa, Sekine, Oba, Kakegawa & Nakazawa (2009, *Nature Geoscience* 2:62–66) made glycine, amines and carboxylic acids by shocking solid carbon, iron, nickel, water and nitrogen/ammonia, simulating an oceanic impact. Maximum glycine yield was ~1×10⁻⁶ mol%.\[52\]\[53\]
- Suzuki et al. (2015, *Geochemistry, Geophysics, Geosystems*) reached ~1×10⁻³ mol% from ammonium formate and ~1×10⁻⁵ mol% from ammonium bicarbonate at 10–18 GPa.\[52\]
- Takeuchi et al. (2020, *Scientific Reports*) reported impact-induced amino acid formation from CO₂/N₂-derived feedstocks relevant to Hadean Earth and Noachian Mars.
- Shocked cometary-ice analogues gave glycine, alanine and others (Martins et al. 2013, *Nature Geoscience*). Ab initio molecular dynamics predicts glycine-containing complexes form during cometary impact (Goldman et al. 2010, *Nature Chemistry*).\[54\]\[55\]
- Glycine partly survives impact, though with decarboxylation to methylamine (Umeda et al. 2016, *Journal of Biological Physics*).\[56\]
- **Critique:** yields are orders of magnitude below solution chemistry. The process is episodic and destructive as well as creative.

### 10. Interstellar ice, meteorites, comets and returned samples

**Lab ice chemistry.**
- UV photolysis of H₂O/CH₃OH/NH₃/CO/HCN ices followed by warm-up and hydrolysis gives racemic amino acids including glycine (Bernstein et al. 2002 and Muñoz Caro et al. 2002, both *Nature* 416).\[57\]
- Ioppolo, Fedoseev, Chuang, Cuppen, Clements, Jin, Garrod, Qasim, Kofman, van Dishoeck & Linnartz (2021, *Nature Astronomy* 5:197–205) made glycine at ~13 K by recombining HOCO with the NH₂CH₂ radical. They report that "glycine forms without the need for energetic irradiation, such as UV photons and cosmic rays, in interstellar water-rich ices," and that "solid methylamine is an important side-reaction product."
- **Caveat** (raised in commentary): the methylamine and O₂ precursors are themselves thought to need energetic processing.\[58\]

**Small-body samples.**
- **Stardust/Wild 2:** glycine with δ¹³C = +29 ± 6‰ (Elsila, Glavin & Dworkin 2009, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science* 44:1323–1330). This was the first cometary amino acid. Co-detected ε-aminocaproic acid (δ¹³C −25‰) was Nylon-6 contamination.\[59\]
- **Rosetta/67P:** ROSINA detected volatile glycine together with methylamine and ethylamine. Glycine/H₂O ranged from 0 to 0.16% and correlated with dust (Altwegg et al. 2016, *Science Advances* 2:e1600285).\[60\] Modeling suggests the glycine was released from dust as a distributed source, at roughly 170 ppb in the nucleus (Hadraoui et al. 2019, *Astronomy & Astrophysics*).\[61\]
- **Ryugu:** 15 quantified amino acids, all approximately racemic. Glycine was 5.6 nmol g⁻¹ by 3D-HPLC versus ~0.6 nmol g⁻¹ by LC-FD/HRMS; the difference is attributed to evaporative losses (Naraoka et al. 2023, *Science* 379:eabn9033). Abundances were lower than in Orgueil and Murchison.\[62\]\[63\]
- **Bennu:**
  - Glavin, Dworkin et al. (2025, *Nature Astronomy* 9:199–210) found 33 amino acids, 14 of them proteinogenic. Glycine was most abundant at 44 nmol g⁻¹.\[64\]
  - Free ammonia was abundant, about 75 times Ryugu's.\[65\]
  - All chiral non-protein amino acids were racemic.\[66\]
  - The authors interpret the high glycine and racemic α-amino acids as indicating "HCN polymerization and/or Strecker-cyanohydrin synthesis during aqueous alteration" in NH₃-rich parent-body fluids.\[66\]\[67\]

**Interpretation.** Small-body data are strong natural evidence that aqueous Strecker/cyanohydrin chemistry makes glycine abiotically. In the parent bodies, this ran at low temperature with NH₃ and H₂CO present. Whether delivery mattered for Earth's inventory is debated. Delivered concentrations (nmol g⁻¹) are low, and impact survival is partial. Most researchers treat delivery as a supplement, or as a proof of concept for in situ chemistry.

### 11. Wet–dry, evaporitic and mineral-surface (clay, oxide) scenarios

Wet–dry cycling and clays mostly concern what happens to glycine after it forms: concentration, oligomerization to peptides, and protection. They have little to say about how it forms. Their main contribution to synthesis is concentrating feedstock. Evaporation or eutectic freezing can raise HCN/NH₃ to the 0.01–0.1 M levels that Strecker and oligomer chemistry need, and ferrocyanide evaporites store cyanide in the cyanosulfidic scenario. Oxide/hydroxide minerals (green rust, brucite, magnetite) and Fe-saponite are the best-documented mineral surfaces that actually take part in amino acid–forming steps (Sections 6 and 8). The surveyed literature contains no quantitative, clay-catalyzed de novo glycine synthesis comparable to the Fe/Ni amination data.

### 12. Photochemical and radiolytic routes

UV makes HCN and aldehydes in reducing atmospheres. UV acting on dilute HCN gives glycine (Abelson, as reviewed by Ruiz-Bermejo et al. 2013).\[22\] UV drives the photoredox steps of cyanosulfidic chemistry and the ice-photolysis syntheses. Radiolytic (ionizing-radiation) syntheses of glycine from simple ices or solutions are reported in the astrochemistry literature but were not quantitatively reviewed here. They should be treated as plausible but secondary.

## Open Questions and Challenges

1. **Feedstock flux and concentration (both camps).**
   - Strecker, HCN-oligomer and cyanosulfidic routes need concentrated HCN (≥0.01–0.1 M) alongside H₂CO and NH₃. Under a neutral CO₂–N₂ atmosphere HCN production is low. Impacts, volcanic plumes, or evaporite/ferrocyanide storage are invoked to fix this but are not quantified as steady sources.
   - Glyoxylate routes lack a demonstrated efficient abiotic glyoxylate source. NH₃ is also a problem: most Fe-based systems used 0.1–1.5 M NH₄⁺, and only Ni/H₂ (6 mM) and Barge's 5 mM tests come near plausible levels.
2. **Selectivity against hydroxy acids.** Every route has a hydroxy-acid side branch: glycolate from glycolonitrile, or from glyoxylate reduction. Ni catalysts give ~1:1 amino:hydroxy acid, while Pd gives more than 5:1. Selectivity rises with pH and NH₃ but falls at low NH₃ (Barge 2019/2020; Kaur 2024).\[31\] This trade-off should be a core parameter in any network model.
3. **Congruence with biochemistry (contested).**
   - Metabolism-first authors (Moran, Martin, Muchowska) argue that ketoacid amination mirrors biosynthesis, and that cyanide is foreign to and toxic for metabolism.
   - Cyanide-chemistry authors (Sutherland, Krishnamurthy) answer with cyanide-based routes that converge on ketoacid substrates (Bucherer–Bergs) and on co-synthesis of nucleotides and lipids.
   - Neither argument is decisive. Congruence is a heuristic, not evidence of historical pathway.
4. **Compatibility of steps.**
   - Cyanosulfidic chemistry needs spatially separated, sequential steps.
   - Fe/Ni amination networks have only recently been run as continuous one-pot networks (Kaur 2024), and with synthetic supported catalysts rather than natural minerals (meteorite powder being the exception).
   - Brucite-bound glycine (up to 97% mineral-associated) raises the question of whether the product is available downstream.\[34\]
5. **Thermal stability.** Glycine and other amino acids decompose rapidly at ≥240 °C (Bada et al. 1995; Aubrey et al. 2009).\[48\] This limits high-temperature vent scenarios to transient synthesis plus quenching.
6. **Atmospheric scenario.** Gas-phase routes depend on the oxidation state of the Hadean atmosphere. The neutral-gas yield enhancement (Cleaves et al. 2008) was challenged as an ascorbate artifact (Kuwahara et al. 2012), so neutral-atmosphere glycine synthesis remains unconfirmed.\[4\]
7. **Delivery versus in situ synthesis.** Exogenous glycine is real, confirmed by isotopes in Stardust and racemic, contamination-controlled data from Ryugu and Bennu. Its delivered concentration and impact survival are poorly constrained, though, so it is unclear whether delivery was significant for Earth.
8. **Single-laboratory results.** The ferroan brucite (2024) and Ni/H₂ (2024) results are recent and not yet independently replicated. Hennet et al. (1992) numbers are available here only through a secondary source (Andersson & Holm 2000).

## Recommendations for Synthesis-Network Design

- **Build two hub nodes.** Aminoacetonitrile is the hub for HCN/H₂CO/NH₃ inputs. Glyoxylate ⇌ hemiaminal ⇌ glycine imine is the hub for C₂-ketoacid/NH₃/reductant inputs. Hydantoin connects the two (Bucherer–Bergs, CO₂-dependent).
- **Encode the competing branches explicitly:** glycolonitrile → glycolate, glyoxylate → glycolate, and glycine + glyoxylate → iminodiacetate. Parameterize the amino/hydroxy split by pH (best near 8–10), [NH₃] and the reductant.
- **Use realistic boundaries.** Low-mM NH₃ for open-ocean scenarios. Mineral-buffered Fe(II)/Fe(III) around 1:1, or Fe(II)-brucite, for serpentinizing settings at 20–70 °C. Exclude >200 °C residence times for product accumulation.
- **Prioritize filling the upstream gaps** with flux-limited sub-models: HCN production under N₂–CO₂ atmospheres with impact or volcanic pulses, and abiotic glyoxylate formation. These, not the final glycine-forming step, decide which framework is viable.
- **Treat glycine as an expected background product.** Because glycine forms under nearly every condition tested, detecting it in a model or experiment says little about which framework is correct. Product distributions such as the alanine/glycine ratio, hydroxy-acid co-products and β-amino acids are more diagnostic.

## Caveats

- The Hennet et al. 1992 values come from a secondary source (Andersson & Holm 2000). Yield definitions differ across studies (per carbon, per limiting reagent, mol%, mM), so cross-route yield comparisons are only order-of-magnitude.
- Barge et al. 2019 (*PNAS*) is often cited for glycine but reports pyruvate → alanine.\[31\] The glyoxylate → glycine data are in Barge et al. 2020 (*JGR Planets*).\[6\]
- Ménez et al. 2018 concerns tryptophan, not glycine. It is relevant only as natural evidence that serpentinite-hosted abiotic amino acid synthesis occurs.\[51\]
- Statements about which environment "was" the site of glycine synthesis are inferences. No route is uncontested, and several of the most quantitative recent results come from single laboratories.
- Some sources could not be checked directly. The numbers for Hennet et al. 1992, Ménez et al. 2018, Barge et al. 2020 and Chimiak et al. 2024 came from a delegated search; the Barge 2020 numbers are from search snippets because its full text could not be opened. The Tutolo 2026 quote comes from his conference abstract of the same title, because the *PNAS* full text was not accessible. The UV-ice papers (Bernstein et al. 2002; Muñoz Caro et al. 2002), the Takeuchi et al. 2020 impact study and Preiner et al. 2020 are cited from secondary mentions, not read directly. The Kuwahara et al. 2012 and Bada et al. 2007 critiques were read only as search excerpts, and their author lists were not verified against the original papers.
