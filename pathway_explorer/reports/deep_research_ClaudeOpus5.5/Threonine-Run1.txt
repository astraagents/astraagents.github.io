# Prebiotic Synthesis of Threonine: A Cross-Framework Evidence Review

No published experiment has made threonine selectively, in useful yield, from simple feedstocks under conditions most researchers accept as plausible for the early Earth. Threonine shows up only as a minor, often trace, by-product of broad "shotgun" chemistry (spark discharge, ice photolysis), and the only route designed on purpose — cyanosulfidic photoredox — stops at the aminonitrile stage without a reported amino acid yield or threo:allo ratio. Threonine is the most poorly supported of the ten "early" amino acids. It is also among the least stable, which may be why it is scarce in every abiotic inventory.

## TL;DR

- **Threonine has been made abiotically, but only in trace amounts.** Spark-discharge experiments detected it at roughly 0.2 relative units, alongside equal allothreonine (Ring et al., PNAS 1972, as tabulated by Weber & Miller 1981). Of Miller's archived experiments, only the 1958 H2S-rich run yielded it (Parker et al., PNAS 2011). In UV-irradiated ice residues it appeared in only a few samples (Nuevo et al. 2008). It was not reported in neutral CO2/N2 discharges, proton irradiation, shock synthesis or Ni/Fe–CO hydrothermal chemistry.
- **Only two chemically designed routes exist, and neither is complete under prebiotic conditions.**
  - Strecker chemistry on lactaldehyde (2-hydroxypropanal) + HCN + NH3: lactaldehyde forms in 19% yield by Cu(I)/H2S/UV photoreduction of lactonitrile, and the aminonitrile was formed, but no aminonitrile yield, hydrolysis yield or threo:allo ratio was reported (Ritson & Sutherland, Angew. Chem. 2013; Patel et al., Nat. Chem. 2015).
  - Aldol condensation of glycine (as a Cu(II) complex) with acetaldehyde (Akabori chemistry): this gives up to 64% threonine + allothreonine, but as a preparative organic method rather than a prebiotic one.
  - Metabolism-first reductive-amination networks have not produced threonine.
- **Threonine is thermodynamically "expensive" and kinetically fragile.** It ranks last among the early amino acids in Gibbs-energy schemes (Higgs & Pudritz, Astrobiology 2009). It undergoes retro-aldol cleavage to glycine + acetaldehyde, dehydration/deamination and epimerization to allothreonine. For network design it is best treated as a late, low-flux product of an HCN/aldehyde (Strecker) or glycine/acetaldehyde (aldol) node, not as a hydrothermal or reductive-amination product.

## Key Findings

1. **Detection is not synthesis yield.** In Miller's archived experiments threonine is a trace product. It was present only in the H2S-rich run (CH4 258, H2S 100, NH3 250, CO2 87 mmHg, 3 days of spark). It was absent from the reanalysed classic, volcanic and silent-discharge runs.
2. **Threonine comes from C3 aldehyde chemistry, not C1 chemistry.** Every working route needs either 2-hydroxypropanal (Strecker) or acetaldehyde + glycine (aldol).\[1\] Plausible syntheses of those C2–C3 precursors are the real bottleneck.
3. **Diastereoselectivity is essentially unsolved.** Non-enzymatic routes give threo/allo mixtures. The spark discharge gave roughly 1:1. Cu-glycinate aldol gives about 1.6:1 threo:allo under classic conditions, and 1.0 to 7.7 depending on base and time.
4. **Metabolism-first chemistry has not reached threonine.** The best-performing nonenzymatic networks make glycine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate, valine, leucine and isoleucine from keto acids. Threonine is biosynthesised from aspartate via homoserine, not by reductive amination, and that pathway has no demonstrated nonenzymatic analogue.
5. **The extraterrestrial record is ambiguous.** Threonine in Murchison carries large L-excesses that have been read both as contamination and as indigenous. It is racemic and indigenous in the pristine CR2 GRA 95229. It is at or below detection limits in returned Bennu and Ryugu samples.

## Details

### 1. Atmospheric and energy-driven syntheses

**Spark discharge (reducing).**
- **Ring, Wolman, Friedmann & Miller (PNAS 1972):** a discharge on CH4 + N2 + H2O with traces of NH3. Glycine, alanine, serine, threonine, allothreonine and other amino acids were confirmed by ion-exchange chromatography and GC-MS.\[2\]
- **Weber & Miller (J. Mol. Evol. 1981):** their compilation of that dataset lists threonine at ~0.2 and allothreonine at ~0.2 relative units, with the normalisation probably glycine = 100 (not verified in the primary table). They state that "the yields of threonine and allothreonine are substantially less in the electric discharge than serine."\[3\] The roughly equal threo/allo yield fits a non-selective Strecker reaction on lactaldehyde.
- **Johnson et al. (Science 2008):** reanalysis of Miller's 1950s volcanic apparatus found 22 amino acids, including the threonine isomer homoserine.\[4\]\[5\]
- **Parker et al. (PNAS 2011):** the 1958 H2S-rich experiment yielded 23 amino acids and 4 amines, plus "amino acids such as threonine, leucine, and isoleucine, which were not detected in the other three discharge experiments."\[6\]\[7\]
  - The authors attribute the extra diversity to H2S-driven radical chemistry. They also note that Miller recorded sulfur deposits on the tungsten electrodes, which "warrants further investigation" as an influence on product ratios.\[6\]

**Neutral atmospheres.** Cleaves, Chalmers, Lazcano, Miller & Bada (Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres, 2008) showed that a CO2/N2 discharge yields substantial amino acids when oxidation inhibitors are present.\[8\] The products tabulated were aspartic acid, glutamic acid, serine, glycine, alanine, β-alanine, γ-aminobutyric acid and α-aminoisobutyric acid.\[9\] Threonine was not reported.

**Proton/cosmic-ray irradiation.**
- Kobayashi et al. (Origins of Life, 1990) irradiated CO + N2 over water with 3 MeV protons and reported various protein and non-protein amino acids after hydrolysis.\[10\]
- A 2026 ApJ Letters study by Kobayashi and co-workers on CH4–CO2–N2–H2O mixtures reported glycine as dominant, followed by aspartic acid, serine, alanine, α-aminobutyric acid and β-alanine. Only trace glycine came from CO2–N2–H2O.\[11\]
- Amino acids appeared mainly after hydrolysis, so the products are precursors, and the authors conclude that "Strecker synthesis is not the dominant formation pathway" in this regime.\[12\]
- Threonine is not among the reported products.

**Shock synthesis.** Bar-Nun, Bar-Nun, Bauer & Sagan (Science 1970) shock-heated CH4/C2H6/NH3/Ar and obtained glycine, alanine, valine and leucine at an efficiency of 5 × 10^10 molecules per erg.\[13\]\[14\] Threonine was not reported. Bar-Nun (Origins of Life, 1975) proposed an α-aminonitrile mechanism.\[15\]

**Interstellar ice photolysis.**
- Muñoz Caro et al. (Nature 2002) found 16 amino acids in UV-irradiated H2O:CH3OH:NH3:CO:CO2 ice residues.\[16\]
- Nuevo et al. (Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres, 2008) reported what they described as the first detection of threonine in such residues. While "serine was present in all the samples, threonine was detected in only a few samples, and never among the four most abundant compounds."\[17\]
- Gamma-irradiated formaldehyde/ammonia/methanol solutions gave threonine only as a "tentatively detected" product (Kebukawa and co-workers, International Journal of Astrobiology 2026).

**Interpretation.** Threonine appears only in chemically rich, sulfur- or methanol-bearing systems that produce many aldehydes. That points to formation from a C3 hydroxy-aldehyde by Strecker chemistry or radical OH addition. Parker et al. noted that hydroxylated compounds were favoured in the volcanic run, consistent with OH-radical chemistry.

### 2. Strecker / cyanide routes (lactaldehyde + HCN + NH3)

**Reaction.**
1. 2-Hydroxypropanal (lactaldehyde) + NH3 gives an imine.
2. The imine + HCN gives 2-amino-3-hydroxybutanenitrile.
3. Hydrolysis via the amide gives threonine + allothreonine.

Cobb & Pudritz (ApJ 2015) list 2-hydroxypropanal as threonine's Strecker aldehyde in their parent-body model.\[1\]

**Cyanosulfidic systems chemistry.** Ritson & Sutherland (Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2013) used:
- glycolonitrile 10 mM, H2S 30 mM, phosphate 33 mM at pH 7;\[18\]
- solid CuCN at 10 mol%;\[18\]
- 254 nm UV.\[18\]

Results:
- Acetaldehyde cyanohydrin (lactonitrile, from acetaldehyde + HCN) "furnished lactaldehyde … the Strecker precursor of (allo-)threonine, in 19% yield" after photoreduction.\[18\]\[19\]
- Adding HCN and NH3 converted lactaldehyde "smoothly" to the (allo)threonine aminonitrile.\[18\]
- No aminonitrile yield, hydrolysis to the free amino acids, or threo:allo ratio is reported in the main text.

Patel, Percivalle, Ritson, Duffy & Sutherland (Nat. Chem. 2015) placed this in a network where HCN is reductively homologated by H2S/UV with Cu(I)/Cu(II) cycling.\[20\] The α-aminonitriles of glycine, serine, alanine and threonine are described as "inevitable by-products".\[21\] Acetaldehyde itself comes from glycolaldehyde reduction in the network.\[22\]

**Precursor origin.** The full chain is HCN → glycolonitrile → glycolaldehyde → acetaldehyde → lactonitrile → lactaldehyde → aminonitrile, and each step costs yield. Lactaldehyde could in principle also come from formose-type aldol chemistry (formaldehyde + acetaldehyde, or reduction of C3 sugars), but no prebiotic demonstration specific to threonine was found.

**Critiques.**
- **Sulfide supply:** sulfite/bisulfide supply and UV attenuation limit where the chemistry could run. Ranjan, Todd, Sutherland & Sasselov (Astrobiology 2018) evaluated whether impact- or volcanism-derived sulfidic anions could reach the required concentrations.\[23\]
- **Copper:** copper is crustally scarce, and sulfide is poorly soluble except under alkaline conditions.\[24\]
- **Not congruent with metabolism:** metabolism-first proponents argue that cyanide "is never directly used in metabolism" (Kaur et al., Chem 2024).\[25\]
- **Hydrolysis:** Strecker products need harsh aminonitrile/amide hydrolysis. Recent work on N-formylaminonitriles in formamide explicitly acknowledges "potential drawbacks" of the Strecker reaction in a prebiotic context (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2023).\[26\]
- **Stereochemistry:** stereochemical control at both Cα and Cβ is absent.

### 3. Aldol routes (glycine + acetaldehyde; "Akabori-type")

**Chemistry.** Glycine is activated at Cα by chelation to Cu(II) or Co(III), or as a Schiff base, so it can add to acetaldehyde.
- Sato, Okawa & Akabori (Bull. Chem. Soc. Jpn. 1957) obtained threonine + allothreonine in 64% combined yield from copper glycinate under basic catalysis.\[27\]
- A later patent reports allo:threo of about 1:1.6 for this method, improvable only to about 1:2.4 by recrystallisation.\[28\]
- A 1973 study (J. Synth. Org. Chem. Jpn. 31:1033) found that stronger base or more catalyst raised both yield and threo/allo, which ranged from 1.0 to 7.7, because "allothreonine … decomposes or isomerizes to threonine."\[29\]
- Ikutani, Okuda, Sato & Akabori (Bull. Chem. Soc. Jpn. 1959) found that cobalt glycinate complexes gave poorer yields but higher threo/allo ratios.\[30\]
- Industrial variants run at 40 °C with HCO3⁻ resins.\[31\]

**Prebiotic-context experiments.**
- A 1983 Origins of Life paper (vol. 13, pp. 97–108) on glycine and its oligopeptides reacting with formaldehyde or acetaldehyde under mildly acidic "possible primitive earth conditions" reported "the expected serine and threonine", along with alanine, glutamic and aspartic acids, norvaline, isoleucine and four non-protein amino acids. Yields were not retrievable for this review.
- Work on hydroxy-amino-acid transformations in the same journal reported conversions of about 2% for threonine to other amino acids. It also proposed β-hydroxy-α-amino acids, made by glycine–carbonyl aldol, as a general prebiotic pathway.\[32\]
- Pyridoxal plus Al³⁺, Cu²⁺ or Fe³⁺ catalyses the same glycine + aldehyde condensation (Metzler/Snell-type model chemistry).\[33\] This is a mechanistic analogue of PLP-dependent threonine aldolase.

**Critiques.**
- High Cu(II) complex concentrations, strong base and preparative aldehyde loads are not geochemically realistic.
- The reaction is reversible, and its equilibrium favours cleavage. Threonine aldolases run physiologically in the retro direction.\[34\]
- Acetaldehyde is volatile and readily condenses with itself.

**Strength.** This is the only route that is congruent with an extant enzyme reaction (threonine aldolase, glycine + acetaldehyde), and it uses glycine, the most abundant abiotic amino acid.

### 4. Hydrothermal, Fischer–Tropsch-type and mineral routes

**Iron–sulfur world.** Huber & Wächtershäuser (Science 2006) used Ni or Ni,Fe precipitates at 80–120 °C, with CO, cyanide and methylthio ligands as carbon sources and Ca/Mg hydroxide as buffer. This gave an ordered series of α-hydroxy and α-amino acids R-CHA-COOH with R = H, CH3, C2H5 and HOCH2: glycine, alanine, α-aminobutyrate and serine, with their hydroxy-acid analogues.\[35\] Threonine (R = CH3CHOH) was not in the series. Huber, Eisenreich & Wächtershäuser extended the chemistry to 145–280 °C with Ni²⁺, cyano ligands and CO.\[36\]

Critiques:
- The syntheses required about 1 bar or more of CO.\[37\]
- Volcanic environments are inherently acidic to neutral, whereas the chemistry needs alkaline conditions. Kitadai et al. (Science Advances 2018) used this argument to propose geoelectrochemical CO production instead.\[37\]

**Reductive amination of keto acids (protometabolic).**
- Muchowska et al. (Nature 2019) added hydroxylamine and Fe⁰ to an Fe²⁺-promoted glyoxylate/pyruvate network and obtained glycine, alanine, aspartate and glutamate.\[38\]
- Kaur et al. (Chem 2024) used Ni/SiO2-Al2O3, Pd or ground iron meteorites with H2 (5 bar) at 22 °C and pH 8. They reductively aminated six biological keto acids plus glyoxylate with NH4⁺ as low as 6 mM.\[25\]
  - Alanine yields were 18–40%, and product mixtures were 6–84%.\[25\]
  - No reduction occurred without a metal catalyst.\[25\]
  - Earlier Fe-based work needed 375 mM–1.5 M NH3.\[25\]
- Threonine's α-keto acid, 3-hydroxy-2-oxobutanoate (2-oxo-3-hydroxybutyrate), has not been tested in any of these systems. Mayer & Moran (Angew. Chem. 2022) found inherent nonenzymatic selectivity among keto acids.\[39\] In biology, threonine is never made by reductive amination.

**Hydrothermal stability constraint.** Aubrey, Cleaves & Bada (Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres, 2009) argued that high-temperature vent fluids destroy amino acids faster than they form. Threonine is among the least thermally stable (see §8).

**Verdict.** No hydrothermal or mineral system has been shown to produce threonine. These routes are contested for threonine specifically, not just in general.

### 5. Biosynthesis-mimicking routes

The extant pathway is aspartate → β-aspartyl phosphate → aspartate-4-semialdehyde → homoserine → O-phospho-homoserine → threonine.\[40\] The last step uses PLP-dependent threonine synthase and involves a β,γ-elimination/addition.

Prebiotic analogues are weak at every step:
- Aspartate is hard to make because oxaloacetate is unstable. Harrison, Webb, Rammu & Lane (Life 2023) called the limited evidence for aspartate synthesis "a problem for the protometabolism hypothesis."
- Homoserine was found in Miller's volcanic samples (Johnson et al. 2008).\[6\]
- No nonenzymatic conversion of homoserine, or of O-phospho-homoserine, to threonine has been reported in the sources reviewed.

This makes biosynthetic congruence a hypothesis, not a demonstrated route.

### 6. Parent-body and extraterrestrial evidence

**Murchison (CM2).**
- Weber & Miller (1981) reported no threonine in the data then available.\[3\]
- Later analyses found L-threonine at about 700 ppb with only trace D-threonine. Koga & Naraoka (Scientific Reports 2017) read this as "suggestive of terrestrial contamination".\[41\] In their formaldehyde–ammonia hydrothermal experiments, threonine was detected only as a small L-contaminant.\[42\]
- Glavin et al. (Meteoritics & Planetary Science 2021) measured L-excesses of 20–48% for aspartic acid, glutamic acid, serine, threonine and valine in Murchison, with no L-alanine excess. They argued that a contaminant containing those amino acids but not L-alanine "is highly improbable, but cannot be ruled out," and that the excesses may have arisen during aqueous alteration.\[43\]
- **This is an explicit, unresolved conflict.**

**CR2 GRA 95229.** Pizzarello et al. (PNAS 2008) reported serine, threonine and allothreonine for the first time in a carbonaceous chondrite. Threonine there was racemic, which is the strongest evidence for indigenous meteoritic threonine.\[44\]

**Bennu.**
- Glavin et al. (Nature Astronomy 2025) reported 33 amino acids, 14 of them proteinogenic.\[45\]
- In the tabulated acid-hydrolysed extract, D- and L-threonine are each <0.2 nmol g⁻¹. Murchison's are about 1.2 (D) and 13 (L) nmol g⁻¹.\[46\]
- Mojarro et al. (PNAS 2025) analysed multiple Bennu samples and detected threonine in three of four by GC-MS.

**Ryugu.** Threonine is <0.02–0.04 nmol g⁻¹ in sample A0106 (Parker et al., Geochim. Cosmochim. Acta 2023; Naraoka et al., Science 2023).\[46\]\[47\]

**Formose-coupled aqueous alteration.** Kebukawa et al. (Science Advances 2017) heated formaldehyde, glycolaldehyde, NH3 and Ca(OH)2 at 150 °C for 72 h. This gave glycine at 278–344 μM, alanine at 479–1081 μM and β-alanine at 171–219 μM.\[48\] Threonine was not among the reported major products.

Cobb & Pudritz (ApJ 2015) used Gibbs-energy minimisation for Strecker synthesis in planetesimals. Threonine (ΔGf ≈ −505.65 kJ mol⁻¹) "is the first amino acid to break the pattern" of the rankings,\[1\] and its results were sensitive to initial conditions.\[1\]\[49\]

**Bottom line.** Indigenous extraterrestrial threonine exists but is rare. Its scarcity in the most pristine samples (Bennu, Ryugu) matches its scarcity in laboratory abiotic syntheses.

### 7. Thermodynamics and the early/late debate

Higgs & Pudritz (Astrobiology 2009) defined ten "early" amino acids, from glycine through threonine. Their observed abundance rank in meteorites and discharge experiments correlates with ΔG of formation under surface conditions. They also calculated ΔG for hydrothermal conditions (100 °C, 250 atm), which did not predict abundances as well.\[50\]\[51\]

Threonine sits at the expensive end of the early set, ranked last. Its inclusion as "early" depends on its detection in discharge experiments and some meteorites. Given the trace yields and contamination debates above, threonine is the most marginal member of the early class.

### 8. Stability and degradation

**Retro-aldol cleavage to glycine + acetaldehyde** is the main abiotic sink.
- Hydrothermal experiments at 121–160 °C show threonine converting to glycine plus peptides and polymers.\[52\]\[53\]
- Vallentyne's first-order data (113–200 °C) give Ea ≈ 141.4 kJ mol⁻¹ and A ≈ 2 × 10¹² s⁻¹.\[54\]
- Extrapolating these parameters (our calculation, not a measured value) gives half-lives of roughly 250 days at 100 °C, about 70,000 years at 25 °C, and under a second at 350 °C.
- Threonine therefore cannot accumulate in hot vent fluids, but could persist in cool surface waters.

**Dehydration/deamination to 2-oxobutanoate** (the biological threonine dehydratase reaction) occurs together with aldol cleavage and decarboxylation in fossil foraminifera.\[52\] Schroeder & Bada (Geochim. Cosmochim. Acta 1977) studied this in their work on threonine epimerisation and decomposition.\[55\]

**Epimerization at Cα** converts L-threonine to D-allothreonine. This blurs diastereomer signatures and can create apparent allothreonine from contaminant L-threonine.\[41\]

**Radiolysis and oxidation.**
- Threonine is among the more radiosensitive amino acids in γ-irradiated solution.\[56\]
- OH-radical rate constants are about 10⁹–10¹⁰ L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ (Wen et al., Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys. 2022).\[57\]
- Minerals, chiefly MnO2, also convert threonine to HCN, at above 0.01 mM after 24 h (Yang et al., PNAS 2026). This is a sink for threonine but a possible source of HCN.

### 9. Analytical caveats

- **Co-elution:** with OPA/NAC derivatisation and UV-fluorescence detection, co-elution causes "small, but nonnegligible loss in quantitative accuracy" (Parker et al. 2011).\[6\] Threonine elutes near alanine, β-alanine and glycine on common methods, so mass-spectrometric confirmation is required.
- **Diastereomers:** threonine and allothreonine must be resolved separately (ion exchange or chiral GC/LC). Older paper-chromatography data cannot do this.
- **Contamination:** L-enrichment of serine, aspartic acid and glutamic acid in the Miller H2S residues signals trace contamination.\[58\] Threonine is a common contaminant (fingerprints, proteins), so D/L ratio, compound-specific δ¹³C/δD and procedural blanks are essential.
- **Free versus bound:** many reports measure only after acid hydrolysis, so threonine may come from precursors (aminonitriles, hydantoins, peptides) rather than being present free.

## Recommendations for synthesis-network design

1. **Model threonine as a two-node product:** lactaldehyde + HCN + NH3 (Strecker) and glycine + acetaldehyde (aldol). Give it low flux and a fast sink (retro-aldol to glycine + acetaldehyde) that grows steeply with temperature.
2. **Treat acetaldehyde and lactaldehyde supply as the rate-limiting nodes.** The best-documented abiotic source is the Cu/H2S/UV homologation sequence: glycolaldehyde → acetaldehyde, then lactonitrile → lactaldehyde (19%).
3. **Do not assign threonine to hydrothermal or reductive-amination modules** unless new data appear. The highest-value experiment would be to test 3-hydroxy-2-oxobutanoate in the Ni/H2 or Fe⁰/NH2OH systems.
4. **Track threo/allo explicitly.** Assume roughly 1:1 for Strecker, and roughly 1.6:1 or more (base-dependent) for metal-chelate aldol. Biological L-threonine selection must come from somewhere else.
5. **Favour cool (<50 °C), mildly alkaline surface waters for accumulation.** Avoid high-temperature residence. Threonine's extrapolated half-life drops from about 10⁴–10⁵ years at 25 °C to under a year at 100 °C.

## Caveats

- Several key quantitative values come from secondary compilations or abstracts rather than primary tables:
  - Ring et al. 1972 relative yields, via Weber & Miller 1981;
  - the threo:allo ratios for Akabori chemistry, via patents and a 1967 review;
  - yields from the Origins of Life glycine + acetaldehyde study, which could not be retrieved.
- Threonine's absence from neutral-discharge, proton-irradiation, shock and Ni–CO product lists may reflect analytical targeting rather than true absence.
- The half-lives in §8 are Arrhenius extrapolations, not measurements.
- Interpretation of meteoritic L-threonine excesses remains genuinely contested.
