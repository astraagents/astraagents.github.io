# Prebiotic Synthesis of Arginine: A Critical, Cross-Framework Review

No published experiment has shown free arginine forming from simple feedstocks under prebiotically plausible conditions with unambiguous modern analysis. The best-developed route, Sutherland-group cyanosulfidic chemistry, gets as far as the arginine α-aminonitrile (or a guanidinylated precursor of it). It does not report hydrolysis to arginine. The other "arginine" claims are either old amino-acid-analyzer detections in HCN-polymer hydrolysates that later GC-MS work did not reproduce, or conversions of an already-made ornithine scaffold to arginine. On the current evidence, arginine is a "late" or "difficult" amino acid. The bottleneck is not the guanidino group itself, since cyanamide, guanidine and urea are readily formed. It is the C5 carbon skeleton with a terminal δ-amine and α-amino acid function, plus selective δ-guanidinylation.

## TL;DR
- **No route goes all the way to arginine under defended prebiotic conditions.** Patel et al. 2015 (*Nature Chemistry*) report chemistry from HCN, acetylene, cyanamide, H2S and Cu(I) under UV light that yields guanidinylated nitrile/aldehyde intermediates. A 45 → 46 step to the arginine α-aminonitrile is tabulated at 90%. But the authors only *infer* that 46 would form in the integrated network, and they never report arginine itself.
- **Claims of arginine in HCN polymers, spark discharges and meteorites are weak or negative.** Arginine appears only in Matthews & Moser 1967 (*Nature*) HCN-polymer hydrolysates, measured by amino acid analyzer. Ferris and co-workers' GC-MS reanalyses (1973–1974) did not confirm it. Modern reanalyses of Miller's spark-discharge archives found ornithine but not arginine. Carbonaceous chondrites consistently lack arginine.
- **Guanidinylating ornithine is the most tractable endgame, but it is mostly demonstrated with non-prebiotic reagents or at the peptide level.** Longo et al. 2020 (*PNAS*) converted ornithine side chains to arginine in a synthetic protein by chemical guanidination and argue that cyanamide could do this. An efficient, selective δ-guanidinylation of free ornithine by cyanamide in water, with yields, has not been published to my knowledge. Theoretical "late arginine" arguments (Weber & Miller 1981; Higgs & Pudritz 2009; Trifonov 2000) are themselves contested.

## 1. Known Prebiotic Synthesis Routes (with classification)

### 1.1 Cyanosulfidic chemistry (Sutherland group)
Patel, Percivalle, Ritson, Duffy & Sutherland (2015, *Nature Chemistry* 7:301–307) showed that "precursors of ribonucleotides, amino acids and lipids can all be derived by the reductive homologation of hydrogen cyanide and some of its derivatives."\[1\] The arginine branch works like this:
1. Cu(I)-catalysed cross-coupling of HCN with acetylene gives **acrylonitrile (33)**.\[2\]
2. Conjugate addition of NH3 gives **β-aminopropionitrile (34)**. The authors note this is "a potential precursor of proline and lysine if the amino group of 34 was left free, and arginine if the amino group of 34 could somehow be guanidinylated."\[2\]
3. Treating 34 with **cyanamide (2)** converts it to the guanidinylated nitrile **35**, "but the reaction is relatively inefficient with the result that 35 is generated in admixture with residual 34 and cyanamide 2."\[2\]
4. H2S-driven photoreduction of 34 gives **β-aminopropionaldehyde (36)** (45% in one step, per the paper's yield table). Residual cyanamide reacts very efficiently with 36 to give adduct **37** (34 → 37, two steps, 77%).\[2\]
5. Further reductive homologation through thioamide and cyanohydrin (45) intermediates, then addition of ammonia (Strecker), gives **46, "the α-aminonitrile precursor of arginine"**. The yield table lists 45 → 46 at 90%.\[2\]

Crucially, the authors frame the integrated outcome conditionally. They say the efficient 36 + cyanamide reaction "suggests that 46, the α-aminonitrile precursor of arginine, would have been produced alongside 47, the corresponding precursor of proline, if cyanamide 2 was present along with ammonia when acrylonitrile 33 was generated." The paper also judges chain extension to the lysine Strecker precursor "unlikely."\[2\] **Classification: (b).** The furthest point is the arginine aminonitrile, made stepwise. One-pot formation within the network is inferred, and hydrolysis to arginine is not reported.

### 1.2 HCN polymers and ammonium cyanide
In the review by Ruiz-Bermejo, Zorzano & Osuna-Esteban (2013, *Life* 3:421–448), both arginine entries trace to **Matthews & Moser (1967, *Nature* 215:1230)**. The conditions were (i) liquid HCN with anhydrous NH3 at room temperature for 4 weeks, and (ii) 1.5 M HCN with NH3 refrigerated for 4 days. The dark solids were acid-hydrolysed (6 N HCl, ~100–110 °C, 16–24 h) and analysed with an automatic amino acid analyzer.\[3\] No quantitative arginine yield is given in the review. Ornithine was reported only from "hot" experiments (90 °C, 4 h) by Labadie, Jensen & Neuzil (1967, *Bull. Soc. Chim. Biol.*; 1968, *Biochim. Biophys. Acta*), from 1 M NH4CN and from 1 M HCN/NH4OH at pH 9.58.\[3\]

Later GC-MS work did not confirm arginine. Ferris, Wos, Nooner & Oró (1974, *J. Mol. Evol.* 3:225) found that the major amino acids released on hydrolysis of HCN oligomers were "Gly, Asp, and diaminosuccinic acid," with "smaller amounts of Ala, Ile and α-aminoisobutyric acid," and Glu from neutral oligomers.\[4\]\[5\] Ferris, Donner & Lobo (1973, *J. Mol. Biol.* 74:499) found citrulline as a major hydrolysis product and no enzymatically detectable peptide bonds.\[6\] Lowe, Rees & Markham (1963, *Nature* 199:219), from ammonium cyanide at 90 °C, did not list arginine.\[3\] **Assessment:** the 1967 arginine detection is single-lab, analyzer-based (prone to co-elution) and unreplicated. It should be treated as disputed. One untested possibility is that guanidino/ureido species in the hydrolysate are degradation-related artefacts; this is my inference, not a published conclusion. The same polymers *do* yield guanidine, guanidinoacetic acid, urea and cyanamide (Ruiz-Bermejo et al. 2013), so the guanidino group is available in HCN chemistry.\[3\]

### 1.3 Spark discharge and Strecker chemistry
Miller's original 1953 experiments did not report arginine. Modern HPLC/LC-MS reanalyses of archived samples found broader inventories. Johnson et al. (2008, *Science* 322:404) identified 22 amino acids and five amines in the "volcanic" apparatus. Parker et al. (2011, *PNAS* 108:5526) found 23 amino acids and 4 amines in a 1958 H2S/CH4/NH3/CO2 experiment.\[7\]\[8\] The volcanic samples contained **ornithine**, "many of which had not been reported previously in spark discharge experiments."\[9\] Arginine was not reported in any of these reanalyses. Miller (1987/1986 reviews; PubMed 11542054) summarized that reasonable prebiotic syntheses exist for the protein amino acids "with the exception of lysine, arginine and histidine."\[10\] The Strecker precursor aldehyde for arginine, N-(4-oxobutyl)guanidine (used computationally by Cobb, Pudritz & Pearce 2015, *ApJ*), has no demonstrated prebiotic source.\[11\] **Classification: (b) for ornithine; nothing for arginine.**

### 1.4 Guanidinylation of ornithine (and ornithine peptides)
Longo et al. (2020, *PNAS* 117:15731, Tawfik group) built a functional (HhH)2 nucleic-acid-binding protein from ornithine. They then showed that "a simple chemical reaction, guanidination, performed in water can convert the ornithine side chains of a chemically synthesized protein into arginine side chains," improving structure and dsDNA binding. They argue guanidination "can also be performed chemically, even by simple reagents such as cyanamide (H2N-CN), which were likely present in primordial environments."\[12\] The demonstration used a standard laboratory guanidinylating reagent, not cyanamide. The cyanamide claim rests on synthetic-chemistry precedent (Alonso-Moreno et al. 2014).\[13\] This "statistical ornithine-to-arginine conversion" hypothesis implies that arginine may first have arisen as a post-translational modification rather than a free monomer.\[14\] Thoma (2023, UCL PhD thesis, Powner group) reports "a δ-selective synthesis of arginine peptides from ornithine monomers" using isothiouronium guanidinylation coupled to peptide ligation.\[15\] This is a thesis, not yet peer-reviewed as far as found. **Classification: (a) from ornithine**, contingent on a prebiotic ornithine source.

A complication comes from Thoma & Powner (2023, *JACS* 145:3121). They found that ornithine (and diaminobutyric acid) "cannot form peptides due to rapid and quantitative cyclization that irreversibly blocks peptide synthesis," whereas lysine thioacids couple "in near-quantitative yield."\[16\] Ornithine's δ-lactamization therefore works against ornithine-peptide-first scenarios, unless guanidinylation precedes or accompanies ligation, which the thesis work proposes.

### 1.5 Biosynthesis-mimicking (urea-cycle) route
In extant biology, ornithine is carbamoylated by carbamoyl phosphate to citrulline (OTC). Citrulline is condensed with aspartate (ATP) to argininosuccinate and cleaved to arginine + fumarate.\[17\] Carbamoyl phosphate forms chemically from phosphate and cyanate, and cyanate is a strong carbamoylating agent.\[18\] Non-enzymatic ornithine → citrulline is therefore chemically straightforward. However, no prebiotic study demonstrating citrulline → arginine (an amidination requiring activation and an N donor) was found. Citrulline also appears in HCN-oligomer hydrolysates (Ferris et al. 1973), which links HCN chemistry to the ureido analog.\[6\] **Classification: (c).**

### 1.6 Metabolism-first / mineral-catalysed routes
Huber & Wächtershäuser (FeS/Fe(OH)2), Barge et al. (2019, *PNAS*, iron oxyhydroxides and pH gradients), Kitadai et al., and Muchowska, Varma & Moran (2019, *Nature* 569:104) demonstrated Fe- and Ni-mediated reductive amination or transamination for glycine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate and related acids.\[19\] Mayer & Moran (2022, *Angew. Chem.*) quantified nonenzymatic reductive amination for α-ketoglutarate, oxaloacetate, pyruvate and glyoxylate only, with reactivity "α-ketoglutarate < oxaloacetate ≈ pyruvate ≪ glyoxylate."\[20\] Stubbs et al. (2020, *Nature Chemistry*) showed metal-free α-ketoacid chemistry with transamination by glycine.\[21\] **No study found tested 5-guanidino-2-oxopentanoate (the arginine keto acid) or 2-oxo-5-aminopentanoate.** Nor did any study generate the ornithine skeleton from glutamate semialdehyde under protometabolic conditions. **Classification: (c).** I found no FeS/vent, alkaline-vent, formamide (Saladino/Di Mauro), or interstellar-ice-photolysis study reporting arginine. The literature appears silent there, but this was not exhaustively verified.

## 2. Key Intermediates and Precursors
- **C1/N feedstocks:** HCN, NH3, cyanamide (H2NCN), urea, guanidine. Lohrmann (1972, *J. Mol. Evol.*) showed that aqueous ammonium cyanide "yield[s] urea, cyanamide and guanidine when exposed to sunlight or an unfiltered 254 nm ultraviolet source."\[22\]
- **C2–C3 building blocks (cyanosulfidic):** acetylene, acrylonitrile, β-aminopropionitrile, β-aminopropionaldehyde.\[23\]
- **Guanidinylated intermediates:** guanidinylated β-aminopropionitrile (35); N-(4-oxobutyl)guanidine (the putative Strecker aldehyde); the arginine α-aminonitrile 46 (2-amino-5-guanidinopentanenitrile; the Wikipedia summary of Patel et al. lists it as "amino((4-amino-4-cyanobutyl)amino)methaniminium").\[24\]
- **Ornithine-branch intermediates:** ornithine (spark discharge; HCN polymers, hot), citrulline (HCN oligomers), carbamoyl phosphate/cyanate, glutamate semialdehyde (biosynthetic; no prebiotic synthesis found).
- **Keto-acid analog:** 5-guanidino-2-oxopentanoate (untested).

## 3. Reaction Mechanisms and Conditions
- **Cyanosulfidic:** aqueous, UV (254 nm), HS⁻/H2S as stoichiometric photoreductant, Cu(I)/Cu(II) photoredox cycling, near-neutral phosphate buffer. The proposed setting is a surface "flow chemistry" scenario with impact-delivered or volcanic sulfide and cyanide. Ranjan, Todd, Sutherland & Sasselov (2018, *Astrobiology*; arXiv 1801.07725) evaluated whether sufficient sulfidic anion concentrations are plausible, noting the chemistry "requires sulfidic anions to proceed, as both a photoreductant and as a feedstock."\[1\]\[25\]
- **HCN polymer:** concentrated HCN (1–1.5 M or neat), NH3, room temperature to 90 °C, days to weeks, then harsh acid hydrolysis (6 N HCl, 100–110 °C).\[3\] The arginine-producing step (if real) happens either during polymerization or during hydrolysis. That is unresolved.
- **Spark discharge:** reduced gases (CH4/NH3/H2/H2O ± H2S, CO2), electrical energy, aqueous Strecker chemistry in the condensate. Ornithine yields are only relative to glycine and low.
- **Guanidinylation:** aqueous amine + cyanamide or isothiourea. Cyanamide guanidinylation of aliphatic amines is generally slow without activation (Patel et al. call it "relatively inefficient").\[2\] In synthetic chemistry, Tsubokura et al. (2014, *Synlett* 25:1302) guanylated amines with cyanamide in water using catalytic Sc(OTf)3, and "the method is applied to the guanylation of (S)-ornithine and an ornithine-containing peptide." Activated reagents are the other common option.

## 4. Catalysts and Surfaces
- **Cu(I)/Cu(II):** experimentally required in the cyanosulfidic network (acetylene + HCN coupling; photoredox).\[1\] The cyanamide guanidinylation of 34 and the 36 + cyanamide reaction proceed without added metal catalysts.
- **Phosphate:** a buffer and general catalyst in cyanosulfidic chemistry.\[24\]
- **No catalyst:** HCN polymerization (base/NH3 only); Strecker aminonitrile formation; UV formation of urea, cyanamide and guanidine from NH4CN.
- **Fe, Ni, FeS, iron oxyhydroxides:** shown for other keto acids only. No arginine-relevant demonstration found.
- **Sc(III) triflate:** an effective aqueous guanylation catalyst in synthetic chemistry.\[26\] It has no prebiotic relevance and is listed only as a mechanistic precedent.

The meteoritic absence of arginine is strong negative evidence for abiotic parent-body formation. Glavin et al. (2021) note that 96 amino acids have been named in Murchison, and its absence is routinely used as a contamination check.\[27\]\[28\] Meteoritic guanidine-type compounds are reported but contested. Hayatsu et al. (1975, *GCA*) reported guanylurea (30–45 ppm) and urea (25 ppm) under harsh HCl extraction.\[29\] Folsome et al. (1971, 1973) did not detect guanylurea with milder extraction.\[30\]

## 6. Environmental Context
No environment is well supported specifically for arginine, because no complete synthesis exists. Conditionally:
- **Surface UV-irradiated, cyanide- and sulfide-rich streams or pools (cyanosulfidic):** the only scenario with experimental intermediates on a defined arginine path. Its support depends on the availability of HCN, acetylene, cyanamide, H2S and Cu, which Ranjan et al. (2018) treat as plausible only in local settings. On the sulfide limitation, Xu, Ritson, Ranjan, Todd, Sasselov & Sutherland (2018, *Chem. Commun.* 54:5566) replaced HS⁻ with sulfite plus ferrocyanide, reporting that "on their own, neither sulfite nor ferrocyanide are efficient sources of photochemically-generated electrons … but together they are." Ranjan et al. (2018) found SO2-derived anions available "for all but the largest volcanic episodes." Copper's low crustal abundance remains a limitation.
- **HCN-polymer-rich settings (ponds, impact-generated HCN, cometary material):** supply guanidine, urea and cyanamide, and ornithine at 90 °C. Arginine evidence is weak.
- **Reducing-atmosphere lightning / volcanic plumes:** supply ornithine, not arginine. Bulk early-atmosphere models (CO2/N2) reduce yields.
- **Hydrothermal vents (FeS/alkaline):** no experimental data for arginine or its keto acid. Amend & Shock (1998, *Science* 281:1659) tabulate the net reaction "6CO2(aq) + 4NH4+ + 11H2(aq) → arginine+ + 3H+ + 10H2O" and report that "the value of ΔGr is negative for 11 of the 20 net amino acid synthesis reactions in a 100°C mixed hydrothermal solution." This is a thermodynamic argument only, with no kinetic pathway or experiment.
- **Meteoritic delivery:** effectively ruled out as a source of arginine by its absence in CM2 chondrites.

## 7. Open Questions, Critiques and Bottlenecks
1. **Skeleton construction (main bottleneck).** A C5 chain with an α-amino acid at one end and a δ-nitrogen at the other needs either acrylonitrile-based homologation (cyanosulfidic) or an unsourced aldehyde (N-(4-oxobutyl)guanidine or 4-aminobutanal). Spark/Strecker chemistry gives ornithine only at trace relative levels.
2. **Selective δ-guanidinylation.** Cyanamide guanidinylation of primary amines is "relatively inefficient" in water (Patel et al. 2015).\[2\] Cyanamide reacts competitively with aldehydes (the 36 → 37 adduct), with itself (dimerization to dicyandiamide), and with α-amines. Patel et al. also show that the very efficient cyanamide–aldehyde reaction diverts material. Whether it helps or hurts arginine depends on the ordering of steps.
3. **Hydrolysis and stability.** Arginine's guanidino group hydrolyses to citrulline and then ornithine under strong acid or base and heat.\[31\] This complicates both the 6 N HCl hydrolysis used to "detect" arginine in HCN polymers and any hot-environment accumulation.
4. **Compatibility of steps.** Cyanosulfidic arginine requires cyanamide and ammonia to be present together when acrylonitrile forms, while other branches of the same network need different conditions. This is the "flow chemistry" contingency critics highlight.\[24\] The authors themselves describe the required sequencing as conditional.
5. **Congruence with biochemistry.** The biological route (glutamate → ornithine → citrulline → arginine) has no demonstrated non-enzymatic counterpart beyond carbamoylation. Proponents of protometabolism (Moran, Krishnamurthy/Springsteen) have not yet tested arginine-pathway keto acids. Ornithine-peptide-first scenarios (Longo et al. 2020) conflict with ornithine's rapid lactamization (Thoma & Powner 2023).
6. **Is arginine "late"? (contested theory).** Weber & Miller (1981, *J. Mol. Evol.*) argued arginine and lysine are the best basic amino-acid choices *functionally*. They also discussed the idea that codons for arginine originally coded for ornithine.\[32\]\[33\] Higgs & Pudritz (2009, *Astrobiology*) restrict the ten "early" amino acids to those formed in Miller-type experiments and meteorites, ranked by formation free energy, which places arginine late. Their early group averages ΔG_surf of 169.3 ± 42.0 kJ/mol versus 285.0 ± 94.1 kJ/mol for the late group (r = 0.96 for the early ten). They describe K, F, R and H as "not found in the Miller experiments or in meteorites and occur only in one or two of the other experiments." They also concede "there is no correlation between R_obs and ΔG_hydro," so the thermodynamic ranking does not hold under hydrothermal conditions. Trifonov's (2000, *Gene*) consensus order (G/A, V/D, P, S, E/L, T, R, …) actually places R *mid-sequence*.\[34\] Wehbi et al. (2024, *PNAS*) classify R among "late" amino acids from LUCA depletion, but they report that Trifonov's consensus has "no additional predictive power" once molecular size is accounted for.\[35\] On the other side, the Yarus group ("Simple, recurring RNA binding sites for L-arginine," *RNA* 2010) found 131 independently derived arginine binding sites and reported that "an arginine coding triplet (codon/anticodon) is highly conserved within the largest family of Arg sites (72% of all sequences)." This is cited as evidence that arginine–RNA interaction could have been early.

## Recommendations for Network Design
- Treat **arginine as reachable only through (i) the acrylonitrile/cyanamide cyanosulfidic branch or (ii) post-hoc guanidinylation of ornithine (free or peptide-bound)**. Model both, and flag the 45 → 46 → Arg hydrolysis step as unverified.
- Prioritize experiments that are missing from the literature: (1) cyanamide δ-guanidinylation of free ornithine in water (pH, temperature, yields, α/δ selectivity); (2) a one-pot acrylonitrile + NH3 + cyanamide + H2S/Cu/UV run to 46 and hydrolysis to arginine; (3) modern LC-MS reanalysis of HCN-polymer hydrolysates for arginine versus citrulline/ornithine artefacts; (4) Fe/Ni-mediated reductive amination of 5-guanidino-2-oxopentanoate.
- Do not use Matthews & Moser 1967 as evidence of HCN-polymer arginine without caveat.

## Caveats
- I could not access the full text or SI of Patel et al. 2015. Yields (34 → 37 77%; 34 → 36 45%; 45 → 46 90%) come from text excerpts of the author manuscript's yield table, and exact conditions are not reported here.
- Primary data of Matthews & Moser 1967 and Labadie et al. were not directly inspected. Attributions rely on Ruiz-Bermejo et al. 2013.
- Searches did not specifically cover formamide, interstellar ice photolysis or hydrothermal FeS studies for arginine. "No report found" there reflects limited search, not a verified absence.
- The Thoma 2023 arginine-peptide result is from a PhD thesis, not peer-reviewed literature.
