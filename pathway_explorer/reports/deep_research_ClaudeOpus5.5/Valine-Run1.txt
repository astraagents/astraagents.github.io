# Prebiotic Synthesis of Valine: A Cross-Framework Evidence Review

Valine is one of the most robustly "prebiotically accessible" proteinogenic amino acids at the level of detection — it appears in spark-discharge products, carbonaceous chondrites, asteroid-return samples and interstellar-ice photolysis residues — but no single route yet delivers it selectively, at high yield, from simple feedstocks under one set of geochemically defended conditions. The best-characterized end-game steps are (i) Strecker chemistry on isobutyraldehyde and (ii) metal-catalyzed reductive amination of α-ketoisovalerate (α-KIV); the critical bottleneck in every framework is the prebiotic origin of the branched C4/C5 skeleton (isobutyraldehyde or α-KIV), and the biosynthetic pyruvate → acetolactate → α-KIV sequence has no published nonenzymatic demonstration.

## TL;DR

- **Valine forms easily but non-selectively.** It has been confirmed in spark-discharge products (Ring et al. 1972, PNAS; Johnson et al. 2009, OLEB reanalysis of Miller's original samples), in Murchison and other CM chondrites, in Bennu samples (Glavin et al. 2025, Nature Astronomy), and in UV-irradiated interstellar ice analogues (Muñoz Caro et al. 2002, Nature) — always as a minor, racemic component accompanied by its isomers norvaline and isovaline.
- **Two end-game chemistries are experimentally solid; the skeleton is not.** Isobutyraldehyde + HCN + NH3 (Strecker) and α-KIV + NH3 + H2 over Ni, Pd or ground iron meteorite (Kaur et al. 2024, Chem; 22 °C, pH 8, no reaction without catalyst) both give valine, but how isobutyraldehyde or α-KIV arise prebiotically is either demonstrated only in a multistep, operator-staged network (Patel et al. 2015, Nature Chemistry: acetone → valine aminonitrile via HCN/H2S/UV/Cu) or not demonstrated at all (nonenzymatic acetolactate pathway).
- **All routes are contested.** Spark discharge depends on reducing atmospheres; cyanosulfidic chemistry on high sulfide, 254-nm UV and staged reagent delivery; metabolism-first routes on a supply of α-KIV that nobody has made abiotically; and extraterrestrial delivery supplies only nmol/g-level valine. For network design, treat valine as a downstream "skeleton-limited" target and prioritize finding a prebiotic source of the C5 branched skeleton.

## Details

### 1. Spark-discharge / Miller-Urey-type experiments

**What was shown.** Ring, Wolman, Friedmann and Miller (1972, *PNAS* 69:765) ran electric discharges on CH4 + N2 + H2O with traces of NH3 and confirmed glycine, alanine, α-amino-n-butyric acid, α-aminoisobutyric acid, **valine, norvaline, isovaline**, leucine, isoleucine, alloisoleucine, norleucine, proline, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, serine, threonine and others.\[1\]\[2\] This is the classic demonstration that the hydrophobic branched amino acids form alongside their unbranched isomers — valine is produced together with norvaline and isovaline, so any analysis must resolve the C5 isomers chromatographically.

**Archived-sample reanalyses.** After Stanley Miller's death in 2007, Bada and colleagues found his dried 1950s residues. Johnson et al. (2008, *Science* 322:404) showed that the "volcanic" apparatus (an aspirator injecting steam into the spark flask) gave 22 amino acids and five amines, more diverse than the classic apparatus. Johnson et al. (2009, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres* 39:240) reported that the original samples contain several aminobutyric acid isomers plus serine, homoserine, isoserine, isovaline, **valine**, phenylalanine, ornithine and others, and that both diversity and yield increased with the water-aspirating device. Parker et al. (2011, *PNAS*) analyzed an unpublished 1958 H2S-rich experiment (CH4, NH3, H2S, CO2), reporting the first sulfur amino acids from spark experiments and the highest relative yields of aminobutyric acid isomers ever found in a spark experiment; that paper's emphasis is on sulfur and C4 amino acids, and this review does not rely on it for a valine-specific yield.\[3\]

**Conditions and yields.** Ambient temperature, 1 bar-scale gas mixtures, electrical energy; no catalyst. Schlesinger and Miller (1983, *Journal of Molecular Evolution*) showed that total amino acid yields are 1.2–4.7% (based on carbon) for CH4-containing mixtures largely independent of H2/CH4 ratio, about 2% for H2 + CO or H2 + CO2 mixtures at H2/C ratios of 2–4, but only ~10⁻³% when H2/CO2 = 0.\[4\] Higher amino acids such as valine are a small fraction of the total.

**Mechanism.** Discharge produces HCN, aldehydes and ketones in the gas phase; amino acids form in the aqueous trap by Strecker (aldehyde + HCN + NH3) and related cyanohydrin chemistry. Valine thus derives from isobutyraldehyde formed by gas-phase radical recombination.

**Critiques.** The main objection is atmospheric redox state: if the Hadean atmosphere was CO2/N2-dominated, yields collapse (the 10⁻³% figure above). Defenders argue that volcanic plumes (Johnson et al. 2008) or transiently H2-rich post-impact atmospheres provide local reducing conditions. A second critique is selectivity: valine is a minor product in a broad mixture with non-proteinogenic isomers.

### 2. Strecker synthesis from isobutyraldehyde — and the origin of isobutyraldehyde

**Chemistry.** Isobutyraldehyde (2-methylpropanal) + NH3 + HCN → 2-amino-3-methylbutanenitrile (valine α-aminonitrile) → hydrolysis → racemic valine. No catalyst is required; the reaction proceeds in water at ambient temperature, with the aminonitrile/cyanohydrin partition controlled by NH3/HCN ratios and pH. Ruiz-Bermejo et al. (2013, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*) used aqueous aerosols with UV and ferrocyanide or Prussian Blue as the cyanide source to perform Strecker syntheses of alanine, valine and phenylalanine, presenting ferrocyanides as a way to store cyanide in an Fe(II)-bearing ocean.

**Competing sinks for isobutyraldehyde.** Isobutyraldehyde is also the precursor of pantoic acid: Miller and Schlesinger (1993, *Journal of Molecular Evolution*) reported that pantoic acid forms in good prebiotic yield from isobutyraldehyde (or α-KIV) + H2CO + HCN. Fairchild et al. (2024, *Science*; Powner group) showed a phosphate-catalyzed, neutral-pH aldol of formaldehyde with isobutyraldehyde — the Strecker precursors of glycine and valine — selectively leading to pantolactone/pantetheine. This shows that, in a mixture rich in formaldehyde, isobutyraldehyde is partly diverted from valine toward coenzyme A precursors; network designers should model this branch explicitly.

**Critiques.** Strecker chemistry itself is uncontroversial; the open question is the supply of isobutyraldehyde. Proposed sources include spark/plume chemistry (above), the cyanosulfidic homologation of acetone (below), FTT chemistry, and decarboxylation of α-KIV (which presupposes α-KIV). Dilute HCN is a further problem: Stribling and Miller (1987, *Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere* 17:261) calculated, for an HCN production rate of 100 nmol cm⁻² yr⁻¹, a steady-state primitive-ocean HCN concentration of about 4 × 10⁻⁶ M at pH 8 and 0 °C, pushing Strecker chemistry toward evaporitic, eutectic-freezing or ferrocyanide-storage scenarios.

### 3. Cyanosulfidic / systems-chemistry route (Sutherland group)

**What was shown.** Patel, Percivalle, Ritson, Duffy and Sutherland (2015, *Nature Chemistry* 7:301) derived precursors of ribonucleotides, amino acids and lipids by reductive homologation of HCN and its derivatives. Valine is accessible: dihydroxyacetone reduction gives acetone; acetone + HCN gives a cyanohydrin; reaction with H2S gives an α-hydroxythioamide; Cu(I)/Cu(II) photoredox cycling under 254-nm UV with HS⁻ as stoichiometric reductant removes the hydroxyl to give the isobutyraldehyde-level product, which is trapped as a cyanohydrin and converted by NH3 to the **valine α-aminonitrile**. The authors demonstrated the conversion of the constitutionally related intermediate to the valine aminonitrile by addition of ammonia and noted that a further homologation cycle gives the leucine aminonitrile. The network accesses 12 amino acids via Strecker intermediates. A later paper (Liu et al. 2023, *Nature Chemistry*, "Thiophosphate photochemistry enables prebiotic access to sugars and terpenoid precursors") showed thiophosphate (PSO3³⁻) photochemistry improves parts of this network and preserves access to valine and leucine.

**Yields.** Patel et al. explicitly decline to give discrete yields for individual products, arguing that in a multi-product network the yield of any one compound is less meaningful; no percentage is published for the valine aminonitrile from acetone. Benchmark homologation steps in related Sutherland-group work (e.g., glycolaldehyde ~42% after 4 h with CuCN/H2S under UV) indicate individual steps can be efficient, but the cumulative yield of the multistep valine sequence is unknown.

**Environment proposed.** Surface streams and pools on an early Earth with UV exposure, cyanide stored as ferrocyanide salts (released by impact/thermal processing), sulfide from volcanism or impacts, and "confluence" of streams delivering different intermediates.

**Critiques.** (i) Sulfide availability: Ranjan, Todd, Sutherland and Sasselov (2018, *Astrobiology* 18:1023) concluded that volcanic outgassing "could have supplied prebiotically relevant levels of SO2-derived anions, but not H2S-derived anions," and that "epochs of moderately high volcanism could have been especially conducive to cyanosulfidic prebiotic chemistry," so the chemistry needs specific local settings (e.g., impact-supplied sulfide or high-outgassing epochs). (ii) The use of 254-nm UV and Cu catalysts, and stepwise reagent addition by the experimenter, is criticized as idealized relative to a natural setting. (iii) Congruence with biochemistry: the route reaches valine via aminonitriles, not via α-KIV and transamination as in extant metabolism. (iv) Comment literature notes the chemistry requires H2S from the outset, possibly corresponding to a later geochemical stage.

### 4. Reductive amination and transamination of α-ketoisovalerate (metal/mineral catalysis)

**Strongest valine-specific demonstration.** Kaur, Rauscher, Werner, Song, Yi, Kazöne, Martin, Tüysüz and Moran (2024, *Chem* 10:1) showed that H2 drives nonenzymatic reductive amination of six biological α-ketoacids — pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, oxaloacetate, α-ketoisocaproate, **α-ketoisovalerate**, α-keto-β-methylvalerate — and glyoxylate to glycine, alanine, glutamate, aspartate, leucine, **valine** and isoleucine.\[5\] Conditions: 30 mM ketoacid, 5 bar H2, NH4HCO3 (5 equiv, 150 mM), pH 8 water, 22 °C, 72 h, with Ni/SiO2-Al2O3 (10 mol% Ni), Pd/C (1 mol%) or 3 mg ground Aletai iron meteorite (18 mol% Ni). With Ni and meteorite, combined amino + hydroxy acid yields were 6–84% at roughly 1:1 amino:hydroxy acid; with Pd, 52–88% with >5:1 selectivity for the amino acid in almost all cases. No reduction occurred without a metal catalyst. For pyruvate, ammonia as low as 6 mM gave alanine in up to 17% (Ni) or 44% (Pd) yield relative to ammonia — 62 to 250 times lower ammonia than earlier prebiotic reductive aminations (375 mM–1.5 M). The same conditions run H2-driven reductive aldol chemistry that builds α-ketoglutarate-family ketoacids, but not the branched α-KIV skeleton. The authors cite 1–15 mmol/kg dissolved H2 in hydrothermal fluids as a geological analog to their H2 pressures.

**Earlier iron-sulfide and iron-hydroxide work.** Hafenbradl, Keller, Wächtershäuser and Stetter (1995, *Tetrahedron Letters*) coupled reductive amination of α-oxo acids to oxidative pyrite formation (FeS/H2S).\[6\]\[7\] Huber and Wächtershäuser (2003, *Tetrahedron Letters* 44:1695) reported efficient reductive amination of α-keto acids with freshly precipitated FeS or Fe(OH)2 at pH near the amine pKa, using stoichiometric Fe(II) reductant and high ammonia.\[8\] Barge, Flores, Baum, VanderVelde and Russell (2019, *PNAS* 116:4828) showed mixed-valence iron oxyhydroxides ("green rust") convert pyruvate to alanine and lactate; alanine is favored at intermediate Fe redox state (~50% Fe(II)), at pH above the NH3 pKa (~9.25), and at 25–70 °C, with no alanine at pH 7.\[9\]\[10\]\[11\] Kitadai et al. (2019, *Science Advances*) electroreduced metal sulfides (e.g., FeS → Fe⁰) at potentials expected at alkaline vent–seawater interfaces and achieved >50% yields for reductive amination of four keto acids (1.25 mM each) in one pot. These studies did not focus on α-KIV; valine is expected by analogy but was not the reported target in the iron-mineral papers cited here.

**Reactivity data.** Mayer and Moran (2022, *Angewandte Chemie*) quantified nonenzymatic reduction and reductive amination of keto acids with BH3CN⁻ as a model hydride, showing α-ketoglutarate is the least reactive Krebs ketoacid toward amination\[12\] — relevant for predicting partitioning between α-KIV and other ketoacids in mixtures.

**Critiques.** (i) Substrate supply: every reductive-amination demonstration starts from purchased α-KIV; no abiotic α-KIV synthesis exists (see §5). (ii) Competing carbonyl reduction yields α-hydroxyisovalerate in roughly equal amounts with Ni/meteorite catalysts. (iii) Earlier Fe(II)-mediated work required 375 mM–1.5 M NH3, considered implausibly high; Kaur et al. reduce this but require concentrated metal catalysts (or Pd, which is rare). (iv) Metal-catalyzed hydrogenation is a textbook industrial reaction, and critics question whether native Ni or platinum-group metals were locally concentrated; proponents point to awaruite (Ni3Fe) in serpentinites, metallic Ni particles in H2-rich vents and ~220 tonnes/year of Fe-Ni cosmic dust.

### 5. Metabolism-first: the biosynthetic analogy (pyruvate → acetolactate → α-KIV → valine)

In biology, acetohydroxyacid synthase (ThDP-dependent) condenses two pyruvates to (S)-2-acetolactate + CO2; ketol-acid reductoisomerase (Mg²⁺/NADPH) performs an alkyl migration plus reduction to 2,3-dihydroxyisovalerate; dihydroxyacid dehydratase ([Fe–S] enzyme) gives α-KIV; and transamination yields valine.\[13\]\[14\]

**Evidence status.** A targeted literature search found **no peer-reviewed nonenzymatic demonstration** of any of the three skeleton-building steps. Thiazolium salts catalyze benzoin/acyloin condensations of aldehydes (Sheehan and Hara 1974, *Journal of Organic Chemistry*), but no prebiotic, metal- or mineral-catalyzed pyruvate → acetolactate conversion has been reported. Acetolactate is known to undergo nonenzymatic oxidative decarboxylation to diacetyl/acetoin, i.e., abiotic chemistry tends to degrade rather than build this intermediate. The reductoisomerization couples methyl migration and reduction in a single active site, with intermediates not released, which makes a nonenzymatic analog mechanistically demanding.

**Iron-promoted networks.** Muchowska, Varma and Moran (2019, *Nature* 569:104) showed Fe²⁺ in 70 °C water drives a pyruvate + glyoxylate network that reproduces 9 of 11 Krebs-cycle intermediates and all five universal metabolic precursors, and with hydroxylamine and metallic iron gives four amino acids (glycine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate); valine is not among them. Stubbs et al. (2020, *Nature Chemistry*) and Krishnamurthy-group cyanide-reductant chemistry (2022, *Nature Chemistry*) build α-ketoacid networks from glyoxylate and pyruvate, also without the branched-chain pathway.

**Alternative evolutionary hypothesis.** Keefe, Lazcano and Miller (1995, *Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere*) argued the acetolactate pathway cannot be the primordial route because it has unstable intermediates, and proposed that branched-chain amino acids were first made by reductive carboxylation of short branched-chain fatty acids (isobutyric acid → α-KIV) followed by transamination. This is a proposal, not an experimental demonstration.

**Thermodynamics.** Amend and Shock (1998, *Science* 281:1659) computed the net synthesis 5CO2(aq) + NH4⁺ + 12H2(aq) → valine(aq) + H⁺ + 8H2O. They report that "ΔGr is negative for 11 of the 20 net amino acid synthesis reactions in a 100°C mixed hydrothermal solution," whereas "all 20 amino acids are strongly endergonic in cooler (18°C), oxidized, surface seawater"; the paper's valine table row (entries of −70.12 and 178.00) was not verified here as to which quantity each represents, so per-valine kJ/mol values should be taken from the primary table. This establishes thermodynamic drive in H2-rich settings but says nothing about kinetic pathways; Aubrey, Cleaves and Bada (2009, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*) cautioned that such equilibrium models neglect kinetics and degradation, observing A ⇌ B ⇌ C (precursor ⇌ amino acid ⇌ degradation product) behavior at 125–175 °C.

**Critiques.** The framework predicts congruence with extant biochemistry (α-KIV → valine by amination), which the Kaur et al. data support for the final step, but the absence of a nonenzymatic α-KIV synthesis is the single largest gap in the metabolism-first account of valine. High temperatures also accelerate amino acid decomposition.

### 6. Bucherer–Bergs chemistry on α-ketoacids

Pulletikurti, Yadav, Springsteen and Krishnamurthy (2022, *Nature Chemistry* 14:1142) showed α-ketoacids react with cyanide and ammonia in bicarbonate buffer at room temperature via the Bucherer–Bergs pathway to hydantoins, which hydrolyze (80 °C) to α-amino acids; e.g., α-ketoglutarate gave the hydantoin in 96% yield, with slower hydrolysis to glutamate (19% with 72% hydantoin remaining). The route bridges cyanide chemistry and ketoacid metabolism. For valine it would require α-KIV and so inherits the same skeleton problem. Kaur et al. (2024) criticize cyanide-based routes as incongruent with metabolism, since cyanide is never directly used in biochemistry and is toxic to metalloenzymes.

### 7. Fischer–Tropsch-type (FTT) and CO/H2 surface chemistry

Yoshino, Hayatsu and Anders (1971, *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* 35:927) reported several α-amino acids and β-alanine from CO + H2 + NH3 over Fe–Ni catalysts at 200–500 °C, proposed as a solar-nebula source of meteoritic organics. A NASA follow-up (NASA TM X-62,411, 1975) heating CO/H2/NH3 with iron meteorite found only small quantities of a few amino acids, "at odds with the existing literature." Glavin et al. (2010, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*) and Elsila et al. (2021, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*) note that FTT synthesis needs re-examination with modern analytics. Valine specifically has not been robustly shown by FTT; the route is contested.

### 8. HCN polymer hydrolysis, formamide and radiolysis

Ferris, Wos, Nooner and Oró (1974, *Journal of Molecular Evolution* 3:225) identified glycine, aspartic acid and diaminosuccinic acid as major and alanine, isoleucine and α-aminoisobutyric acid as minor amino acids from hydrolyzed HCN oligomers — valine is not among them. Ferris et al. (1978, *Journal of Molecular Evolution* 11:293) showed 0.1 M HCN oligomerizes at pH 9.2. Sweeney, Toste and Ponnamperuma (1976, *Origins of Life* 7:187) exposed aqueous HCN solutions (0.004–0.1 M) to cobalt-60 gamma rays and identified glycine, alanine, valine, serine, threonine, aspartic acid and glutamic acid among the hydrolysis products; Ruiz-Bermejo et al. (2013, *Life*) list 12 of 20 protein amino acids across HCN-polymer studies. Valine is therefore a minor, condition-dependent product of HCN polymer chemistry rather than a characteristic one. Formamide-based chemistry has been developed mainly for nucleobases; this review found no valine-specific formamide synthesis.

### 9. Interstellar ice irradiation

Muñoz Caro et al. (2002, *Nature* 416:403) UV-irradiated H2O:CH3OH:NH3:CO:CO2 (2:1:1:1:1) ice at 12 K and identified 16 amino acids in the hydrolyzed room-temperature residue, valine among them. Bernstein et al. (2002, *Nature* 416:401) independently found glycine, alanine and serine from H2O/CH3OH/NH3/HCN ices. Modica et al. (2014, *Astrophysical Journal*) measured small enantiomeric excesses induced by circularly polarized UV for five amino acids including valine and norvaline. Critique: products are detected only after acid hydrolysis, so precursors rather than free valine form in the ice; yields are tiny; relevance depends on delivery and survival.

### 10. Impact/shock synthesis

Martins, Price, Goldman, Sephton and Burchell (2013, *Nature Geoscience* 6:1045) fired projectiles at ~7 km/s into comet-ice analogues and obtained racemic alanine, α-aminoisobutyric acid and isovaline (plus precursors) after hydrolysis. Valine was not reported. Impact routes are therefore unproven for valine.

### 11. Meteoritic occurrence and extraterrestrial delivery

**Abundance.** Valine is a minor meteoritic amino acid. Glavin et al. (2021, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*) reported amino acids in Aguas Zarcas and Murchison at ~0.1 to 158 nmol/g overall; valine was at the low end, too low for compound-specific δ¹³C measurement. Bennu samples (Glavin et al. 2025, *Nature Astronomy* 9:199) contain 33 amino acids including 14 of the 20 protein amino acids, valine among them, with chiral non-protein amino acids racemic or nearly so; the same paper measured an L-valine excess of ~34% in an acid-hydrolyzed hot-water extract but also observed elevated L-valine in the procedural blank, so laboratory contamination is a possible explanation.

**Enantiomeric data — contested.** Murchison's D/L valine ratio of 0.67 ± 0.03 (≈20% L-excess) in Glavin et al. (2021) differs from Aguas Zarcas and Murchison soil and may be extraterrestrial, but could not be confirmed isotopically. Pizzarello and Cronin (2000, *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta*) found α-H amino acids including valine racemic or attributed excesses to contamination, with the documented meteoritic excesses confined to α-methyl amino acids such as isovaline (up to 18.5% L in Murchison; Glavin and Dworkin 2009, *PNAS*). Dworkin et al. (2026, arXiv:2609.12249), an OSIRIS-REx contamination-knowledge paper, report that "the hummocky sample shows an excess of ~20% L-valine (Kim et al. 2026)," conclude that this excess "is not attributable to spacecraft contamination," and cite only a modest 2.2% L-valine excess as the sole earlier chondrite report (Pizzarello and Cronin 2000). These literatures conflict; any valine L-excess in meteorites should be regarded as unconfirmed. In the primitive CM Asuka 12236, valine D/L ratios of ~0.82–0.89 indicate near-racemic composition (Glavin et al. 2020, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*).

**Isomer context.** Meteorites contain valine together with norvaline, isovaline and other C5 amino acids, consistent with Strecker/cyanohydrin chemistry on parent bodies acting on mixtures of C4 aldehydes and ketones (isobutyraldehyde → valine; butanal → norvaline; 2-butanone → isovaline). The dominance of α-methyl amino acids such as isovaline in some chondrites contrasts with biology's selection of valine.

**Critiques.** Delivery fluxes and survival through atmospheric entry/impact limit concentrations; nmol/g valine in meteorites cannot by itself sustain concentration-dependent chemistry without strong local accumulation.

## Environmental Context

- **Reducing atmospheres/volcanic plumes with lightning**: supported for valine by spark experiments, contingent on local reducing gas compositions (Johnson et al. 2008).
- **UV-lit surface pools/streams with HCN, sulfide, Cu and phosphate (cyanosulfidic)**: valine aminonitrile demonstrated; sulfide and UV requirements are the main plausibility constraint, with Ranjan et al. (2018, *Astrobiology*) finding outgassing could supply SO2-derived but not H2S-derived anions at relevant levels.
- **H2-rich hydrothermal/serpentinizing systems with Ni/Fe-Ni alloys or iron oxyhydroxides**: best supported for the final α-KIV → valine step (Kaur et al. 2024; Barge et al. 2019 for analogous pyruvate chemistry) and thermodynamically favorable (Amend and Shock 1998), but with no demonstrated α-KIV source.
- **Meteorite parent bodies and interstellar ices**: proven valine sources at trace levels.

On the evidence, no single environment is demonstrated to make valine from simple feedstocks selectively. The most experimentally complete feedstock-to-valine chain is the cyanosulfidic acetone route (multistep, staged), while the most mild, catalytic and biochemically congruent final step is metal-catalyzed reductive amination.

## Open Questions and Challenges

1. **The branched-skeleton problem.** No abiotic synthesis of α-KIV is published; isobutyraldehyde arises only from non-selective discharge/FTT chemistry or the staged cyanosulfidic homologation of acetone. This is the central bottleneck for valine.
2. **Nonenzymatic acetolactate pathway.** Pyruvate → acetolactate, reductoisomerization and dehydration all lack nonenzymatic demonstrations; finding thiazolium-free, metal-promoted pyruvate homocoupling would be a high-value experiment.
3. **Selectivity versus isomers.** Valine co-forms with norvaline and isovaline in all non-enzymatic chemistries; mechanisms that favor the branched α-H isomer are unknown.
4. **Concentration and compatibility.** HCN dilution (~4 × 10⁻⁶ M at pH 8 and 0 °C in Stribling and Miller's 1987 primitive-ocean estimate), the ammonia requirement for amination, sulfide levels for photoredox chemistry, and isobutyraldehyde diversion to pantoate/pantetheine are all unresolved compatibility issues.
5. **Congruence with biochemistry.** Strecker and cyanosulfidic routes do not pass through α-KIV, while metabolism-first routes match biology's final step but not its skeleton-building steps.
6. **Chirality.** Abiotic valine is racemic; claimed meteoritic and Bennu L-excesses are contradictory and unconfirmed.

## Recommendations for Synthesis-Network Design

- Model two independent entry points into valine: (a) isobutyraldehyde + HCN + NH3 (catalyst-free Strecker), and (b) α-KIV + NH3 + H2 over Ni/Fe-Ni (or formate over Pd). Treat both as demonstrated end-games.
- Treat the upstream skeleton step as the explicit unknown: include the cyanosulfidic acetone → cyanohydrin → reductive homologation branch (with no published yield) and flag the biosynthetic acetolactate branch as hypothetical.
- Include the formaldehyde + isobutyraldehyde → pantolactone side reaction and α-hydroxyisovalerate formation as competing sinks.
- Track norvaline and isovaline as co-products and use chromatographic separation of C5 isomers in any experimental validation.
- Prioritize experiments on nonenzymatic pyruvate homocoupling or reductive carboxylation of isobutyrate (Keefe et al. 1995 hypothesis) over further optimization of the final amination step.

## Caveats

- Several valine-specific numbers (e.g., per-compound yields in Kaur et al. 2024 and absolute valine yields in spark experiments) are reported here only as ranges or qualitative detections because individual values were not extracted from primary tables.
- The Amend and Shock per-valine free energies were not verified against the primary table and should be checked before quantitative use; the ⁶⁰Co-HCN valine detection is from Sweeney, Toste and Ponnamperuma (1976, *Origins of Life*).
- Absence of a nonenzymatic acetolactate-pathway demonstration is based on targeted searches; an obscure report could exist.
