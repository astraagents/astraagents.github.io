# Prebiotic Synthesis of Serine: Routes, Evidence, Catalysts and Open Problems

The best-demonstrated prebiotic route to serine is the Strecker reaction of glycolaldehyde (glycolaldehyde + HCN + NH3 → serine nitrile → serine). In the Sutherland group's cyanosulfidic network, the glycolaldehyde can come from UV/Cu–H2S-driven reductive homologation of HCN.\[1\] A second route has been shown experimentally but needs a catalyst: hydroxymethylation (aldol addition) of glycine with formaldehyde, promoted by Cu²⁺ or pyridoxal plus metal ions.\[2\]\[3\] Every other route is either unselective, giving serine as a minor product (spark discharge, interstellar-ice photolysis, hydrothermal and parent-body chemistry), or not yet shown for serine at all. The biomimetic hydroxypyruvate → serine reductive amination or transamination has not been demonstrated in the modern metal-catalysed "protometabolism" literature I could find. Serine's instability is the main bottleneck shared by all routes: it dehydrates to pyruvate and alanine and undergoes retro-aldol cleavage back to glycine and formaldehyde.

## TL;DR
- **Most supported route:** glycolaldehyde Strecker chemistry. Glycolaldehyde forms in 42% yield from glycolonitrile under 254 nm UV with H2S and CuCN (Ritson & Sutherland 2013), and the Sutherland group reports that the Strecker reaction then gives serine nitrile in "excellent yield" (Foden et al. 2020).\[4\] The route is contested on geochemical grounds: it needs sulfide, copper, UV and HCN concentrations, and the reduction step can overshoot to acetaldehyde.
- **Strongest alternative:** a direct C1 + C2 aldol, glycine + formaldehyde → serine. It is well established synthetically with catalytic Cu²⁺ (Akabori et al. 1959) and with pyridoxal plus Al³⁺/Cu²⁺/Fe³⁺, and is reversible. It suffers from over-hydroxymethylation to α-(hydroxymethyl)serine and from limits on how much formaldehyde could accumulate. The biochemically congruent α-keto-acid route (hydroxypyruvate) has not been demonstrated non-enzymatically: recent Ni/H2, Fe and FeS reductive-amination networks make glycine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate and branched-chain amino acids, but not serine.
- **Environment verdict:** no single environment is convincingly established. Mild surface or near-surface aqueous settings (≤~100 °C) are favoured over high-temperature submarine vents, where serine peaks within hours and then decomposes (Aubrey, Cleaves & Bada 2009). Exogenous delivery is a weak serine source: meteoritic serine is a minor component, often at sub-nmol/g levels, and has long been suspected as contamination.

## Details

### 1. Known prebiotic synthesis routes

**Route A — Cyanosulfidic glycolaldehyde Strecker chemistry (Sutherland group).** Ritson & Sutherland (2012, *Nature Chemistry* 4:895–899) showed that UV irradiation of HCN with cyanocuprates drives a Kiliani–Fischer-type reductive homologation to glycolaldehyde and glyceraldehyde.\[5\]\[6\] In that first version the sugars were trapped as cyanate (oxazolidinone) adducts.\[1\] Ritson & Sutherland (2013, *Angewandte Chemie Int. Ed.* 52:5845–5847) then made H2S the stoichiometric reductant.\[1\]\[7\] Starting conditions were 10 mM glycolonitrile, 30 mM H2S, 33 mM sodium phosphate and 10 mol% solid CuCN, at pH 7 under 254 nm light.\[8\] After 2 h of irradiation, glycolaldehyde hydrate was present "in 42 % yield in solution".\[7\] Adding HCN and excess ammonia after reduction converted formaldehyde, acetaldehyde and glycolaldehyde into the aminonitriles of glycine, alanine and serine "over a period of days to weeks".\[1\]\[4\] The paper gives no quantitative serine-nitrile yield.

Patel et al. (2015, *Nature Chemistry* 7:301–307) integrated this chemistry into a single network, the "cyanosulfidic protometabolism". It yields precursors of glycine, alanine, serine and threonine together with ribonucleotide and lipid precursors.\[5\]\[9\] Foden et al. (2020, *Science*) state that glycolaldehyde "undergoes the Strecker reaction in excellent yield to afford a stable aminonitrile product, Ser-CN".\[10\] They then convert Ser-CN biomimetically to cysteine nitrile via dehydroalanine, which positions serine as a hub for cysteine. Xu et al. (2018, *Chemical Communications*) published a cyanosulfitic variant using sulfite plus ferrocyanide under UV. It gave 68% total reduced products after 1 h (glyceronitrile 40%, glycolaldehyde–sulfite adduct 28%), against 20% in the control without ferrocyanide.\[7\]

**Route B — Formose-derived glycolaldehyde plus classical Strecker chemistry.** This is the older version of the same disconnection: base-catalysed (formose) condensation of formaldehyde supplies glycolaldehyde, which then undergoes a Strecker reaction. A related test is Weber (1998, *Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere* 28:259–270), who reacted formaldehyde and glycolaldehyde with ammonia and a thiol catalyst.\[11\] Conditions were pH 5.2, 20 mM each aldehyde, 20 mM NH4Cl and 23 mM 3-mercaptopropionic acid, at 40 °C for 35 days. The products were 1.8% alanine and 0.08% homoserine, and serine was not reported.\[12\] Without cyanide, therefore, formose substrates plus ammonia are not selective for serine. That is a useful negative constraint for network design.

**Route C — Aldol hydroxymethylation of glycine by formaldehyde.** Akabori et al. (1959, *Archives of Biochemistry and Biophysics* 83:1–9) described "a single-step synthesis of DL-serine from the interaction of formaldehyde and glycine in an alkaline aqueous solution containing catalytic amounts of copper sulfate".\[2\]\[13\]\[14\] The coproduct is α-(hydroxymethyl)serine.\[2\] The analogous acetaldehyde reaction on copper glycinate (Sato et al., *Bulletin of the Chemical Society of Japan*, 1957–1959) gives threonine and allo-threonine, and became an industrial threonine process.\[13\]\[15\]

A non-copper variant uses pyridoxal plus Al³⁺, Cu²⁺ or Fe³⁺. Metzler, Longenecker & Snell (1954, *Journal of the American Chemical Society*) showed that this system cleaves serine reversibly and synthesises serine from glycine and formaldehyde.\[3\]\[15\] Metzler & Snell (1952, *Journal of Biological Chemistry*) reported the related non-enzymatic deamination and transamination of serine to hydroxypyruvate. I have this only via secondary sources and have not verified the original yields. Chadha & Choughuley (Bhabha Atomic Research Centre; 1984, *Origins of Life* 14:469–476) reported that "the reaction of formaldehyde with glycine in dilute and mildly alkaline conditions gave serine, hydroxymethylserine, sarcosine, iminodiacetic acid and alanine" (10⁻² M glycine, glycine:aldehyde = 1:2), and that "clays do not seem to influence most of these reactions".

**Route C′ — Akabori's polyglycine hypothesis.** Akabori, Okawa & Sato (1956, *Bulletin of the Chemical Society of Japan* 29:608–611) adsorbed polyglycine on kaolinite and converted glycyl residues to seryl and threonyl residues with formaldehyde and acetaldehyde.\[16\] Kuroda et al. (2024, *Molecules* 29(18):4403, "Alkylation of Complex Glycine Precursor (CGP) as a Prebiotic Route to 20 Proteinogenic Amino Acids Synthesis") revived the idea as alkylation of a "complex glycine precursor", stating that "the side-chain precursors of Ser and Thr are formaldehyde and acetaldehyde, respectively". The authors concede that most side-chain reactions "may be too slow to proceed under typical laboratory reaction conditions".

**Route D — Biomimetic α-keto-acid route (hydroxypyruvate → serine).** In biology, serine comes from 3-phosphoglycerate → 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate → phosphoserine (transamination), or from glycine + 5,10-methylene-THF via serine hydroxymethyltransferase.\[17\] The second pathway is the enzymatic counterpart of Route C. Metabolism-first frameworks would therefore predict non-enzymatic reductive amination or transamination of hydroxypyruvate. Goldford et al. (2019, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*) list hydroxypyruvate (serine) among the keto acids in their network-expansion analysis.\[18\]

The experimental record does not yet include serine. Muchowska, Varma & Moran (2019, *Nature* 569:104–107) obtained glycine, alanine, aspartate and glutamate with Fe⁰/NH2OH.\[19\]\[20\] Kaur et al. (2024, *Chem* 10:1528–1540) used Ni/SiO2–Al2O3, Pd or the Aletai meteorite with H2/NH4HCO3 (5 bar / 5 equiv) at pH 8 and 22 °C for 72 h. They converted glyoxylate plus six α-keto acids (pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, oxaloacetate, α-ketoisocaproate, α-ketoisovalerate, α-keto-β-methylvalerate) into seven amino acids, and serine was not among them.\[21\]\[22\]\[23\]\[24\] Kitadai et al. (2019, *Science Advances* 5:eaav7848) reported >50% yields for the reductive amination of four keto acids over electrochemically generated metals at pH 9.6.\[25\] Mayer & Moran (2022, *Angewandte Chemie*) quantified non-enzymatic reductive amination reactivity as α-ketoglutarate < oxaloacetate ≈ pyruvate ≪ glyoxylate.\[26\] In short, hydroxypyruvate is the missing substrate, and both its own prebiotic supply and its amination remain to be demonstrated.

Pulletikurti et al. (2022, *Nature Chemistry* 14:1142–1150) gave a cyanide-based alternative: Bucherer–Bergs conversion of α-keto acids to α-amino acids.\[27\] Whether it works for hydroxypyruvate is not established in the sources reviewed.

**Route E — Spark discharge.** Johnson et al. (2008, *Science* 322:404) reanalysed Miller's 1950s "volcanic" apparatus, which injected steam into the spark. They found 22 amino acids and five amines, including serine, and reported that "hydroxylated compounds were preferentially synthesized", which they attributed to OH radicals. Miller's glycine yields, based on carbon added as methane, were 2.1%, 1.8% and 0.4% for the classic, volcanic and silent-discharge apparatus.\[28\] Serine is reported only relative to glycine, and that ratio could not be extracted here. Parker et al. (2011, *PNAS* 108:5526–5531) analysed Miller's 1958 H2S-rich experiment (H2S, CH4, NH3, CO2) and found 23 amino acids and 4 amines.\[29\]\[30\]

**Route F — Hydrothermal synthesis.** Hennet, Holm & Engel (1992, *Naturwissenschaften* 79:361–365) heated HCN, CH2O and NH4Cl at 150 °C and 10 bar with a pyrite–pyrrhotite–magnetite redox buffer and obtained several amino acids.\[31\] Aubrey, Cleaves & Bada (2009, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres* 39:91–108) heated 30 mM each of Na2S, HCHO and NH3 at pH 8 in 0.5 M NaCl. Serine reached its maximum within the first ~2.5 h at 125 °C and then decayed exponentially; at 175 °C it showed "early maxima" within 5–10 h.\[32\] Serine "appears to be the precursor to alanine".\[32\]

**Route G — Extraterrestrial and parent-body chemistry.** Bernstein et al. (2002, *Nature* 416:401–403) showed "that glycine, alanine and serine naturally form from ultraviolet photolysis of the analogues of icy interstellar grains" (H2O, CH3OH, NH3, HCN).\[33\] Muñoz Caro et al. (2002, *Nature* 416:403–406) identified 16 amino acids, 6 of them proteinogenic, after UV irradiation at 12 K and acid hydrolysis.\[34\]\[35\] Kebukawa et al. (2017, *Science Advances* 3:e1602093) heated formaldehyde, glycolaldehyde, ammonia and water at 90–250 °C for 72 hours (C/N/H2O = 7.2:0.72:100) and made amino acid precursors alongside insoluble organic matter.\[36\] In a separate study from another group, Koga & Naraoka (Kyushu University; 2022, *ACS Earth and Space Chemistry* 6(5):1311–1320) reacted aldehydes with ammonia and reported "glycine, alanine, β-alanine, and C3 and C4 hydroxy amino acids (HAAs) … with α-, β-, and γ-structural isomers". Kebukawa and co-workers (2022, *ACS Central Science*) showed that γ-irradiation of aqueous formaldehyde and ammonia gives amino acid yields that scale linearly with dose.\[37\]

### 2. Key intermediates and precursors

- **C1:** HCN, H2CO, glycolonitrile (HOCH2CN, the HCN–H2CO cyanohydrin), CO/CO2 (indirect only, via formate/formaldehyde in reductive frameworks).
- **C2:** glycolaldehyde (the central node for Routes A, B and G); glycine or aminoacetonitrile (Route C); glyoxylate/glycolate (Route D precursors).
- **C3:** serine aminonitrile, 2-amino-3-hydroxypropanenitrile (Ser-CN); hydroxypyruvate (Route D); metal-glycinate or pyridoxal–glycine Schiff bases (Route C).
- **N sources:** NH3/NH4⁺ (Strecker, reductive amination); hydroxylamine (Muchowska et al. 2019); amino-acid donors (transamination).
- **Downstream and side products to model:** α-(hydroxymethyl)serine (over-aldol); dehydroalanine / N-formyldehydroalanine nitrile from Ser-CN under acylating or formylating conditions (Green et al. 2023, *JACS*, Sutherland group); pyruvate and alanine (β-elimination); glycine + formaldehyde (retro-aldol).

### 3. Conditions and environments by route

- **Route A:** 254 nm UV, pH ~7, mM-level HCN/H2S, phosphate buffer, Cu(I) (rate-accelerating), ambient temperature. The proposed setting is shallow surface pools or streams fed by ferrocyanide salts, with impact-derived or volcanic sulfide (Patel et al. 2015). Ranjan et al. (2018, *Astrobiology*; arXiv 1801.07725) modelled sulfidic-anion concentrations from atmospheric H2S/SO2 and concluded that the requirement imposes "an additional, local requirement for this chemistry to function".\[38\] Ranjan & Sasselov (2016, *Astrobiology*) examined whether the Hadean surface UV spectrum is compatible with the cyanocuprate photochemistry.
- **Route C:** mildly to strongly alkaline aqueous solution; Cu²⁺ or pyridoxal plus metal; ambient to reflux temperatures in preparative work. Its prebiotic setting is undefined. Any formaldehyde- and glycine-bearing alkaline water body with dissolved Cu²⁺/Fe³⁺ would qualify.
- **Route D:** Ni⁰/H2 at 22 °C and pH 8 (Kaur et al. 2024).\[21\]\[23\] Proponents link this to serpentinizing alkaline vents, where native Ni/Fe and H2 occur.\[24\]\[39\] Kitadai et al. (2019) used FeS/metal precipitates under vent-like pH gradients.\[25\]
- **Route E:** reducing (CH4/NH3/H2) or volcanic-plume atmospheres with lightning.
- **Route F:** 125–175 °C (Aubrey et al. 2009), 150 °C and 10 bar (Hennet et al. 1992); submarine hydrothermal systems.
- **Route G:** 10–12 K vacuum-UV ices; 80–250 °C aqueous alteration in planetesimals.

### 4. Catalysts and surfaces

- **No catalyst required:** the Strecker step (glycolaldehyde + HCN + NH3 → Ser-CN) and aminonitrile hydrolysis. Ritson & Sutherland (2013) also show that HCN reduction proceeds without copper, more slowly. Ice photolysis and spark discharge are energy-driven rather than catalysed.
- **Experimentally shown to facilitate serine-specific steps:** Cu²⁺ for glycine + HCHO → serine (Akabori et al. 1959); pyridoxal + Al³⁺/Cu²⁺/Fe³⁺ for reversible glycine + HCHO ⇌ serine (Metzler, Longenecker & Snell 1954); kaolinite for polyglycine hydroxymethylation (Akabori, Okawa & Sato 1956); Cu(I) cyanide as a photoredox accelerant upstream of glycolaldehyde (Ritson & Sutherland 2012, 2013); ferrocyanide in the sulfite system (Xu et al. 2018).
- **Shown for analogous steps but not for serine:** Ni⁰, Pd, meteorite powder (Kaur et al. 2024); Fe⁰/Fe²⁺ (Muchowska et al. 2019); FeS and other metal sulfides reduced to metals (Kitadai et al. 2019); mixed-valence iron oxyhydroxides (Barge et al. 2019, *PNAS* 116:4828);\[40\] pyrite–pyrrhotite–magnetite buffer (Hennet et al. 1992).\[19\]\[22\]\[25\]\[40\] Aluminium-smectites stimulate and iron-smectites hinder amino acid formation from hexamethylenetetramine (Vinogradoff et al. 2020).\[41\]

### 5. Experimental evidence, summarised quantitatively

- 42% glycolaldehyde from glycolonitrile in 2 h (UV, H2S, CuCN; Ritson & Sutherland 2013). With prolonged irradiation the aldehyde distribution shifts from 14:41:8:37 at 2 h to 0:0:12:88 at 6 h as glycolaldehyde is over-reduced toward acetaldehyde.\[4\]
- 68% total reduced products in 1 h with sulfite + ferrocyanide, against 20% without (Xu et al. 2018).\[7\]
- Ser-CN forms "in excellent yield" from glycolaldehyde (Foden et al. 2020); the number sits in supporting information I could not access.
- DL-serine was obtained preparatively from glycine + HCHO + catalytic CuSO4 (Akabori et al. 1959); the yield was not retrieved.
- Hydrothermal: in hydrothermal simulations, amino acid yields "generally no greater than a few percent"; a "10-fold drop in concentration results in orders of magnitude lower yields"; serine is transient (Aubrey et al. 2009).\[32\]
- Thiol/formose substrates: 1.8% alanine and 0.08% homoserine, no serine (Weber 1998).\[12\]
- Ice photolysis: serine detected, but only after acid hydrolysis of residues (Bernstein et al. 2002; Muñoz Caro et al. 2002).\[33\]\[35\]
- Theory: Higgs & Pudritz (2009, *Astrobiology*) place serine among the ~10 "early" amino acids formed in abiotic syntheses and meteorites, ranked by thermodynamic cost. The automated reaction-network study "Discovery of New Synthetic Routes of Amino Acids in Prebiotic Chemistry" (2024, *JACS Au* 4(12):4757) found that "the rate-determining step for serine synthesis is the coupling of glycine and formaldehyde". The HCOOH-catalysed version of that step has a barrier of Gₐ = 47.9 kcal/mol, and in another pathway glycine "can overcome a higher barrier of 55.5 kcal mol–1 to couple with formaldehyde". Taken literally, those barriers mean the aldol step needs metal or base catalysis.

### 6. Environmental context — which settings are best supported?

My assessment is that **low-temperature (≤~100 °C), mildly alkaline-to-neutral aqueous surface or near-surface environments** are best supported for serine. The evidence is as follows. (i) Every route with a high or clean serine-relevant yield (glycolaldehyde Strecker; Cu/pyridoxal aldol) operates at ambient to modest temperature. (ii) Serine decomposes faster than most simple amino acids under hydrothermal conditions. Sato et al. (2004, *Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research*) found degradation rates at 200–340 °C and 20 MPa in the order Asp > Ser > Phe > Leu > Ala, with glycine and alanine produced from serine.\[42\] Aubrey et al. (2009) concluded that "the equilibrium at high temperatures characteristic of SHSs favors net amino acid degradation rather than synthesis".\[32\] (iii) The UV-driven surface scenario has the most complete serine-specific demonstration, though it depends on local geochemistry (sulfide, Cu, ferrocyanide, UV). Alkaline-vent and serpentinizing settings remain viable for Route D in principle, but serine itself has not been made there. The exogenous contribution is likely minor. Engel & Nagy (1982, *Nature* 296:837) found only "minor traces" of serine in Murchison and noted that serine and threonine "are usually considered to be terrestrial contaminations".\[43\] Glavin et al. (2021, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*) measured serine at ~0.2–0.3 nmol/g in Aguas Zarcas with large L-excesses. They could not fully exclude contamination, although they argued that a contaminant set lacking L-alanine is improbable.\[44\]

### 7. Open questions, bottlenecks and published critiques

**Contested routes and why:**
- **Route A (cyanosulfidic).** (a) Geochemical: it needs concentrated HCN, sulfidic anions and copper together in UV-lit water. Ranjan et al. (2018) argue that sulfidic-anion supply is a local constraint. Commentators note that sulfide is poorly soluble except at alkaline pH and that copper has low crustal abundance, which motivated the cyanosulfitic variant.\[45\] (b) Compatibility: the same reduction that makes glycolaldehyde destroys it. Ritson & Sutherland (2013) note that full reduction would turn "all of the glycolaldehyde … needed to make ribonucleotides and serine" into acetaldehyde, so the network needs staged or "convergent" stream mixing (Patel et al. 2015).\[4\] (c) Downstream: Ser-CN readily eliminates to dehydroalanine derivatives under acylating or formylating conditions (Foden et al. 2020; Green et al. 2023). That is useful for cysteine but is a loss channel for serine. (d) Biochemical congruence: the route resembles no extant serine biosynthesis.
- **Route C (glycine + HCHO).** It is efficient only with Cu²⁺ or pyridoxal–metal catalysis; free Cu²⁺ is tied to an oxidised-surface assumption, and pyridoxal itself lacks a robust prebiotic synthesis. Over-reaction to α-(hydroxymethyl)serine limits selectivity. Formaldehyde steady-state concentrations were probably ≤~10⁻⁴ M (Holland 1984, as cited by Aubrey et al. 2009). Formaldehyde also feeds competing formose and HMT chemistry. The reaction's reversibility caps serine accumulation. In its favour, it is the closest chemical analogue of serine hydroxymethyltransferase.
- **Route D (hydroxypyruvate).** It is the most congruent with biology but has no demonstrated prebiotic source of hydroxypyruvate and no demonstrated amination to serine. It is a priority experiment, not an established route.
- **Routes E–G.** These are unselective. Yields are reported relative to glycine or after acid hydrolysis, which can convert bound precursors to free amino acids. They depend on contested reducing atmospheres (E) or on high starting concentrations that Aubrey et al. (2009) judged unrealistic:\[37\] their 30 mM HCHO is ~30× the 1 mM high-end ocean estimate, upper-limit oceanic HCN is ~2 × 10⁻⁶ M (Miyakawa et al. 2002) and NH3 ~2 × 10⁻⁷ M (Summers 1999).\[32\]

**Bottlenecks:**
1. **Stability:** serine is lost by β-elimination (to pyruvate/alanine), retro-aldol (to glycine + HCHO) and thermal decarboxylation or deamination.\[42\]\[46\] Ter-Ovanessian et al. (2025, *Angewandte Chemie*) even use serine's phosphorylation, dehydration and transamination with glycine as a source of pyruvate and glyoxylate.\[47\] That makes serine a plausible metabolic hub, but also a sink.
2. **Glycolaldehyde supply and selectivity:** formose is uncontrolled; photoreduction must be stopped at the right stage.
3. **Concentration:** HCN, NH3 and HCHO steady states in open water are likely far below laboratory values, so evaporative, eutectic or mineral-salt (ferrocyanide) concentration steps are needed.
4. **Chirality:** every route gives racemic serine. Meteoritic L-excesses remain debated as possible contamination.\[44\]\[48\]
5. **Analytical gaps:** quantitative serine yields are missing from several landmark studies, including the Johnson et al. 2008 serine/glycine ratio, the Akabori 1959 serine yield and the Patel 2015 Ser-CN yield. These should be pulled from the original supplementary information before modelling.

## Recommendations (for synthesis-network design)
- **Model Route A as the primary node, with explicit kinetic competition.** Use glycolonitrile → glycolaldehyde (≈42% at 2 h) → acetaldehyde over-reduction as competing steps. Treat the Strecker step as uncatalysed, and include Ser-CN → dehydroalanine-nitrile loss under acylating conditions.
- **Include Route C as a parallel, catalyst-gated edge** (glycine + HCHO ⇌ serine). Make it reversible, Cu²⁺/pyridoxal-dependent, and give it an α-(hydroxymethyl)serine side product. Without a catalyst, assign it a very slow rate, consistent with the ~48 kcal/mol computed barrier.
- **Treat Route D as a hypothesis to test experimentally.** Run hydroxypyruvate under Kaur et al. (2024) Ni/H2 conditions and under Fe/NH2OH, FeS and pyridoxal–metal conditions; this is the most informative missing experiment for metabolism-first frameworks.
- **Add serine degradation sinks at all temperatures**, especially above ~100 °C. Do not count high-temperature vent chemistry as a net serine source.
- **Weight exogenous delivery low** for serine specifically.

## Caveats
- Several numbers come from secondary descriptions or abstracts because primary full texts or supplementary information (Patel 2015; Akabori 1959; Johnson 2008 Table S1; Metzler & Snell) were inaccessible; the original Metzler/Snell yields are unverified.
- Absence of serine in the Ni/H2, Fe and FeS reductive-amination studies reflects substrates not tested, not a demonstrated failure.
- Many "serine detected" results (ice photolysis, spark discharge, parent-body simulations) rely on acid hydrolysis and are relative or qualitative. They show that serine can form, not that the yields are useful.
