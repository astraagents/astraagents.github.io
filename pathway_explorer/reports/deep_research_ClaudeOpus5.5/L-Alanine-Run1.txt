# Prebiotic Synthesis of L-Alanine: Routes, Evidence, Conditions and Contested Points

Racemic alanine is one of the easiest proteinogenic amino acids to make abiotically. It has been synthesized from several unrelated feedstocks: HCN with acetaldehyde and NH3 (Strecker), pyruvate with NH3 and a reductant (reductive amination), pyruvate with cyanide, NH3 and CO2 (Bucherer–Bergs), and aldehyde/sugar chemistry with NH3. It also arrives in meteorites. No experiment, however, makes the **L** enantiomer selectively. The real bottleneck for "L-alanine" is enantioenrichment, plus the problem of joining each route's feedstock synthesis to its amination step under one set of conditions. Which route is "best" depends on which framework you accept, and each is contested on yield, concentration, or geochemical plausibility.

## TL;DR
- **Two families of routes dominate, and both work in the lab.** (1) Aldehyde/cyanide chemistry: Strecker from acetaldehyde + HCN + NH3, starting with Miller's 1953 spark discharge, and today Sutherland's cyanosulfidic network. (2) Keto-acid chemistry: reductive amination of pyruvate on FeS, green rust, electro-reduced FeS, Fe0 or Ni0/H2. Pyruvate amination gives the highest single-step yields (~50–70%, "nearly complete" in some runs), but needs high NH3, alkaline pH and a pyruvate source that is itself low-yield (≤10 µM from CO2 + H2).
- **Every demonstrated route gives racemic alanine.** The only claimed natural L-excess, ~33% L-alanine in Murchison, was reported by Engel & Macko (1997, *Nature*) on the basis of "nearly equal, extraterrestrial δ15N values measured for each alanine enantiomer". It was disputed (Pizzarello & Cronin 1998). Later Murchison and Bennu analyses found alanine racemic. Asymmetric photolysis by circularly polarized light gives only percent-level excesses. Getting from racemic to L is unsolved for every framework.
- **Environment is unresolved.** Alkaline hydrothermal/serpentinizing settings best fit the keto-acid routes, but face the "hot-vent decomposition" critique (Miller & Bada 1988). Surface UV/sulfidic or atmospheric-discharge settings fit the HCN routes, but depend on reducing gases or locally concentrated HCN/sulfide. For network design, treat pyruvate⇄alanine as a robust, multi-catalyst node. Treat the upstream C3 source and the chirality step as the open problems.

## Details

### 1. Strecker chemistry (HCN + acetaldehyde + NH3)

**Chemistry.** CH3CHO + NH3 → imine. Adding HCN gives α-aminopropionitrile, and hydrolysis (via the amide) gives alanine. Strecker made alanine this way from acetaldehyde, ammonia and HCN in 1850, the first laboratory amino acid synthesis.\[1\] In Strecker chemistry the imine/aminonitrile and the cyanohydrin are in equilibrium until hydrolysis begins. Where NH3 is low or pH is low, aminonitriles partly convert to cyanohydrins, which hydrolyze to lactate rather than alanine (as noted in the 2021 *Science Advances* paper "A cyanosulfidic origin of the Krebs cycle").\[2\]

**Miller–Urey evidence.** Miller (1953, *Science*) sparked CH4/NH3/H2/H2O and obtained glycine, alanine, β-alanine and α-aminobutyric acid.\[3\] Miller attributed this to Strecker chemistry: HCN and aldehydes formed in the gas phase reacted in the water.\[3\] Bada's retrospective (Mètode, citing Miller & Urey 1959) gives these yields relative to glycine = 1: alanine ≈ 0.54 mol, β-alanine ≈ 0.24 mol. From 710 mg of methane carbon, 4.8 mg glycine formed.\[3\]\[4\] Johnson et al. (2008, *Science*) give glycine at 2.1% of methane carbon for the classic apparatus, versus 1.8% and 0.4% for the other two.\[5\] Miller estimated that 50–60% of the methane carbon became organic compounds, mostly polymeric material.\[3\]

**Variants.**
- **H2S-rich atmosphere.** Parker et al. (2011, *PNAS*) reanalyzed a 1958 Miller experiment run with CH4, NH3, H2S and CO2. It produced 23 amino acids and 4 amines, with high relative abundances of alkyl amino acids including alanine.\[6\]\[7\]
- **Neutral atmosphere.** Cleaves et al. (2008, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*) showed that CO2/N2 atmospheres give far more amino acids than previously thought, including alanine, once nitrite/nitrate (which destroy amino acids during workup) are controlled and the solution is buffered. The alanine was racemic within experimental error.\[8\]\[9\]

**Cyanosulfidic protometabolism (Sutherland school).** Patel et al. (2015, *Nature Chemistry*) showed that UV-driven (254 nm), HS⁻/Cu-mediated reductive homologation of HCN produces precursors of ribonucleotides, lipids and amino acids. In that network 12 amino acids are reached by Strecker synthesis.\[2\]\[10\]\[11\] Alanine is fairly photostable in these systems. At high pH it photolyzes slowly (~6% products after 2.5 h), and NaSH suppresses its photodegradation. Slow oxidation of alanine to pyruvate was also observed (2021 *Sci. Adv.*).\[2\] This links the cyanide and keto-acid worlds.

**Isotopic fingerprint.** Murchison alanine has a ¹³C-rich α-carbon, consistent with Strecker synthesis from acetaldehyde with a ¹³C-rich carbonyl carbon (Chimiak et al. 2020). Experiments then measured Strecker-specific isotope effects for alanine, e.g. a 55.1‰ nitrogen equilibrium isotope effect between NH3 and the aminonitrile (*Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta*, 2022).\[12\] This is the strongest natural-sample evidence that Strecker chemistry actually made alanine somewhere in the early Solar System.

**Critiques.**
- The early atmosphere was probably weakly reducing or neutral (CO2/N2), which sharply lowers HCN and aldehyde production.\[5\]
- The cyanosulfidic network needs locally high HCN and sulfide. Ranjan et al. (arXiv 1801.07725) analyzed sulfidic anion concentrations as an extra local constraint.\[11\]
- Strecker chemistry is aldehyde-based. Extant biochemistry makes alanine from pyruvate. This "disconnect" is the main congruence critique raised by Pulletikurti et al. (2022).\[13\]

### 2. Bucherer–Bergs chemistry from pyruvate (cyanide + NH3 + CO2)

Pulletikurti, Yadav, Springsteen & Krishnamurthy (2022, *Nature Chemistry* 14:1142–1150) showed that α-ketoacids react with cyanide and ammonia sources to give the corresponding α-amino acids by the Bucherer–Bergs pathway (hydantoin and N-carbamoyl intermediates).\[14\] The same chemistry gives dihydroorotate from oxaloacetate.\[15\] CO2 is mechanistically essential. Krishnamurthy: "If you mix only the keto acid, cyanide and ammonia, it just sits there. As soon as you add carbon dioxide, even trace amounts, the reaction picks up speed."\[16\] This route is a bridge: it uses a cyanide feedstock but a metabolic (keto-acid) carbon skeleton.\[13\] Its weakness is that it still needs both a pyruvate source and cyanide in the same place. A related paper (Yadav et al. 2022, *Nature Chemistry*) showed cyanide acting as a reductant on TCA intermediates, but pyruvate itself was not reduced.\[17\]\[18\]

### 3. Reductive amination of pyruvate (metabolism-first / hydrothermal frameworks)

**Chemistry.** Pyruvate + NH3 + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → alanine + H2O, via iminopyruvate.\[19\] This is the same transformation alanine dehydrogenase catalyzes with NADH.\[20\] The universal competing reaction is reduction of pyruvate to lactate. Product selectivity depends on pH, NH3 concentration and the reductant.

**Iron sulfide / ferrous hydroxide (Wächtershäuser school).**
- Huber & Wächtershäuser (2003, *Tetrahedron Letters* 44:1695–1697) reported that "amino acids are formed efficiently by reductive amination of α-keto acids under aqueous conditions with freshly precipitated FeS or Fe(OH)2 and with NH3… at pH values near their pKa" (pH ~9–10 for NH3).\[21\]
- A secondary summary (Milner-White & Russell 2008, *Biology Direct*) quotes alanine, glutamate, phenylalanine and tyrosine yields up to ~50% at 50 and 75 °C.\[22\] The primary table was not verified here.
- These experiments used large NH3 excesses. Kaur et al. (2024) characterize earlier Fe-based reductive aminations as using "375 mM to 1.5 M" ammonia.\[23\]
- FeS acts as a stoichiometric reductant (oxidized toward pyrite/Fe(III)), not as a true catalyst.\[19\]

**Mixed-valence iron hydroxides (green rust; Russell/Barge alkaline-vent school).** Barge et al. (2019, *PNAS* 116:4828–4833) reacted pyruvate + NH3 with Fe(II)/Fe(III) hydroxides:\[24\]
- Alanine formed only when the mineral was partially oxidized. The maximum was at 1:1 Fe(II):Fe(III).\[24\]
- Alkaline pH was required. At 75% Fe(II) and pH 7, "no alanine was produced", and lactate rose to 30–50% after 72 h. Lactate is favored at low pH.
- The highest yield was at pH 10, Fe(II):Fe(III) = 1:1 and 70 °C: "∼70%" alanine with "∼30% lactate", relative to all organics detected in the liquid phase. At 50 °C "the same reactions proceeded but more slowly."
- Neither end member worked: "Purely ferrous hydroxides did not drive reductive amination but instead promoted pyruvate reduction to lactate, and ferric hydroxides did not result in any reaction."
- The authors place this in iron-rich sediments near alkaline vents, where redox and pH gradients would set alanine/lactate selectivity. They state that "the precise mechanism of pyruvate reductive amination to alanine in these systems was not established."\[24\]\[25\]

**Electro-reduced FeS and native metals (Kitadai/Nakamura).** Kitadai et al. (2019, *Science Advances* 5:eaav7848):
- FeS was electrolyzed at −0.7 V (vs SHE) for 7 days, producing partial Fe0 ("FeS_PERM"). The authors argue such potentials arise naturally in H2-rich alkaline vents.\[26\]
- At pH 9.6 (0.5 M NH3), 5 mM pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate "reacted nearly completely" to alanine and glutamate within 48 h at 23–25 °C.\[26\]
- In a competitive one-pot run (four keto acids, 1.25 mM each), all amino acids formed in >50% yield, with alanine and glutamate favored.\[27\]
- Pure Fe0 was less selective: it also made pyruvate and alanine by oxaloacetate decarboxylation.\[27\]

**Ni0/H2 (Moran/Martin school).** Kaur et al. (2024, *Chem* 10:1528–1540):
- Screening conditions: 30 mM pyruvate, 5 bar H2, 22 °C, pH 7–8, 24 h. Ni/SiO2–Al2O3 or Pd/C gave 18–40% alanine with 5–15% lactate.\[23\]
- At only 6 mM NH3, alanine reached up to 17% with Ni and 44% with Pd (relative to ammonia). This substantially relaxes the ammonia-concentration critique of the Fe-based work.\[23\]
- The Aletai iron meteorite (18 mol% Ni) gave 24% alanine and 43% lactate.\[23\]
- The system also runs a reverse-Krebs-cycle analog, making keto acids and amino acids in one pot.\[28\]
- Follow-up work without H2 (Ni at 100 °C, pH 11, 18 h) aminated 6.4% of pyruvate to alanine.\[29\]

**Hydroxylamine + Fe0.** Muchowska, Varma & Moran (2019, *Nature* 569:104–107) showed an Fe²⁺-promoted network in which pyruvate and glyoxylate build 9 of 11 TCA-cycle intermediates. Adding hydroxylamine and Fe0 gave four biological amino acids, including alanine, by oxime reduction "in a manner that parallels biosynthesis".\[30\] The prebiotic source of NH2OH is the weak link.\[26\]

**Where does the pyruvate come from?** This is the upstream bottleneck for every keto-acid route.
- Cody et al. (2000, *Science*) made pyruvate from formic acid with nonyl thiol and FeS at 250 °C and 200 MPa. Wächtershäuser himself noted the paradox: pyruvate is thermally fragile (it decomposes near 165 °C).\[31\]
- Preiner et al. (2020, *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 4:534–542) showed greigite (Fe3S4), magnetite (Fe3O4) and awaruite (Ni3Fe) fixing CO2 with H2 at 100 °C under alkaline conditions. Their abstract reports "formate (up to 200 mM), acetate (up to 100 µM), pyruvate (up to 10 µM), methanol (up to 100 µM) and methane". Higher figures quoted in a 2023 *Frontiers in Microbiology* review (560 µM acetate, 0.3 M formate) do not match this primary abstract.
- Huber, Wächtershäuser and co-workers made pyruvate from acetylene + CO on NiS above 100 °C (2020, *Life*/PMC).\[32\]

A tens-of-percent amination step fed by micromolar pyruvate gives micromolar alanine at best. Concentrating mechanisms (pores, thermophoresis, mineral adsorption) are invoked but not demonstrated for this specific pair.

### 4. Transamination (pyridoxal and metal ions)

Pyridoxal (PL) transaminates amino acids without enzymes, and metal ions strongly modulate this.\[33\] Dherbassy, Mayer, Muchowska & Moran (2023, *JACS* 145:13357–13370) found cooperative metal–pyridoxal catalysis, strongest with Fe(III) and Al(III) (roughly 180-fold over Fe(III) alone and 90-fold over pyridoxal alone, as quoted in a PMC review). They also found that the mechanism converges on the biological one.\[34\] Amino-group transfer from aspartate to pyruvate (giving alanine) proceeds readily, but alanine→aspartate is poor (*Life* 2023, "Prebiotic Synthesis of Aspartate Using Life's Metabolism as a Guide").\[35\] In Ni/H2 systems (FEBS J), adding pyridoxal raised pyruvate→alanine conversion from 7.6% to 34% at pH 11 and 80 °C, and up to 48% at 80–100 °C. Pyridoxamine acts as the nitrogen shuttle and favors amination over lactate formation.\[36\] Counterpoint: Zabinski & Toney (2001, *JACS*) found that metal ions inhibit some PLP-catalyzed transaminations, so metal effects depend on the system.\[37\]

### 5. Sugar/aldehyde routes without cyanide (Weber's "triose model")

Weber (1985, *Journal of Molecular Evolution*) made alanine from glyceraldehyde and ammonium at ambient temperature. A thiol (3-mercaptopropionic acid) made the reaction about six times faster: 0.6% vs 0.1% after 60 days. The proposed path runs through dehydration of glyceraldehyde to a methylglyoxal-type species, a hemithioacetal and an imine, with lactate as a parallel product.\[38\] Weber (1998, *OLEB* 28:259–270) then showed that formaldehyde + glycolaldehyde (formose substrates) + NH3 + thiol at pH 5.2 and 40 °C for 35 days gave 1.8% alanine (on formaldehyde), and essentially none without thiol.\[39\] Attractions: no HCN, a link to formose sugars, and thioester (activated) intermediates. Critiques: low yield, month-long timescales, and an organic thiol catalyst whose own prebiotic source must be explained.

### 6. Parent-body and interstellar synthesis (delivery framework)

- **Parent-body aqueous chemistry.** Kebukawa et al. (2017, *Science Advances*) heated formaldehyde, glycolaldehyde and ammonia (90–250 °C) and made α-, β- and γ-amino acids together with insoluble organic matter.\[40\] Koga & Naraoka (2017, *Scientific Reports*) made glycine, alanine and hydroxy amino acids from formaldehyde/acetaldehyde/NH3 at 60 °C and pH 11.5.\[41\] In their Murchison sample, "the second most abundant amino acid was alanine (1.7 ppm) with a relative enrichment of L-alanine". That L-enrichment conflicts with the racemic results discussed below.
- **Interstellar ice photolysis.** UV-irradiated ice analogues give racemic amino acids, including alanine (Bernstein et al. 2002 and Muñoz Caro et al. 2002, *Nature* 416).
- **Returned samples.** Bennu samples (Glavin, Dworkin et al. 2025, *Nature Astronomy* 9:199–210) contain 33 amino acids, 14 of them proteinogenic. The total is ~70 nmol/g, ~3.5× less than Murchison, and alanine is racemic.\[42\]\[43\] The authors interpret the high glycine and racemic α-amino acids as consistent with HCN-polymerization/Strecker-type chemistry in an ammonia-rich parent body.\[42\]

Delivery supplies alanine without terrestrial geochemical constraints. It is racemic, though, and quantitative impact survival and delivery fluxes are uncertain.

### 7. Thermodynamics and stability (hydrothermal frameworks)

Amend & Shock (1998, *Science* 281:1659–1662) calculated that net alanine synthesis from CO2, NH4⁺ and H2 in a 100 °C mixed hydrothermal solution releases 12.12 kJ/mol. Synthesis is exergonic there but endergonic in cold oxidized surface seawater.\[44\] This supports the idea that H2-rich hydrothermal mixing zones thermodynamically favor alanine. The kinetic counterpoint: Aubrey, Cleaves & Bada (2009, *OLEB*) found that at 175 °C glycine and alanine peak after ~75 h and then decompose by deamination/decarboxylation.\[45\] Miller & Bada (1988, *Nature* 334:609–611) argued that vent temperatures "would not allow synthesis of organic compounds, but would decompose them, unless the exposure time at vent temperatures was short."\[46\] Note that the most productive keto-acid experiments (Barge, Kitadai, Kaur) run at 22–70 °C, well below black-smoker temperatures. That partly answers the critique for off-axis alkaline systems, but not for high-temperature (>150 °C) pyruvate formation steps like Cody et al.

### 8. The "L" problem

All routes above give racemic alanine. The evidence on natural enantioenrichment:
- **Murchison.** Engel & Macko (1997, *Nature* 389:265–268) reported an L-alanine excess ([L − D]/[L + D]) of ~33%. They argued it was extraterrestrial on the basis of "nearly equal, extraterrestrial δ15N values measured for each alanine enantiomer". Pizzarello & Cronin (1998, *Nature* 394:236) found interior Murchison alanine racemic. They attributed the excess to exterior contamination or incomplete chromatographic resolution.\[47\] Glavin et al. (2021, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*) found Murchison alanine "both racemic (D ≈ L) and heavily enriched in 13C, indicating no measurable terrestrial alanine contamination" (16.5 nmol of racemic alanine in a 1 g extract). In the same samples glutamic acid showed ~15–39% L-excess.
- **Bennu.** Alanine is racemic (2025).\[48\]\[49\]
- **Circularly polarized light.** Asymmetric photolysis of racemic alanine films (Meinert et al. 2014, *Angewandte Chemie*) and CPL irradiation of ice analogues (Modica et al. 2014, *ApJ* 788:79; up to |1.34|% ee) induce only small, helicity- and wavelength-dependent excesses.\[50\]\[51\]\[52\]\[53\]

So no published route produces L-alanine selectively. An amplification mechanism (crystallization, autocatalysis, chiral templating, or later selection by chiral catalysts) has to be added to any route.\[54\] This is common to all frameworks and is not specific to alanine.

## Catalysts and surfaces: what is actually shown

- **No catalyst needed:** the Strecker steps (imine formation, cyanide addition, nitrile hydrolysis under acid or base) and Bucherer–Bergs (needs CO2/bicarbonate as reagent, not a mineral catalyst). Spark discharge supplies the energy for HCN and aldehyde formation.
- **Mineral or metal reductants/catalysts for pyruvate → alanine:**
  - freshly precipitated FeS and Fe(OH)2 (stoichiometric)
  - mixed-valence green rust (Fe(II):Fe(III) ≈ 1:1)
  - electro-reduced FeS / Fe0
  - native Fe0 with NH2OH
  - Ni0 or Pd with H2 (catalytic in metal, H2 as reductant)
  - Ni-bearing meteorite metal
- **Upstream C–C bond formation (CO2 → pyruvate):** greigite, magnetite, awaruite (with H2); FeS with thiols at high T/P; NiS with CO/acetylene.
- **Organocatalysts/cofactors:** thiols (Weber); pyridoxal with Fe(III)/Al(III) (transamination); Cu(I)/HS⁻ photoredox (cyanosulfidic).

## Recommendations for synthesis-network design

1. **Model pyruvate ⇄ alanine as a reversible, multi-catalyst hub.** It is the single best-supported step across frameworks: Fe minerals, Ni/H2, NH2OH/Fe0, pyridoxal transamination, and Bucherer–Bergs with cyanide. Always include lactate as the competing sink. Make selectivity a function of pH (alkaline favors alanine), NH3 activity and reductant redox state.
2. **Treat the C3 source as the rate-limiting, framework-defining choice.** Use either acetaldehyde + HCN (Strecker; needs a reducing atmosphere or local HCN/sulfide) or CO2/H2 → pyruvate (µM yields on Fe/Ni minerals). Do not assume high pyruvate concentrations without an explicit concentrating mechanism.
3. **Use realistic NH3.** Fe-mineral yields come from 0.1–1.5 M NH3. Only the Ni/H2 (Kaur 2024) and pyridoxal-shuttle data show useful yields near 5–6 mM. Flag any pathway that needs molar NH3.
4. **Keep amination steps ≤~100 °C.** The best amination data are at 22–75 °C. Above ~150 °C, alanine decomposes on timescales of days.
5. **Add an explicit, separate enantioenrichment module.** No feedstock route provides it, and claims of natural L-alanine excess are not reliable.

## Caveats and contested points

- **Hydrothermal vs surface.** Contested. Vent chemistry has thermodynamic support (Amend & Shock) and mild-temperature experimental support (Barge, Kitadai, Kaur). It is still criticized for dilution, decomposition at high T, and reliance on lab-prepared minerals, electrochemical potentials or 5 bar H2. Surface cyanide chemistry is criticized for needing reducing atmospheres or local HCN/sulfide/UV, and for incongruence with keto-acid biochemistry.
- **Yields not verified against primary tables.** The Huber & Wächtershäuser (2003) ~50% figure is from a secondary review. Kitadai's alanine is described as "nearly complete" (exact percentage in their Table S1). Miller's alanine figure is given relative to glycine; the commonly quoted absolute value (~1.7% of carbon) was not confirmed from the 1955 *JACS* table.
- **Yield definitions differ** (percent of keto acid, of NH3, of carbon), so cross-route comparisons in the table are indicative only.
- **The NASA/JPL press release** on Barge et al. described the chemistry as "reproducing origins of life on the ocean floor"; that framing outruns the data, which show one reaction step.\[55\]
