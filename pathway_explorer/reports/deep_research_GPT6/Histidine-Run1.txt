# Prebiotic Synthesis of Histidine: Routes, Mechanisms, Geochemical Context, and Outstanding Problems

## Executive assessment

Histidine is unusually difficult to place in a convincing prebiotic synthesis network because its molecular structure requires the simultaneous solution of two nontrivial problems: formation of an **imidazole-bearing C₄ side-chain precursor** and installation of the **α-amino-acid center**. The literature nevertheless contains **four experimentally distinct families in which histidine itself has been reported**: Fischer–Tropsch-type surface catalysis from CO/H₂/NH₃; the mechanistically explicit erythrose–formamidine–Strecker synthesis developed by Chun Shen, Lily Yang, Stanley Miller, and Joan Oró; Miller–Urey-type electrical-discharge experiments; and UV/EUV photochemistry of astrophysical ice containing naphthalene, H₂O, and NH₃. These routes differ enormously in evidential strength and in what exactly was demonstrated.

The **chemically best resolved pathway** remains the Shen–Yang–Miller–Oró route. Erythrose and formamidine generate imidazole-4-acetaldehyde, and the latter undergoes a conventional Strecker sequence with HCN and NH₃ to histidine. Histidine was identified by multiple chromatographic and mass-spectrometric methods and obtained at **3.5% yield relative to erythrose**. The difficulty is not whether the chemistry works—it does—but whether tens-of-millimolar tetrose and formamidine inventories could coexist long enough in a realistic Hadean setting.

The **simplest-feedstock experimental evidence** comes from spark-discharge chemistry. Jiang et al. in *Scientific Reports* in 2014 reported 17 natural amino acids after seven days of discharge through CH₄/NH₃/H₂/H₂O; the authors state that they detected all non-sulfur protein amino acids except glutamate, which necessarily includes histidine. Criado-Reyes et al. in *Scientific Reports* in 2021 independently reported histidine from CH₄/NH₃/N₂/H₂O spark experiments, with about **0.08–0.31 mg histidine per experiment** depending on pH and reactor configuration. These are significant observations, but neither study established a histidine-specific reaction sequence.

The often-repeated claim that histidine has never been made in Miller–Urey-type chemistry is therefore no longer tenable—and was already inconsistent with Jiang et al. when it was stated by Vázquez-Salazar et al. in 2018. The discrepancy is a useful warning that the histidine literature is fragmented and that “prebiotic synthesis” has sometimes been reserved for mechanistically defined syntheses rather than untargeted product detection.

There is also a much older and frequently overlooked result. Yoshino, Hayatsu, and Anders and Hayatsu, Studier, and Anders reported histidine in 1971 from **CO + H₂ + NH₃ over heterogeneous catalysts** at elevated temperature. The follow-up used deuterated NH₃ and H₂ and mass spectrometry specifically to address contamination. These experiments establish an alternative surface-catalytic route family, although neither a histidine-specific mechanism nor a histidine-specific yield was resolved.

Finally, Chen et al. in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* in 2008 reported histidine after UV/EUV irradiation of a **1:1:1 naphthalene:H₂O:NH₃ ice at 15 K**, with a histidine quantum yield of **1.6 × 10⁻⁶ molecule per incident photon**. That experiment provides a genuine extraterrestrial synthesis scenario, but the ice composition is deliberately much richer in NH₃ and PAH than typical interstellar ice, and the amino acids were measured after strong acid hydrolysis, so free histidine cannot be distinguished from acid-labile histidine precursors.

The overall evidence therefore does **not** support a single privileged prebiotic environment. A useful evidence ranking for synthesis-network design is:

| Route family | Histidine itself reported? | Mechanism resolved? | Quantitative result | Main uncertainty |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Erythrose + formamidine → imidazole-4-acetaldehyde → Strecker | Yes | **Yes, substantially** | 3.5% His/erythrose; precursor aldehyde up to 1.6% | Availability/stability of erythrose and formamidine |
| CO/H₂/NH₃ heterogeneous catalysis | Yes | No, not for His | Amino-acid products ≤ about 0.1% overall; no reliable His-specific yield in the accessible report | High laboratory temperatures, undefined His mechanism, natural relevance |
| CH₄/NH₃/H₂/H₂O or CH₄/NH₃/N₂/H₂O electrical discharge | Yes | No | 2014: relative detection; 2021: ~0.08–0.31 mg total His | Atmosphere, mechanism, low selectivity |
| PAH/H₂O/NH₃ ice + UV/EUV | Yes, after hydrolysis | No | QY = 1.6 × 10⁻⁶; His/Ala = 0.195 | Unrealistic ice ratios, very low yield, hydrolysis ambiguity |
| Hydrothermal amino-acid chemistry | **No convincing His-specific endpoint located** | — | Other amino acids produced at 200–275 °C | Histidine not specifically demonstrated; thermal destruction |
| Cyanosulfidic HCN/H₂S photochemistry | **No His endpoint demonstrated in the foundational network** | — | Produces other amino-acid, nucleotide, and lipid precursors | Missing imidazole-bearing His branch |
| Impact chemistry from CO₂/N₂/H₂O + Fe/Ni | **No His-specific endpoint reported** | — | Other amino acids synthesized experimentally | Target-specific chemistry absent |

## Experimentally reported synthesis routes

### The erythrose–formamidine–Strecker pathway

This is the most important route for mechanistic network design because it specifies an identifiable precursor sequence rather than merely detecting histidine in a complex product mixture.

Shen, Yang, Miller, and Oró first reported the key imidazole chemistry in 1987 in *Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere*. A typical experiment contained **0.03 M D-erythrose and 0.03 M formamidine**, adjusted to approximately **pH 6.0**, and was heated at **80 °C for 12 h**. No mineral or metal catalyst was added. Imidazole-4-acetaldehyde and imidazole-4-glycol were identified chromatographically and by mass spectrometry.

The maximum reported yields relative to erythrose were:

| Product | Maximum reported yield |
|---|---:|
| Imidazole-4-acetaldehyde | **1.6%** |
| Imidazole-4-ethanol | **5.4%** |
| Imidazole-4-glycol, erythrose + formamidine system | **6.8%** |
| Imidazole-4-glycol, erythrose + formaldehyde + ammonia branch | about **1.8%** |

These values came from Shen et al. (1987), with the first three also stated in the abstract.

The authors proposed an **Amadori-like rearrangement/condensation chemistry** in which erythrose and formamidine generate the imidazole skeleton. Their mechanistic discussion allowed more than one microscopic route: direct condensation followed by rearrangement and dehydration, or initial erythrose dehydration to a reactive dicarbonyl-type species followed by formamidine capture. Imidazole-4-glycol can in principle dehydrate to imidazole-4-acetaldehyde, especially under acid catalysis.

In the 1990 *Journal of Molecular Evolution* paper, Shen et al. closed the sequence experimentally. Instead of isolating the imidazole aldehyde, they added **HCN and ammonia to the first reaction mixture**, generating histidine through Strecker chemistry. Histidine was characterized by liquid chromatography, HPLC, thermospray LC-MS, and tandem MS and was obtained in **3.5% yield based on His/erythrose**.

The second stage is chemically straightforward:

\[
\text{imidazole-4-CH}_2\text{-CHO}
+\mathrm{NH_3}
\rightleftharpoons
\text{imine/iminium}
\]

\[
\text{imine}+\mathrm{HCN}
\rightarrow
\text{imidazole-4-CH}_2\text{-CH(NH}_2)\text{-CN}
\]

followed by nitrile hydrolysis to

\[
\mathrm{HOOC-CH(NH_2)-CH_2-imidazole}
\]

which is histidine. The mechanistically crucial intermediate is therefore **imidazole-4-acetaldehyde**; the immediately preceding Strecker intermediate is the corresponding **α-aminonitrile**. The 1990 experiment is especially valuable because the aldehyde did not have to be purified before the Strecker step.

There is also a related branch in the 1987 study using **0.03 M erythrose + 0.03 M formaldehyde + 0.06 M NH₄Cl at pH ≈6.6 and 80 °C for 12 h**. This produced imidazole-4-glycol and imidazole-4-ethanol. In network terms, however, these are **potential upstream reservoirs**, not a fully demonstrated alternative histidine synthesis. Conversion of the glycol to aldehyde by dehydration is chemically plausible; oxidation of imidazole-4-ethanol to the aldehyde was explicitly acknowledged by the authors as less well established prebiotically.

A further unresolved step occurs in the formaldehyde/ammonia branch: production of the oxidized glycol requires an oxidizing equivalent. Shen et al. considered formaldehyde, erythrose, atmospheric oxygen contamination, sulfur species, or ferric iron as possibilities but did not identify the actual oxidant experimentally.

#### Upstream feedstocks

The attractive feature of this pathway is that its immediate reagents can, in principle, be traced to very small molecules:

\[
\mathrm{H_2CO}
\longrightarrow
\text{formose chemistry}
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{C_4\ sugars\ including\ erythrose}
\]

while HCN/NH₃ chemistry can supply nitrogen-rich species and the final Strecker reagents. Shen et al. themselves regarded formaldehyde, erythrose, formamidine, HCN, and ammonia as prebiotic candidates. They nevertheless already noted that producing useful concentrations of formamidine from dilute HCN/NH₃ chemistry was problematic.

The most substantial published criticism is from Vázquez-Salazar and colleagues in *PLOS ONE* in 2018. They emphasized that erythrose and threose are minor and unstable products of uncontrolled formose chemistry and that formamidine hydrolyzes to formate/formic acid plus ammonia, making simultaneous accumulation difficult. They also argued that the chemistry has no demonstrated evolutionary continuity with the modern ATP/PRPP-dependent histidine biosynthetic pathway.

One quantitative detail in that critique should be corrected. Vázquez-Salazar et al. state that Shen's reaction required **0.3 M** precursor concentrations, whereas the original 1987 experimental section specifies **0.03 M erythrose and 0.03 M formamidine** for the relevant experiment—a tenfold discrepancy. The broader concentration objection remains meaningful—30 mM tetrose is still demanding in an open natural water body—but 0.3 M should not be cited as the primary experimental value.

### Fischer–Tropsch-type mineral and metal catalysis

A second, much older route begins directly from **CO, H₂, and NH₃**.

Daisuke Yoshino, Kyoichi Hayatsu, and Edward Anders reported in *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* in 1971 that reactions of CO/H₂/NH₃ at approximately **200–700 °C** in the presence of **nickel, alumina, or clay catalysts** generated amino acids together with purines, pyrimidines, and hydrocarbons. Histidine was listed among the amino acids they considered definitely synthesized. Overall amino-acid yields were reported in the very low range, reaching at most approximately **0.1%**, but the paper did not establish an isolated histidine yield or selectivity.

The companion study by Ryoichi Hayatsu, Martin Studier, and Edward Anders used **CO, ND₃, and D₂** and mass spectrometric analysis. Histidine, glycine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate, lysine, ornithine, and tyrosine were reported. The use of deuterated nitrogen and hydrogen feedstocks was specifically intended to distinguish products synthesized in the experiment from ordinary biological contamination, a serious concern in the earlier amino-acid analyses.

This pathway should therefore not be omitted from a comprehensive histidine survey. At the same time, it is less useful for a mechanistic reaction network than the Shen route. No sequence such as

\[
\mathrm{CO/H_2/NH_3}
\rightarrow X
\rightarrow Y
\rightarrow\mathrm{His}
\]

was chemically resolved. The label “Fischer–Tropsch-type” describes a heterogeneous surface reaction family involving reduction, carbon-chain growth, and nitrogen incorporation; it does **not** identify the histidine-forming intermediate.

Anders, Hayatsu, and Studier subsequently argued that related catalysis might operate in the solar nebula at much lower temperatures, roughly **360–400 K**, pressures of about **4–10 × 10⁻⁶ atm**, and on magnetite or hydrated-silicate grains. That is an environmental extrapolation from the laboratory program, not a direct demonstration of histidine formation at those low-temperature nebular conditions.

Modern meteoritic work keeps Fischer–Tropsch-type surface synthesis in consideration for some amino-acid distributions, particularly in metal-rich parent bodies, but explicitly notes that more experiments with modern analytical techniques are required to establish product distributions. Glavin et al., for example, emphasized this limitation when discussing amino acids in the Almahata Sitta meteorite.

There is also an observational problem for treating this as a dominant natural source of histidine. Modern analyses of Murchison have failed to identify histidine above approximately **0.1 nmol g⁻¹**, and recent Bennu analyses likewise explicitly report that histidine was not observed.

### Miller–Urey-type electrical-discharge chemistry

Histidine has now been reported in at least two modern high-sensitivity versions of spark-discharge chemistry.

**Jiang et al. (2014), *Scientific Reports*.** A Miller apparatus containing **125 mL water heated to 80 °C** was supplied with CH₄, NH₃, and H₂ from separate 150-mL gas balloons. Electrical discharge between tungsten electrodes was continuous for either three or seven days; CH₄ and NH₃ were replenished twice daily after evacuation of the system. Products were analyzed after AQC derivatization by LC-MS/MS, including selected-reaction monitoring against amino-acid standards.

After three days the authors identified 15 natural amino acids; after seven days they identified **17 natural amino acids** and seven non-protein amino acids/amines. Their summary states that the experiment generated **all non-sulfur-containing natural amino acids except glutamic acid**. Since histidine contains no sulfur, this reported inventory includes histidine. The method had femtomole-scale sensitivity and used chromatographic separation plus tandem-MS transitions, but the main paper does not provide an absolute histidine production yield.

**Criado-Reyes et al. (2021), *Scientific Reports*.** This experiment used approximately **200 ± 20 mbar CH₄, 200 ± 20 mbar NH₃, 100 ± 20 mbar N₂, and 200 mL H₂O**, with a ~30-kV electrical discharge. Experiments lasted 14 days with alternating discharge intervals, amounting to roughly seven days of energized exposure. Buffered runs were maintained at about **pH 8.7** with 0.05 M NH₄Cl, while unbuffered mixtures began near **pH 11.1**. Borosilicate glass, Teflon, and Teflon reactors containing borosilicate pieces were compared.

Histidine was reported in **every reactor configuration**, including Teflon with no borosilicate. In the buffered experiments the reported amounts were approximately **0.08–0.12 mg total histidine**, corresponding to **2.64–3.95 µg per mg crude product**. In unbuffered experiments they rose to approximately **0.20–0.31 mg**, or **7.18–8.21 µg mg⁻¹ crude**. Thus borosilicate glass is not required for histidine production, even though the paper found important glass effects on the broader organic-product network.

The mechanism is unresolved. The supplementary discussion allows classical **Strecker chemistry**, Bucherer–Bergs-type chemistry, and formamide/formate-related routes as possible explanations for amino-acid production generally. None was demonstrated specifically for histidine, and imidazole-4-acetaldehyde was not established as its precursor in this experiment.

Analytically, the 2021 authors identified derivatized products by GC-MS spectral-library matching. Histidine received a high library match, but it was **not among the subset explicitly confirmed by co-injection with an authentic standard**. This does not invalidate the assignment, but it makes it less structurally secure than the multi-technique characterization in Shen et al.'s targeted synthesis.

These experiments also expose an important historical inconsistency. Vázquez-Salazar et al. wrote in 2018 that no imidazole-bearing amino acid had been obtained in Miller–Urey experiments. Jiang et al.'s 2014 paper was already in print at that time, and the 2021 result subsequently provided a second report. The safest interpretation is that spark synthesis of histidine should be considered **experimentally reported but mechanistically unresolved and in need of independent target-focused replication**.

### UV/EUV processing of extraterrestrial ice

Y.-J. Chen, Michel Nuevo, T.-S. Yih and colleagues reported a chemically very different route in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* in 2008. Their starting carbon source was **naphthalene**, not a small C₁ feedstock. Naphthalene, H₂O, and NH₃ were codeposited in a **1:1:1 molecular ratio at 15 K** under a pressure below **5 × 10⁻¹⁰ torr** and irradiated with **4–20 eV photons (62–310 nm)** from a synchrotron.

The irradiation lasted about **240 min**, delivering approximately **1.6 × 10²⁰ photons**, or about 0.5 incident photon per initially deposited molecule. After irradiation, warming, extraction, **12 M HCl hydrolysis at 110 °C for 24 h**, OPA derivatization, and HPLC analysis, 13 amino acids were identified, including histidine. A blank carried through the analytical procedure did not show amino-acid peaks.

Histidine had a relative abundance of **19.5 when alanine = 100** and a reported quantum yield of **1.6 × 10⁻⁶**. Since total alanine was 2.2 nmol in the residue, the relative ratio corresponds to approximately **0.43 nmol histidine**, or roughly **67 ng**, as an inferred amount rather than a value explicitly tabulated by the authors.

The route is mechanistically opaque. UV/EUV processing of aromatic carbon, H₂O, and NH₃ creates radicals and a highly complex refractory residue; the paper did not establish which intermediate constructs the histidine imidazole ring or α-amino-acid center. The post-irradiation acid hydrolysis is also significant: experimentally, the result shows that the irradiated residue contains material that **yields histidine on hydrolysis**. It does not uniquely establish that all measured histidine existed as free histidine in the ice before work-up. This distinction is an inference from the published analytical protocol.

The authors themselves noted that their 1:1:1 ice is not compositionally representative of known astrophysical ices: observationally inferred solid NH₃ is at most around several percent of H₂O in many protostellar environments, whereas the laboratory mixture was equimolar in NH₃ and water. They estimated that their laboratory photon dose corresponded roughly to **4 × 10⁵ years in the diffuse interstellar medium** and more than **4 × 10⁸ years in a shielded dense cloud**, although the energetic EUV component complicates that scaling.

## Precursors and reaction mechanisms

For network construction, the literature can be separated into **identified intermediates**, **plausible but unverified intermediates**, and **endpoint-only detections**.

### Intermediates with direct experimental support

The best-supported histidine precursor network is:

\[
\boxed{\text{erythrose}}
+
\boxed{\text{formamidine}}
\rightarrow
\boxed{\text{imidazole-4-glycol / imidazole-4-acetaldehyde}}
\]

followed by

\[
\boxed{\text{imidazole-4-acetaldehyde}}
+
\boxed{\mathrm{NH_3}}
+
\boxed{\mathrm{HCN}}
\rightarrow
\boxed{\text{histidine aminonitrile}}
\rightarrow
\boxed{\text{histidine}}
\]

The direct experimental evidence is strongest for erythrose → imidazole-4-acetaldehyde and imidazole-4-acetaldehyde → histidine. Imidazole-4-glycol is a substantial coproduct and plausible aldehyde reservoir.

**Formaldehyde** is relevant in two ways. First, it is the canonical C₁ precursor for formose chemistry and therefore a possible upstream source of erythrose. Second, Shen et al. directly used formaldehyde with erythrose and ammonia to generate imidazole-4-glycol and imidazole-4-ethanol. The latter branch does not by itself constitute a completed histidine route because oxidation/dehydration to imidazole-4-acetaldehyde was not closed experimentally in the same sequence.

**Formamidine** is chemically important because it contributes the nitrogen required to close the imidazole ring. Its weak point is environmental persistence: later analysis emphasizes rapid hydrolysis to ammonia plus formate/formic acid, while Shen et al. themselves noted difficulties obtaining it from dilute HCN/ammonia mixtures.

### Proposed but unverified intermediates in electrical-discharge systems

Electrical discharge of CH₄/NH₃/H₂O systems is known to generate HCN, carbonyl compounds, and many nitrogenous organics; therefore a Strecker-like route is chemically possible. But neither Jiang et al. nor Criado-Reyes et al. showed that their histidine passed through **erythrose**, **formamidine**, or **imidazole-4-acetaldehyde**. The 2021 supplementary analysis explicitly treats several broad amino-acid pathways as possibilities rather than assigning a target-specific mechanism.

For synthesis-network purposes, it would therefore be an error to draw

> CH₄/NH₃ spark → erythrose → imidazole-4-acetaldehyde → histidine

as though the intermediate sequence had been experimentally measured. Only the first and last nodes—simple gases and detected histidine—are experimentally connected in the spark studies.

### Surface-catalytic intermediates

The 1971 Fischer–Tropsch-type route has the same problem even more strongly. Its primary result is that CO/H₂/NH₃ on solid catalysts produces histidine among a chemically broad product spectrum. No experimentally isolated aldehyde, nitrile, imidazole aldehyde, or other target-specific intermediate was linked to histidine.

Thus for a formal reaction network, the only defensible representation at present is a **coarse-grained edge**:

\[
\mathrm{CO+H_2+NH_3}
\xrightarrow[\text{Ni, alumina, clay}]{200-700^\circ\mathrm C}
\mathrm{His}
\]

with an annotation that the edge represents unresolved multistep surface chemistry, not an elementary transformation.

### Imidazole chemistry that is relevant but does not yet make histidine

There are multiple routes to prebiotic imidazoles that are often mentioned in discussions of histidine. Oró and coworkers, for example, synthesized imidazole derivatives from glyoxal, aldehydes, and ammonia. Such chemistry demonstrates that formation of the heterocycle itself is not uniquely difficult. The missing problem is **installing the correctly substituted –CH₂–CH(NH₂)–COOH chain at the proper imidazole carbon**. Vázquez-Salazar et al. make this distinction in their comparison of prebiotic imidazole and histidine chemistry.

Vázquez-Salazar and colleagues also proposed a possible imidazole-forming transformation of an **Ala–Ser–Gly peptide motif**, by analogy with spontaneous formation of the MIO electrophilic cofactor in proteins. That work was framed as a hypothesis about nonenzymatic imidazole generation, not an experimentally demonstrated synthesis of histidine from the tripeptide. It should therefore **not** be entered as a direct histidine-production route.

Likewise, modern biological histidine biosynthesis proceeds through ATP/PRPP chemistry and imidazole-glycerol phosphate, but no complete nonenzymatic analogue from ATP/PRPP to histidine has been demonstrated. Similarities in Amadori-type imidazole construction between the Shen chemistry and extant HisH/HisF chemistry are chemically intriguing but do not demonstrate evolutionary continuity.

### Frameworks that make amino acids but have not closed a histidine branch

The foundational cyanosulfidic network of Patel, Percivalle, Ritson, Duffy, and Sutherland (*Nature Chemistry*, 2015) reduces HCN-derived compounds using H₂S under UV irradiation, with Cu(I)/Cu(II) photoredox chemistry accelerating parts of the network. It provides common chemistry for nucleotide, amino-acid, and lipid precursors, but it does not establish an experimental path to histidine. Thus HCN/H₂S photochemistry is relevant upstream chemistry, but **histidine remains a missing branch** of that framework.

Similarly, Takeuchi, Furukawa and colleagues showed in *Scientific Reports* in 2020 that hypervelocity-impact simulations involving **Fe, Ni, Mg₂SiO₄, H₂O, CO₂, and N₂** can generate amino acids while simultaneously reducing oxidized carbon and nitrogen. Histidine was not reported as a product. This is important because it demonstrates that CO₂ and N₂ cannot be excluded as ultimate feedstocks for prebiotic amino-acid chemistry, but it does not yet provide a CO₂/N₂ → histidine route.

William Marshall's 1994 *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* hydrothermal experiments likewise generated several amino acids from NH₄HCO₃/C₂H₂/H₂/O₂ at **200–275 °C for 0.2–2 h** and from NH₄OH/HCHO/NaCN/H₂ near **210 °C**, but the accessible experimental record does not establish histidine as one of the products. Hydrothermal chemistry therefore remains an amino-acid framework, not a demonstrated histidine route.

## Catalysts, surfaces, and physicochemical conditions

The catalyst requirements are unusually informative because the direct routes span the full spectrum from mineral-catalyzed synthesis to reactions requiring no solid catalyst at all.

| Chemistry | Catalyst or surface requirement | Experimental implication |
|---|---|---|
| Erythrose + formamidine → imidazole aldehyde | **No added mineral catalyst** | Runs at ~pH 6, 80 °C in water; heat and solution acid/base chemistry suffice. |
| Imidazole-4-acetaldehyde Strecker reaction | **No mineral required** | HCN and NH₃ provide the reaction chemistry; conventional imine/cyanide chemistry closes the α-amino-acid skeleton. |
| CO/H₂/NH₃ Fischer–Tropsch-type route | **Solid catalyst essential to reported framework** | Nickel, alumina, and clay were employed; later nebular models emphasized magnetite/hydrated silicates. |
| 2014 Miller discharge | **No mineral catalyst deliberately required** | Electrical energy drives gas-phase/aqueous organic synthesis in glass apparatus. |
| 2021 Miller discharge | **No solid catalyst required for His** | Histidine appears even in Teflon-only reactors; borosilicate alters the broader product network but is not necessary for His. |
| Naphthalene/H₂O/NH₃ ice | **No mineral catalyst** | UV/EUV photons at 15 K provide activation. |

Minerals may nevertheless matter **after** histidine has formed. Madrigal-Trejo et al. in *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres* in 2021 studied DL-histidine on antigorite, pyrite, and aragonite. Antigorite and aragonite strongly stabilized associated histidine; differential scanning calorimetry shifted decomposition behavior to roughly **272 °C and 275 °C**, respectively, and IR spectroscopy was interpreted as evidence consistent with some peptide-bond formation on antigorite and aragonite. This is evidence for mineral-assisted **preservation/organization and possibly condensation**, not for mineral-catalyzed synthesis of histidine from smaller precursors.

That distinction is important when building a prebiotic network: antigorite, aragonite, pyrite, and similar surfaces should not currently be inserted as catalysts on the **precursor → histidine** edge merely because they adsorb histidine.

High-temperature aqueous stability is a related constraint. Ito, Gupta, Masuda, and Kawahata in *Organic Geochemistry* in 2006 found that total hydrolysable amino acids in heated sediment/water systems were progressively destroyed: approximately **23.5% decomposition at 100 °C, 62.7% at 150 °C, 90.1% at 200 °C, and 99.7% at 300 °C** under their experimental conditions. No dissolved amino acids remained after 240 h at 250–300 °C. These are not histidine-specific rate constants, but they strongly caution against assuming that amino acids synthesized in the hottest hydrothermal zones will simply accumulate there.

Target-specific thermodynamic data are conspicuously sparse. None of the direct histidine studies reports a complete \(\Delta G\) landscape or rate-constant set for all intermediate steps. Most of the quantitative evidence consists instead of product yields, concentration dependencies, thermal stability, irradiation quantum yields, or time-dependent product accumulation. For kinetic-network modeling, therefore, substantial parameters would currently have to be measured rather than extracted from the prebiotic literature.

## Environmental plausibility across competing settings

### Warm subaerial or geothermally heated waters

The Shen pathway is most naturally mapped onto a **warm, concentrated aqueous setting** rather than an open primitive ocean. Its key imidazole-forming experiment operates at ~80 °C, pH ~6, with approximately 30 mM erythrose and formamidine. Such temperature and pH are geochemically easy to imagine in terrestrial geothermal systems, but the **concentrations** are the difficult part.

A realistic environment would have to solve several sequential compatibility problems: generate formaldehyde; generate and concentrate C₄ sugars; preserve or repeatedly replenish erythrose; generate and preserve formamidine; move the mixture into mildly acidic conditions favorable to the imidazole chemistry; and supply HCN/NH₃ for the Strecker step. Because formose sugar production and the Shen condensation favor different chemical regimes, the pathway is more naturally interpreted as a **multistage geochemical sequence** than a single homogeneous pond reaction. The need for such staging is an inference from the experimentally different precursor and target conditions.

Consequently, the route is **mechanistically strong but geochemically contested**.

### Reduced-atmosphere lightning chemistry

The 2014 and 2021 electrical-discharge experiments support a setting with liquid water plus a locally or globally reduced gas phase and lightning-like energy. Jiang et al. used CH₄/NH₃/H₂, while Criado-Reyes et al. used CH₄/NH₃/N₂ without added H₂.

The principal environmental criticism is the abundance of reduced C and N species required. A globally persistent CH₄/NH₃-rich Hadean atmosphere is not necessary to accept the chemistry itself, but it matters if the experiment is being interpreted literally as the dominant global atmosphere. Impact-generated and other transient reducing environments offer one way around that objection: Takeuchi et al. experimentally showed that metallic Fe/Ni impact chemistry can reduce CO₂/N₂/H₂O while producing amino acids. Yet that experiment did **not** produce a verified histidine branch, so it only strengthens the plausibility of reduced local feedstocks, not histidine synthesis specifically.

Spark discharge is therefore one of the best-supported **endpoint-production environments** for histidine, but much less well supported as a **defined molecular pathway**.

### Mineral grains, planetesimals, and the solar nebula

The 1971 CO/H₂/NH₃ experiments provide a direct reason to consider hot mineral grains or planetesimal surfaces. The catalysts—Ni, alumina, clays and, in later interpretation, magnetite/hydrated silicates—are more explicitly geochemical than those of the Shen or spark routes.

The weakness is the large extrapolation between laboratory and astrophysical conditions. Histidine was demonstrated in the high-temperature laboratory surface-reaction program, whereas later nebular arguments envisioned substantially cooler, extremely low-pressure conditions. Direct histidine formation under the latter conditions was not established.

Actual astromaterial also provides a reality check. The 2025 Bennu analyses found **33 amino acids including 14 protein amino acids**, along with abundant NH₃, formaldehyde, and N-heterocycles, but histidine was not among the detected protein amino acids. A later Bennu study explicitly lists histidine, cysteine, glutamine, lysine, and arginine among those not observed.

Modern Murchison analyses similarly report histidine below about **0.1 nmol g⁻¹**. Thus extraterrestrial parent bodies clearly synthesize rich amino-acid inventories, but they do not presently provide observational evidence that histidine is a common delivered amino acid.

Interestingly, Bennu does contain imidazole-related heterocycles, including imidazole carboxylic acids that have been discussed as potentially relevant precursor chemistry. This suggests that extraterrestrial delivery could have supplied **heterocyclic building blocks even where intact histidine was absent**, but conversion of these Bennu compounds into histidine has not been experimentally demonstrated as a prebiotic sequence.

### Interstellar and circumstellar ices

The Chen et al. experiment directly supports low-temperature irradiated ice as another possible source. Its **15 K** temperature, ultrahigh vacuum, and UV/EUV activation are meant to model icy astronomical grain chemistry, not early-Earth surface chemistry. Thus this route is best interpreted as **exogenous production followed by delivery**.

Its chief strength is that no elaborate aqueous precursor inventory has to be assembled first. Its chief weakness is quantitative: histidine formation is extremely inefficient, and the laboratory ice contained naphthalene and NH₃ in unrealistically high proportions relative to H₂O. Furthermore, Bennu and meteorite analyses show that whatever histidine-producing reactions can occur in model astrophysical ices, intact histidine does not appear to be a robustly abundant product in sampled primitive bodies.

### Hydrothermal vents

Hydrothermal systems are strongly supported as environments capable of redox chemistry and synthesis of some amino acids, but **histidine specifically has substantially weaker evidence**. Marshall's 1994 experiments generated amino acids rapidly at 200–275 °C from several simple precursor systems, yet do not establish a histidine-specific product in the accessible report. Meanwhile, amino-acid destruction accelerates rapidly above ~150–200 °C.

A cooler vent gradient, mineral pore, or mixing zone could in principle avoid this destruction problem, but no experimentally validated **vent-specific precursor → histidine pathway** comparable to Shen's sequence has been reported in the literature surveyed here. Hydrothermal settings should therefore be treated as **possible enabling environments, not currently demonstrated histidine synthesis environments**.

## Experimental limitations and unresolved questions

### The central precursor problem

The fundamental bottleneck is not the final Strecker reaction. Once **imidazole-4-acetaldehyde** exists, classical aldehyde + NH₃ + HCN chemistry gives a chemically credible route to the histidine α-amino-acid unit. The harder question is how a realistic prebiotic environment continuously produces the correctly substituted imidazole aldehyde.

Shen chemistry solves this synthetically through erythrose + formamidine, but both precursors are problematic reservoirs. Erythrose is one member of a complex and unstable sugar mixture, while formamidine undergoes hydrolysis. The pathway therefore needs either concentration/selection mechanisms, rapid production-consumption coupling, or a new route to the same imidazole aldehyde.

### Concentration and sequential compatibility

The direct imidazole experiment uses approximately **30 mM** of both main precursors. That is not an extreme synthetic-organic concentration, but it is high compared with what would normally be expected for globally distributed trace prebiotic products. Evaporation, eutectic freezing, adsorption, mineral pores, or repeated wet–dry cycling could in principle concentrate feedstocks, but none has yet been experimentally coupled end-to-end to the Shen histidine sequence.

This is one reason the concentration criticism should be stated quantitatively rather than rhetorically. The actual primary experiment is 0.03 M, not the 0.3 M quoted in one later critique.

### Unclosed redox steps

The erythrose/formaldehyde/ammonia branch makes imidazole-4-ethanol and imidazole-4-glycol, but conversion of all of those products into imidazole-4-acetaldehyde is not experimentally closed under a single demonstrated geochemical redox system. In particular, oxidation of the alcohol requires an oxidant compatible with the surrounding chemistry.

For synthesis-network design, these transformations should be marked **proposed**, not assigned the evidential weight of the demonstrated aldehyde → histidine Strecker step.

### Unknown mechanisms in one-pot experiments

The electrical-discharge experiments are almost the inverse of the Shen synthesis: they start from extremely simple feedstocks but do not reveal the molecular route to histidine. This leaves several critical unknowns:

\[
\text{Which molecule forms the imidazole ring?}
\]

\[
\text{When is the C}_3\text{ amino-acid side chain installed?}
\]

\[
\text{Is histidine produced directly, or during later aqueous aging/work-up?}
\]

\[
\text{Is the pathway Strecker-like or chemically unrelated?}
\]

Neither the 2014 nor 2021 spark study answers those questions.

A high-value experiment would therefore combine isotope-labelled CH₄, HCN, formaldehyde, NH₃, and candidate imidazole intermediates with time-resolved LC-HRMS to establish carbon- and nitrogen-atom provenance.

### Reproducibility and analytical identification

Evidence quality varies substantially.

The Shen target synthesis has the strongest characterization: LC/HPLC plus thermospray LC-MS and tandem MS.

Jiang et al. used LC-MS/MS selected-reaction monitoring and amino-acid standards with very high analytical sensitivity, but did not report an absolute histidine yield in the main paper.

Criado-Reyes et al. quantified a histidine GC-MS signal, but the target was assigned from derivatized mass spectra/library matching rather than being one of the compounds explicitly verified by co-injection in the paper.

Chen et al. compared HPLC retention behavior with standards and ran a procedural blank, but their material underwent 12 M HCl hydrolysis before amino-acid analysis. Consequently, “histidine produced by the ice experiment” may operationally include **histidine released from hydrolysable precursor material**.

The 1971 Fischer–Tropsch work began with acknowledged contamination concerns; the companion deuterium-labeling/mass-spectrometry experiment was specifically designed to answer them and did confirm histidine synthesis. The remaining issue is not simple contamination but the absence of modern replication and target-specific mechanistic analysis.

### The extraterrestrial paradox

Laboratory simulations can make histidine under both hot mineral-catalytic and cold photochemical astrophysical conditions, yet **modern primitive samples do not show abundant intact histidine**. Bennu is particularly informative because it was returned directly from an asteroid and avoided uncontrolled terrestrial residence; histidine was not detected even though many other amino acids, abundant ammonia, formaldehyde, and numerous N-heterocycles were present.

This could mean that histidine synthesis is inefficient under natural parent-body conditions, that it degrades more readily than simpler amino acids, that model experiments overconcentrate favorable feedstocks, or that the correct precursors were absent. Distinguishing among these possibilities is a major open problem.

### Relationship to extant biochemistry

Modern histidine biosynthesis is unusually elaborate and is linked to purine metabolism through ATP-derived chemistry. The superficial mechanistic parallel between Amadori-type imidazole construction in Shen's abiotic reaction and in biological imidazole-glycerol-phosphate formation is interesting, but comparative biochemical work argues against treating this resemblance as proof of direct continuity.

Thus there are currently two conceptually different possibilities, neither established: histidine was available prebiotically through chemistry such as the routes above and later recruited by biology, or histidine was scarce before life and became abundant only after metabolic evolution. The low abundance or non-detection of histidine in primitive extraterrestrial material is consistent with the latter possibility, but laboratory syntheses show that it is chemically possible to make histidine abiotically.

### Overall route assessment for synthesis-network design

For an evidence-weighted reaction network, the pathways should not be treated equally.

**Tier A — direct, mechanistically connected experimental route:**  
erythrose + formamidine → imidazole-4-acetaldehyde → Strecker aminonitrile → histidine. This is the best route for explicit reaction-network modeling, but its precursor supply should receive a strong geochemical uncertainty penalty.

**Tier B — direct endpoint production, unresolved mechanism:**  
electrical discharge from CH₄/NH₃/H₂/H₂O or CH₄/NH₃/N₂/H₂O; Fischer–Tropsch-type CO/H₂/NH₃ catalysis; irradiated PAH/H₂O/NH₃ ice. These should be represented as empirical source terms until intermediates are established.

**Tier C — enabling chemistry without demonstrated histidine endpoint:**  
cyanosulfidic HCN/H₂S photochemistry, hydrothermal amino-acid synthesis, impact synthesis from CO₂/N₂, general imidazole synthesis, and nonenzymatic analogues of biological histidine intermediates. These frameworks may eventually connect to histidine, but inserting such edges today would exceed the experimental evidence.

The most defensible environmental conclusion is therefore that **no single early-Earth setting currently has both a fully demonstrated histidine reaction sequence and independently strong geochemical support for all required precursor concentrations**. Warm concentrating waters have the best mechanistic target chemistry; electrical-discharge environments have the simplest direct feedstocks; mineral surfaces have historical direct endpoint evidence; and extraterrestrial ices provide a fourth independent route. All remain contested for different reasons.

## Key primary literature and evidentiary map

**Yoshino, D.; Hayatsu, K.; Anders, E. (1971), *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* 35, 927–938, “Origin of organic matter in early solar system—III. Amino acids: Catalytic synthesis.”** CO/H₂/NH₃ at 200–700 °C with nickel, alumina, or clay; histidine reported among synthesized amino acids. This is the earliest direct histidine-production report found in this survey, but with low overall yields and initially significant contamination concerns.

**Hayatsu, R.; Studier, M. H.; Anders, E. (1971), *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* 35, 939–951, “Origin of organic matter in early solar system—IV. Amino acids: Confirmation of catalytic synthesis by mass spectrometry.”** Deuterated ND₃/D₂ experiments and mass spectrometry confirmed histidine and several other amino acids, substantially strengthening the earlier surface-catalysis claim.

**Shen, C.; Yang, L.; Miller, S. L.; Oró, J. (1987), *Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere* 17, 295–305, “Prebiotic synthesis of imidazole-4-acetaldehyde and histidine.”** Established erythrose/formamidine → imidazole-4-acetaldehyde and related imidazole chemistry; maximum aldehyde yield 1.6%, imidazole alcohol 5.4%, imidazole glycol 6.8%.

**Shen, C.; Yang, L.; Miller, S. L.; Oró, J. (1990), *Journal of Molecular Evolution* 31, 167–174, “Prebiotic synthesis of histidine.”** Closed the targeted route by adding HCN/NH₃ to the imidazole-aldehyde-generating mixture; histidine yield **3.5% based on erythrose**, identified by multiple MS/chromatographic methods. DOI 10.1007/BF02109492.

**Chen, Y.-J.; Nuevo, M.; Yih, T.-S.; Ip, W.-H.; Fung, H.-S.; Cheng, C.-Y.; Tsai, H.-R.; Wu, C.-Y. R. (2008), *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* 384, 605–610, “Amino acids produced from the ultraviolet/extreme-ultraviolet irradiation of naphthalene in a H₂O+NH₃ ice mixture.”** Histidine detected in hydrolyzed photoproducts; His/Ala = 0.195 and quantum yield **1.6 × 10⁻⁶**.

**Jiang, L.; Dziedzic, P.; Spacil, Z.; Zhao, G.-L.; Nilsson, L.; Ilag, L. L.; Córdova, A. (2014), *Scientific Reports* 4, 6769, “Abiotic synthesis of amino acids and self-crystallization under prebiotic conditions.”** Modern LC-MS/MS reanalysis of CH₄/NH₃/H₂/H₂O Miller chemistry; reports 17 natural amino acids after seven days and all non-sulfur protein amino acids except glutamate. DOI 10.1038/srep06769.

**Patel, B. H.; Percivalle, C.; Ritson, D. J.; Duffy, C. D.; Sutherland, J. D. (2015), *Nature Chemistry* 7, 301–307, “Common origins of RNA, protein and lipid precursors in a cyanosulfidic protometabolism.”** Important HCN/H₂S/UV/Cu photoredox framework, but not a demonstrated histidine synthesis.

**Vázquez-Salazar et al. (2018), *PLOS ONE* 13, e0196349, “Evolutionary convergence in the biosyntheses of the imidazole moieties of histidine and purines.”** Major published critique of the Shen route, focusing on tetrose availability, formamidine hydrolysis, concentration requirements, and lack of direct continuity with extant histidine anabolism. Its quoted 0.3 M precursor concentration conflicts with the original 1987 experimental value of 0.03 M.

**Takeuchi, Y.; Furukawa, Y.; Kobayashi, T.; Sekine, T.; Terada, N.; Kakegawa, T. (2020), *Scientific Reports* 10, 9220, “Impact-induced amino acid formation on Hadean Earth and Noachian Mars.”** Demonstrates that CO₂/N₂/H₂O can ultimately feed amino-acid synthesis when Fe/Ni impact reduction supplies reducing power; no histidine-specific endpoint was reported.

**Criado-Reyes, J.; Bizzarri, B. M.; García-Ruiz, J. M.; Saladino, R.; Di Mauro, E. (2021), *Scientific Reports* 11, 21009, “The role of borosilicate glass in Miller–Urey experiment.”** Reports histidine from CH₄/NH₃/N₂/H₂O discharge chemistry in borosilicate and Teflon systems. Histidine therefore does not require a glass/mineral surface in this experiment; the individual molecular mechanism remains unresolved.

**Madrigal-Trejo et al. (2021), *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*, “Histidine Self-assembly and Stability on Mineral Surfaces as a Model of Prebiotic Chemical Evolution: An Experimental and Computational Approach.”** Antigorite and aragonite stabilize histidine and may promote condensation; this is post-synthesis mineral chemistry, not a demonstrated precursor-to-histidine catalytic route. DOI 10.1007/s11084-021-09606-3.

**Marshall, W. L. (1994), *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta* 58, 2099–2106, “Hydrothermal synthesis of amino acids.”** Demonstrates rapid amino-acid formation at 200–275 °C from several plausible precursor systems but does not establish a histidine-specific hydrothermal synthesis in the accessible experimental report.

**Ito, M.; Gupta, L. P.; Masuda, H.; Kawahata, H. (2006), *Organic Geochemistry* 37, 177–188, “Thermal stability of amino acids in seafloor sediment in aqueous solution at high temperature.”** Provides an important kinetic/environmental constraint: amino-acid decomposition becomes severe above about 150–200 °C and nearly complete at 300 °C under the studied conditions.

**Glavin and collaborators (2025), *Nature Astronomy* 9, 199–210, “Abundant ammonia and nitrogen-rich soluble organic matter in samples from asteroid (101955) Bennu.”** Bennu contained 33 identified amino acids, 14 protein amino acids, abundant ammonia (~13.6 µmol g⁻¹), formaldehyde, and extensive N-heterocycle chemistry, providing a particularly clean extraterrestrial benchmark. Histidine was not established among the detected amino acids.

**Subsequent Bennu targeted organic analysis (2025, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*), “Prebiotic organic compounds in samples of asteroid Bennu indicate heterogeneous aqueous alteration.”** Explicitly reports that **cysteine, glutamine, lysine, histidine, and arginine were not observed**, while imidazole-related compounds were present. This is currently one of the strongest empirical constraints against abundant extraterrestrial delivery of intact histidine despite rich parent-body prebiotic chemistry.
