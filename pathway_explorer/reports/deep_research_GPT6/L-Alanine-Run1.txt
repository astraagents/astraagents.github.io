# Prebiotic Synthesis of L-Alanine: Routes, Precursors, Catalysts, Environments, Evidence, and Unresolved Problems

## Scope and central conclusion

Alanine is among the most reproducibly generated α-amino acids in prebiotic-chemistry experiments. It has been obtained in electrical-discharge atmospheres, Strecker-type aqueous chemistry, hydrothermal mineral systems, reductive amination of pyruvate, nonenzymatic transamination, shock chemistry, formaldehyde/ammonia and formaldehyde/hydroxylamine networks, Fischer–Tropsch-type gas chemistry, irradiated interstellar-ice analogues, and simulated aqueous planetesimals. The routes differ much more in their upstream carbon and nitrogen chemistry than in the immediate chemistry of alanine formation: **acetaldehyde → alanine by Strecker chemistry** and **pyruvate → alanine by reductive amination or transamination** are the two dominant mechanistic hubs. Stanley Miller's mechanistic work already identified HCN and aldehydes as primary electrical-discharge products and showed that aminonitrile hydrolysis could account for amino-acid production.

The crucial qualification is that **“prebiotic alanine synthesis” is not equivalent to “prebiotic L-alanine synthesis.”** Alanine has one stereogenic carbon. Reactions beginning with achiral acetaldehyde or pyruvate and using achiral reagents normally have no intrinsic reason to select L over D. Miller explicitly identified his product as **DL-alanine**; Parker et al. found alanine and most other chiral amino acids in Miller's H₂S-containing discharge products to be racemic within approximately ±10%; and Bernstein et al.'s interstellar-ice photochemistry produced racemic amino acids. In a particularly instructive recent exception, natural-pyrite photocatalysis produced **15.8% ee of D-alanine**, not L-alanine. Thus, no experimentally mature route surveyed here establishes a general, geochemically compelling, **direct synthesis of L-alanine in substantial enantiomeric excess from achiral simple feedstocks**. A separate symmetry-breaking, enrichment, selective-destruction, crystallization, adsorption, or biological-selection step is therefore still needed to explain specifically L-alanine.

This distinction matters for synthesis-network design. The experimentally supported target at the abiotic stage should generally be represented as **alanine / DL-alanine**, with “L enrichment” treated as a separate node unless a route explicitly includes a demonstrated chiral selection mechanism. The 2024 pyrite result further shows that a mineral surface can, in principle, break symmetry, but also demonstrates that “mineral-mediated chirality” does not automatically point toward the biological L configuration.

### The acetaldehyde–Strecker hub

For alanine, the classical Strecker sequence is

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CHO + NH_3 \rightleftharpoons CH_3CH(OH)NH_2
\rightarrow CH_3CH{=}NH}
\]

followed by cyanide attack,

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CH{=}NH + CN^- \rightarrow CH_3CH(NH_2)CN}
\]

and hydrolysis of **2-aminopropanenitrile (alaninonitrile)**,

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CH(NH_2)CN + 2H_2O \rightarrow CH_3CH(NH_2)CO_2H + NH_3}.
\]

A competing branch is HCN addition directly to acetaldehyde, giving **lactonitrile, CH₃CH(OH)CN**, which hydrolyzes toward lactic acid rather than alanine. Consequently, NH₃/HCN ratio and pH affect whether an aldehyde reservoir is directed into α-amino- or α-hydroxy-acid chemistry. Miller's 1957 mechanistic experiments supported precisely this interpretation: electrical discharge made HCN and aldehydes, amino- and hydroxynitriles followed, and measured aminonitrile hydrolysis rates were sufficient to account for amino-acid accumulation over approximately a week. Addition of ferrous ammonium sulfate did not materially improve the discharge yields, so this classic Strecker branch did **not** require an Fe mineral catalyst.

### The pyruvate–reductive-amination hub

The second central reaction is

\[
\mathrm{CH_3COCO_2^- + NH_3 \rightleftharpoons
CH_3C(=NH)CO_2^- + H_2O}
\]

followed by two-electron reduction,

\[
\mathrm{CH_3C(=NH)CO_2^- + 2H^+ + 2e^-
\rightarrow CH_3CH(NH_2)CO_2H}.
\]

The identity of the electron source differs across frameworks: freshly precipitated FeS/Fe(OH)₂, mixed-valence Fe(II)/Fe(III) oxyhydroxides, H₂ activated by Ni or meteoritic metal, photoelectrons generated in ZnS or pyrite, or Fe⁰ coupled to an N–O reagent. Because free NH₃ is the nucleophile, alanine synthesis generally becomes more favorable as pH approaches or exceeds the NH₄⁺/NH₃ pKₐ of about 9.25; both Huber & Wächtershäuser and Barge et al. experimentally observed maxima in this alkaline region.

The upstream difficulty is **pyruvate supply**. Cody et al. (2000, *Science*) demonstrated pyruvate formation in high-temperature, high-pressure Fe–S/CO chemistry, while more recent Ni–Fe work has produced pyruvate directly from CO₂ + H₂. Beyazay et al. (2023, *Nature Communications*) obtained formate, acetate and pyruvate from CO₂/H₂ over Ni₃Fe at 25 °C and 25 bar; at pH about 6 and a 3:2 CO₂:H₂ gas ratio, the measured products after 24 h included **26.72 mM formate, 0.04 mM acetate and 0.02 mM pyruvate**. Thus CO₂/H₂ → pyruvate is experimentally established, but current pyruvate concentrations are far below those used in many downstream alanine experiments.

## Atmospheric, Strecker, radiolytic, and impact routes

### Electrical-discharge atmospheres

Stanley L. Miller's 1953 *Science* experiment and expanded 1955 *Journal of the American Chemical Society* study remain the classic direct synthesis. CH₄, NH₃, H₂, and H₂O were circulated past a high-voltage spark for about a week. Alanine was one of the major identified amino acids, together with glycine and β-alanine. In the more detailed 1955 work Miller identified the product as **DL-alanine** by derivatization and optical analysis and reported an alanine yield of roughly **1.7% on the carbon-yield basis used in his table** in a representative classic run. HCN and aldehydes were direct discharge products, leading Miller to the Strecker interpretation described above.

Miller's 1957 *Biochimica et Biophysica Acta* mechanistic study is important because it broadened the nitrogen source. Replacing NH₃ with **N₂** in a CH₄/H₂O/H₂ discharge still produced the same general products, although at lower yield. This shows that reduced nitrogen need not always be supplied initially as NH₃: an energetic discharge can perform some N₂ activation. The same work found no substantial catalytic advantage from soluble ferrous sulfate, reinforcing the conclusion that the atmospheric Strecker sequence itself can operate without a mineral catalyst.

Archived Miller experiments strengthen a **local volcanic-plume** interpretation rather than requiring the entire atmosphere to have Miller's original composition. Adam P. Johnson et al. (2008, *Science*) reanalyzed samples from Miller's water-mist “volcanic” apparatus and found a wider amino-acid inventory than in the classic setup. Eric T. Parker, Henderson Cleaves, Jason Dworkin, Daniel Glavin, Michael Callahan, Andrew Aubrey, Antonio Lazcano and Jeffrey Bada (2011, *PNAS*) analyzed a 1958 Miller experiment containing **CH₄, NH₃, H₂S and CO₂** and found 23 amino acids and four amines; alanine and the other abundant chiral products were racemic to within roughly 10%.

The principal geochemical criticism is not the organic chemistry—it is the atmospheric boundary condition. Dustin Trail, E. Bruce Watson and Nicholas Tailby (2011, *Nature*) used Ce redox systematics in Hadean zircons to infer mantle-derived melts near the fayalite–magnetite–quartz oxygen buffer as early as about 4.35 Ga, and argued that such outgassing would not have produced a persistently strongly reducing CH₄/NH₃-dominated atmosphere. That result directly weakens a **globally ubiquitous** Miller CH₄/NH₃ atmosphere, although it does not rule out transient or spatially localized reduced plumes, impact atmospheres, or water–rock systems.

Accordingly, electrical-discharge alanine is **chemically very well demonstrated but environmentally contested in its classic global form**. The most defensible modern version is a local or episodic one: volcanic plumes and other reducing microenvironments supply reduced gases, while lightning supplies the energy. Johnson et al.'s volcanic apparatus and Parker et al.'s H₂S-rich discharge directly support that localized interpretation.

### Proton irradiation and weakly reducing atmospheres

Kensei Kobayashi, Tairo Oshima and Hiroshi Yanagawa (1989, *Chemistry Letters*) irradiated **CO/CO₂, N₂ and H₂O** mixtures with energetic protons intended to model cosmic-ray/solar-particle chemistry. Glycine, alanine, aspartic acid and β-alanine were among the products. This route is especially significant for network design because it provides a pathway from **N₂ rather than pre-existing NH₃** and from CO/CO₂ rather than requiring a methane-rich feed.

Kobayashi and co-workers revisited this energetic-particle framework in 2023 in *Life*, using mixtures of CO₂, N₂, H₂O and varying CH₄. Amino-acid precursors detectable after acid hydrolysis formed under proton irradiation with only about **0.5 vol% CH₄**, whereas spark experiments using the same gas family required at least about **15% CH₄** for amino-acid detection; UV irradiation did not generate detectable amino acids even at 50% CH₄ in those experiments. This does not prove that early solar-particle fluxes generated the dominant terrestrial alanine inventory, but it demonstrates that energetic particles can relax the requirement for a highly reducing bulk atmosphere. Kensei Kobayashi et al. (2023, *Life*).

A major interpretive caution is that energetic-particle experiments frequently quantify amino acids **after laboratory acid hydrolysis**. In such cases the experiment establishes production of alanine-yielding precursors, not necessarily an equivalent concentration of free alanine in the unprocessed environmental mixture.

### Acetylene-to-acetaldehyde-to-alanine

A 2023 *Communications Chemistry* study provides a particularly useful bridge between metal–sulfur geochemistry and Strecker chemistry. In the authors' standard hydrothermal setup, NiS was precipitated from Ni²⁺ and sulfide, and **acetylene plus CO** was reacted in water at **105 °C**. NiS catalyzed hydration chemistry giving acetaldehyde; FeS alone did not give detectable free acetaldehyde, whereas mixed NiS/FeS retained the activity. NiS systems reached acetaldehyde concentrations on the order of millimolar, while catalyst-free controls produced less than about 40 μM.

When KCN and NH₄Cl were introduced into the alkaline reaction matrix, the same study measured **180 μM alanine after seven days, corresponding to approximately 0.02% yield based on NH₄Cl**. At pH 3 the system instead accumulated about **27 mM acetaldehyde and 10.2 mM 2-hydroxypropanenitrile**, with no alanine. Restoring alkaline conditions with ammonia allowed detection of **1-aminoethane-1-ol and 2-aminopropanenitrile**, directly identifying intermediates expected on the acetaldehyde Strecker pathway.

This is therefore unusually strong mechanistic evidence linking a simple unsaturated hydrocarbon to alanine:

\[
\mathrm{C_2H_2 \xrightarrow[H_2O]{NiS} CH_3CHO
\xrightarrow{NH_3,HCN}
CH_3CH(NH_2)CN
\xrightarrow{H_2O} alanine}.
\]

Its weaknesses are equally clear: the reported alanine yield was very low, cyanide and ammonia had to be supplied, and successful synthesis depended on temporal/pH evolution of the mixture. The route is therefore **demonstrated but quantitatively inefficient and geochemically multicomponent**.

### Cyanosulfidic photochemistry

Bhavesh Patel, Claudia Percivalle, Dougal Ritson, Colm Duffy and John Sutherland (2015, *Nature Chemistry*) showed that HCN-based photoredox chemistry can generate precursors belonging to RNA, lipid and amino-acid chemistry from a common “cyanosulfidic” network. In the amino-acid branch, H₂S acts as a stoichiometric reductant and the network generates **α-aminonitrile Strecker precursors to glycine, serine, alanine and threonine**. Copper(I)/copper(II) photochemistry is important in other connected branches of the system.

For alanine the key point is that this is primarily a **prebiotic synthesis of the alanine aminonitrile precursor**, rather than a unique new bond-forming mechanism for alanine itself: hydrolysis still connects the α-aminonitrile to alanine. Its strength is systems integration—amino-acid precursors emerge from chemistry also serving nucleotide and lipid precursors. Its principal unresolved issue is environmental integration: UV access, cyanide accumulation, reduced sulfur, appropriate metal ions, pH changes and reagent transport must coexist or be naturally sequenced. Patel et al. explicitly formulate the scenario in terms of streams, pools and changing geochemical settings rather than having demonstrated that the complete network runs spontaneously as one undirected one-pot reaction.

### Impact/shock synthesis

Chizuka Suzuki, Yoshihiro Furukawa, Takamichi Kobayashi, Toshimori Sekine, Hiromoto Nakazawa and Takeshi Kakegawa (2015, *Geochemistry, Geophysics, Geosystems*) subjected aqueous **ammonium formate and ammonium bicarbonate** to shock-recovery experiments at projectile velocities of about **0.51–0.92 km s⁻¹**, corresponding to shock pressures on the order of **10–18 GPa**. Ammonium formate produced glycine, alanine, β-alanine, sarcosine and amines; amino-acid yields were **below 0.1 mol% of the formic-acid reactant**. The bicarbonate system produced less extensive chemistry.

This establishes shock waves as a genuine alanine-producing energy source but not a high-yield steady-state process. Its relevance depends on impact frequency, the pre-impact inventory of reduced C/N compounds, survival/quenching of products, and transport away from the shocked zone. Because the experimental precursor was ammonium formate, this route also transfers part of the prebiotic burden upstream to the synthesis and concentration of formate and reduced nitrogen.

## Pyruvate-centered mineral and protometabolic routes

### FeS and Fe(OH)₂ reductive amination

Claudia Huber and Günter Wächtershäuser (2003, *Tetrahedron Letters*) demonstrated efficient aqueous reductive amination of α-keto acids using freshly precipitated FeS or Fe(OH)₂. A typical preparation contained **100 μmol α-keto acid, 2 mmol freshly precipitated FeS and 15 mmol ammonium salt in 10 mL**, under anaerobic conditions. Alanine from pyruvate reached a reported maximum of **52 mol% at pH 8.8**; the experimental table reports **50 °C** for the alanine entry, while the general series used reaction times of about 17 h and temperatures extending to 75 °C. Optimum pH values clustered close to the pKₐ of the corresponding amine source, around pH 9 for NH₃/NH₄⁺.

An important network-design detail is that FeS in this experiment should not simply be encoded as a trace “catalyst.” The experiment employed roughly **20 molar equivalents of FeS relative to keto acid**, so the mineral is better regarded as a **redox-active promoter/stoichiometric reducing material** under the demonstrated conditions. Fresh preparation of FeS was important for reproducibility. This distinguishes the route from the genuinely catalytic H₂/Ni chemistry discussed below.

A major historical criticism was supplied by Andrew Keefe, Stanley Miller, Gene McDonald and Jeffrey Bada (1995, *PNAS*). Testing a broader proposed FeS/H₂S autotrophic scheme, they found no amino acids, purines or pyrimidines from CO₂ with FeS/H₂S and failed to reproduce the proposed production of amino acids from carboxylic acids by reductive amination/carboxylation. They concluded that the particular **CO₂ + FeS + H₂S autotrophic network** they tested lacked geological robustness. This critique does **not** negate Huber & Wächtershäuser's later demonstration that externally supplied α-keto acids such as pyruvate can be reductively aminated; rather, it identifies the upstream generation of those keto acids from CO₂ as the key distinction.

### Transition-metal sulfides in simulated vent fluids

Yehor Novikov and Shelley Copley (2013, *PNAS*) mapped pyruvate chemistry over transition-metal sulfide minerals between **25 and 110 °C**. In a representative vent-fluid experiment they used **70 mM NH₄Cl, 20 mM H₂S, 100 mM H₂ and 100 mM CO₂**; products across the network ranged from about **0.1 to 60% of initial pyruvate**, depending on mineral and conditions. Alanine was formed when ammonium was present with each mineral tested for that comparison, but competing reduction, aldol, decarboxylation and other reactions frequently dominated.

This study is important because it demonstrates that “a catalyst can make alanine” is insufficient for evaluating a prebiotic network. Mineral identity changes the entire **reaction landscape**. The alanine yield reflects not only acceleration of reductive amination but acceleration or suppression of competing pyruvate sinks. Novikov and Copley explicitly noted that NH₃ concentrations on the primitive seafloor are difficult to constrain, so their 70 mM NH₄Cl condition should be regarded as an experimental probe rather than an established Archean seawater concentration.

### Mixed-valence iron oxyhydroxides

Laura Barge, Erika Flores, Marc Baum, David VanderVelde and Michael Russell (2019, *PNAS*) obtained some of the highest reported alanine fractions in a geochemically motivated mineral experiment. Pyruvate plus ammonia was reacted with freshly precipitated iron oxyhydroxides spanning Fe(II):Fe(III) ratios, **pH 5–11**, and **25–80 °C**. Maximum alanine production occurred near **Fe(II):Fe(III) = 1:1**, alkaline pH and moderately warm temperature. At **pH 10, 70 °C and 1:1 Fe(II):Fe(III)**, the liquid organic products approached about **70% alanine and 30% lactate** after 48–72 h. These values are relative product abundances/pyruvate conversion in the analyzed liquid mixture, rather than an isolated preparative yield.

The redox dependence was striking. Fully ferric precipitates produced essentially no reaction; highly ferrous precipitates favored lactate; **partially oxidized iron** was required for strong alanine formation. Likewise, neutral pH favored lactate, whereas alanine rose steeply above about pH 9. At pH 9.2 and 50% Fe(II), only roughly 15% of pyruvate reacted at room temperature over 72 h, producing only several percent alanine/lactate, whereas at **50 °C and above approximately 60% alanine** accumulated after 72 h.

The paper also supplies an unusually valuable negative result. When the investigators grew laboratory hydrothermal-chimney structures rather than using closed sediment-like batch vials, **alanine was not detected in the chimney experiments**, although lactate could form. Thus, the evidence favors reactive wet sediments or long-residence-time mineral zones much more strongly than it demonstrates alanine production in a flowing vent chimney. The authors also explicitly cautioned that their excess of ammonia relative to pyruvate may exceed natural prebiotic conditions and could inflate alanine yield. This makes the route highly promising experimentally but still environmentally conditional.

### ZnS photocatalysis

Wei Wang, Qiliang Li, Bin Yang, Xiaoyang Liu, Yanqiang Yang and Wenhui Su (2012, *Chemical Communications*) demonstrated reversible amination/deamination chemistry on illuminated sphalerite **ZnS**. Pyruvate was among the α-keto acids converted to its corresponding amino acid, alanine. Representative experiments were performed around **pH 9, 30 °C and several hours of irradiation**; sulfite could serve as a sacrificial hole scavenger. Controls established that illumination and ZnS were required for the photocatalytic chemistry.

Mechanistically, photoexcitation supplies conduction-band electrons that reduce the carbonyl/NH₃-derived Schiff-base intermediate. This makes ZnS qualitatively different from stoichiometric FeS. The environmental criticism is straightforward: **photochemistry requires light**, so although ZnS may be relevant at illuminated terrestrial pools, shallow vents, mineral surfaces or volcanic settings, Novikov and Copley correctly noted that such a ZnS photochemical mechanism cannot operate in a dark deep-ocean vent interior.

### Natural pyrite photocatalysis and the chirality problem

Ruiqi Li, Quanzheng Deng, Lu Han, Tianwei Ouyang, Shunai Che, Yuxi Fang and colleagues (2024, *Nature Communications*) irradiated natural pyrite in the presence of α-keto acids and ammonia. For alanine, the reported reaction used **40 μmol pyruvic acid, 4 mmol NH₃, 10 mg natural pyrite in 2 mL H₂O, Xe-lamp irradiation, 20 °C, 24 h**. Alanine formation was on the order of **7 × 10² nmol**, about **1.8 mol% relative to the 40 μmol pyruvate charge**, under the reported optimized setup.

The unusually important result was stereochemical: alanine had **15.8% ee in favor of D-alanine**; D excesses were also reported for isoleucine, glutamate and phenylalanine. The authors attribute the effect to enantiodifferentiating adsorption/reduction on natural pyrite surfaces and supported the proposal with surface calculations. Whatever the ultimate interpretation of that mechanism, it is direct evidence that an inorganic mineral can influence chirality during amino-acid formation—but the sign is **opposite to the requested target L-alanine**.

Accordingly, this paper should not be cited as an explanation for biological L-alanine. It instead establishes a stronger and more interesting constraint: mineral-induced asymmetric synthesis is chemically possible, but the geological distribution of surface chirality and the reason biological systems came to use L-amino acids remain unresolved.

### H₂/Ni reductive amination

Harpreet Kaur, Sophia Rauscher, Emilie Werner, Youngdong Song, Jing Yi, Wahnyalo Kazöne, William Martin, Harun Tüysüz, Joseph Moran and colleagues (2024, *Chem*) showed that H₂ can drive a metabolism-like network containing both keto-acid chemistry and amino-acid synthesis. Their standard pyruvate test used **30 mM pyruvate, water at pH 7–8, 22 °C, 24 h and 5 bar H₂**, with catalytic Ni or other metals and NH₄⁺ between **6 and 150 mM**.

With only **6 mM ammonia and 30 mM pyruvate**, alanine reached **up to 17% yield with Ni** and **44% with Pd**, with these low-ammonia yields calculated relative to ammonia. Pd is geochemically rarer and therefore much less compelling as an abundant prebiotic catalyst, whereas Ni is both biologically and geochemically salient. With powdered **Aletai iron meteorite containing about 18 mol% Ni**, the reported alanine yield was **24%**, accompanied by **43% lactate**; ten tested iron meteorites generated amino/hydroxy acids. Crucially, removal of H₂ abolished product formation, showing that the meteoritic metal was acting catalytically rather than simply being consumed as the stoichiometric electron donor.

This chemistry has a good environmental match to H₂-producing serpentinization. Deborah Kelley and colleagues' 2005 *Science* field study of the Lost City hydrothermal field measured fluids rich in H₂ and CH₄ at **pH 9–11 and temperatures below 40 to about 90 °C**, providing a modern natural analogue for alkaline, H₂-rich water–rock chemistry. The Kaur reaction itself works under milder, near-neutral conditions, so it does not require every aspect of Lost City chemistry simultaneously.

Its largest bottleneck is again upstream carbon supply. Kaur et al. used **30 mM pyruvate**, whereas Beyazay et al.'s direct CO₂/H₂ fixation experiment gave about **0.02 mM pyruvate** at room temperature. Comparing experiments run under different conditions is not a mass-balance proof, but the roughly three-orders-of-magnitude concentration difference illustrates the present integration problem: **CO₂ → pyruvate** and **pyruvate → alanine** have separately been demonstrated, but a natural one-pot or continuously coupled system producing alanine at the high downstream yields has not yet been demonstrated.

### Hydroxylamine/iron chemistry

Kamila Muchowska, Sreejith Varma and Joseph Moran (2019, *Nature*) reported an Fe²⁺-promoted network in which pyruvate and glyoxylate generated much of the carbon skeleton chemistry of the TCA/glyoxylate cycles. When **hydroxylamine hydrochloride (six equivalents)** and **Fe⁰ powder (ten equivalents)** were introduced, glycine, alanine, aspartate and glutamate appeared. Reactions of the four corresponding α-keto acids under related conditions supported reductive nitrogen incorporation as the source of the amino acids.

The route is interesting because it uses an **N–O compound rather than free ammonia** as the nitrogen entry point. Its weakness is therefore shifted to hydroxylamine: the amount, persistence and continuous geochemical production of NH₂OH in candidate early-Earth environments must be independently justified. The Fe⁰ requirement is also more restrictive than generic “iron mineral” chemistry because metallic iron requires strongly reducing conditions or meteoritic/native-metal sources.

### Nonenzymatic transamination with glycine

R. Trent Stubbs, Mahipal Yadav, Ramanarayanan Krishnamurthy and Greg Springsteen (2020, *Nature Chemistry*) demonstrated that pyruvate can accept an amino group from glycine:

\[
\mathrm{pyruvate + glycine \rightarrow alanine + glyoxylate}.
\]

Using **350 mM glycine**, approximately **1.2 equivalents of pyruvate**, **pH 5**, **0.1 equivalent potassium alum [KAl(SO₄)₂]** and **80 °C**, they reported about **19% alanine after 24 h**.

This route has an attractive biochemical congruence because extant metabolism heavily uses transamination rather than direct reductive amination for amino-acid interconversion. But it does **not fix new nitrogen into organic matter**: it moves nitrogen from glycine into alanine while generating glyoxylate. Thus a prebiotic network using this route must already possess a viable source of glycine. Also, although the paper's carbon-skeleton cycle is described as “metal-free,” the reported transamination experiment used catalytic alum, so the alanine-forming step should not be encoded as literally metal-free.

## Formaldehyde, hydrothermal, Fischer–Tropsch, and extraterrestrial routes

### Formaldehyde plus ammonia

Sidney Fox and Charles Windsor (1970, *Science*) showed that heating **formaldehyde and ammonia** produces material which, after hydrolysis, yields seven amino acids including alanine; glycine was the dominant amino acid. Subsequent discussions of the experiment report heating in the neighborhood of **185 °C**. This establishes a cyanide-free route from HCHO and reduced nitrogen into alanine-containing material.

Its interpretation should be conservative: the experimentally observed alanine appears substantially as a **hydrolysis product of more complex material**, not necessarily as a high steady-state concentration of free alanine in the heated reaction. The chemistry is also poorly selective, generating a broad mixture. It is therefore best represented as a **precursor/polymer network capable of releasing alanine**, rather than a clean preparative alanine reaction.

### Formaldehyde plus hydroxylamine and transition metals

Hiroshi Hatanaka and Fujio Egami (1977, *Bulletin of the Chemical Society of Japan*) reacted formaldehyde and hydroxylamine in “modified sea media” at **pH 5.5 and 105 °C**, with elevated transition-metal ions including Zn²⁺, molybdate, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Co²⁺ and Mn²⁺ and, in many experiments, kaolin or montmorillonite. About 40 amino-acid-like compounds were detected; glycine, alanine, serine and β-alanine were among those more firmly confirmed. High-molecular-weight products released substantially more amino acids after acid hydrolysis.

Martha Ventilla and Fujio Egami reported related chemistry in 1977 in the *Journal of Molecular Evolution*, again obtaining amino acids and oligomeric material from formaldehyde/hydroxylamine in transition-metal-containing solutions. These studies show that N–O chemistry can supply alanine without HCN, but the large product complexity, reliance on concentrated synthetic “sea medium,” post-reaction hydrolysis and poorly constrained natural hydroxylamine inventory make this a **historically important but environmentally underconstrained route**.

### High-temperature hydrothermal synthesis

William L. Marshall (1994, *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta*) showed that amino-acid mixtures can form quickly at hydrothermal temperatures. Systems based on **NH₄HCO₃, acetylene, H₂ and O₂** were reacted at approximately **200–275 °C for 0.2–2 h**; related systems used NH₄OH, HCHO, NaCN and H₂ around 210 °C. Amino-acid/amine formation was not observed below roughly 150 °C in the acetylene experiments.

These experiments establish a high-temperature hydrothermal production regime, but they should not be conflated with the later low-temperature alkaline-vent experiments. The former require a hot reaction zone and a complex reducing/oxidizing reagent mix; the latter synthesize alanine from a defined keto acid under much milder conditions. For early-Earth network modeling, Marshall's route is therefore best treated as a **high-temperature source of amino-acid/amine precursors** whose products would need rapid transport or quenching into cooler zones.

### Fischer–Tropsch-type gas chemistry

Sandra Pizzarello (2012, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*) directly tested whether **CO + H₂ + NH₃** could generate amino acids on meteoritic/mineral catalysts. Five hundred milligrams of catalyst was exposed to a **1:1:1 CO:H₂:NH₃ gas mixture at 370 °C for 24 h**, followed by hot-water extraction. The catalysts included an Fe/Ni iron meteorite, powdered Murchison material, magnetite and bentonite.

Alanine production was strongly catalyst-dependent:

| Catalyst | Alanine recovered |
|---|---:|
| Santiago Papasquiaro Fe/Ni meteorite | **407 nmol g⁻¹ catalyst** |
| Magnetite | **88.0 nmol g⁻¹** |
| Murchison powder | **4.9 nmol g⁻¹** |
| Bentonite | **0.3 nmol g⁻¹** |

Pizzarello (2012, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*).

The reaction is attractive because its primary feedstocks are extremely simple and no preformed aldehyde or pyruvate is supplied. Its weaknesses are the **370 °C operating temperature, low absolute alanine abundance, heterogeneous product spectrum and uncertain correspondence between the laboratory catalyst treatment and specific early-Earth surface environments**. It may be more natural as a nebular, meteoritic or very hot hydrothermal/impact-associated source than as a bulk surface-ocean synthesis.

### Interstellar-ice photochemistry

Max Bernstein, Jason Dworkin, Scott Sandford, George Cooper and Louis Allamandola (2002, *Nature*) demonstrated that UV irradiation of interstellar-ice analogues followed by warm-up generated **glycine, alanine and serine**. Their title is itself chemically important: the products were **racemic amino acids**. The work therefore supplies a plausible **extraterrestrial source of DL-alanine**, but not an explanation of L homochirality.

Bernstein et al. noted that circularly polarized irradiation could, in principle, introduce an enantiomeric excess. That is conceptually relevant to L-alanine, but their reported alanine-forming experiment did not demonstrate a robust L-alanine excess. The evidence should therefore be separated into two claims: **ice photochemistry makes alanine**—experimentally demonstrated; **the same process explains terrestrial L selection**—not established by that experiment.

### Aqueous synthesis in planetesimals

Yoko Kebukawa, Queenie Chan, Shogo Tachibana, Kensei Kobayashi and Michael Zolensky (2017, *Science Advances*) simulated aqueous chemistry inside small Solar System bodies. Their starting mixture contained approximately **2 mmol formaldehyde, 1 mmol glycolaldehyde, ammonia, Ca(OH)₂ and 1 mL water**, initially at **pH 11.5–11.7**, and was heated between **90 and 250 °C for 72 h**. Following acid hydrolysis, the products contained α-, β- and γ-amino acids through C₅, including alanine, together with insoluble organic matter resembling meteoritic macromolecular carbon.

Again, the analytical distinction is critical: much of the amino-acid inventory was measured **after 6 M HCl hydrolysis**, meaning the experiment robustly demonstrates **alanine precursors** rather than requiring all recovered alanine to have existed free inside the planetesimal. This route is nevertheless geochemically attractive for exogenous delivery because aqueous alteration of carbonaceous parent bodies is independently evidenced and the input chemistry uses small molecules expected in primitive icy material. Its relevance to the origin of terrestrial alanine depends on accretion, survival during impact, and subsequent concentration on Earth.

## Catalysts, surfaces, conditions, and comparative environmental support

The experimentally demonstrated catalytic roles are not interchangeable.

| Catalyst/surface or energy source | Demonstrated role in alanine network | Important caveat |

| **Fresh FeS / Fe(OH)₂** | Reductive amination of pyruvate; up to 52 mol% alanine reported. Huber & Wächtershäuser (2003). | FeS used in large stoichiometric excess; not demonstrated as a low-loading recyclable catalyst. |

### Which environments are best supported?

**Alkaline H₂-rich hydrothermal/serpentinizing systems have one of the strongest environment-to-chemistry matches for the pyruvate route.** Modern Lost City fluids demonstrate that serpentinization naturally supplies pH 9–11 water, H₂/CH₄ and temperatures overlapping those used in mineral reductive-amination studies. Ni/Ni–Fe materials can couple H₂ chemistry to carbon fixation and amino-acid synthesis, while mixed-valence iron precipitates provide another reductive-amination mechanism. The weakness is not the terminal pyruvate → alanine reaction; it is generating and concentrating pyruvate and reduced nitrogen at comparable rates in the same place. Kelley et al. (2005, *Science*), Barge et al. (2019, *PNAS*), Beyazay et al. (2023, *Nature Communications*) and Kaur et al. (2024, *Chem*) collectively establish the individual pieces but not yet an end-to-end natural system.

**Surface/volcanic atmospheric settings are strongly supported chemically but more sensitive to assumptions about atmospheric redox state.** Spark discharge readily generates the required HCN and acetaldehyde, and volcanic-discharge variants broaden the amino-acid inventory. Trail et al.'s zircon work argues against a globally persistent extremely reducing atmosphere generated by normal Hadean mantle outgassing, making regional volcanic, impact-generated or otherwise transient reducing environments more defensible than a planet-wide CH₄/NH₃ atmosphere. Miller (1955, *JACS*), Johnson et al. (2008, *Science*), Parker et al. (2011, *PNAS*) and Trail et al. (2011, *Nature*) therefore support a nuanced conclusion: **the chemistry is robust; the spatial extent of the necessary gas mixture is contested**.

**UV-exposed cyanide/sulfide pools are chemically attractive because they integrate alanine precursors with other biomolecular classes.** Their challenge is geochemical orchestration: UV, HCN, reduced sulfur, metals and concentration cycles must be co-located or sequentially transported. The Patel et al. experiments show chemical compatibility of a designed network, not that one specific documented Hadean locality necessarily supplied every reagent at the required activity.

**Extraterrestrial synthesis is strongly demonstrated as a source term but does not identify the terrestrial site of life's origin.** UV ice experiments make racemic alanine, and aqueous parent-body chemistry makes alanine-yielding precursors. Meteorites and dust could therefore augment terrestrial inventories regardless of which endogenous route dominated. Bernstein et al. (2002, *Nature*) and Kebukawa et al. (2017, *Science Advances*) support production; delivery, survival, release and concentration are additional steps.

**High-temperature FTT and shock chemistry should be treated as plausible supplementary sources rather than the quantitatively strongest established alanine factories.** Both directly produce alanine, but Pizzarello's FTT alanine yields are hundreds of nanomoles per gram of catalyst after 370 °C treatment, while Suzuki et al.'s shock yield was below 0.1 mol% of formate. These routes may matter because the early Earth experienced impacts and hot mineral/gas interfaces, not because current data establish them as the principal source.

## Open questions, contested points, and implications for synthesis-network design

### The largest unresolved problem is L selectivity

The literature supports **abiotic alanine much more strongly than abiotic L-alanine**. Miller's alanine was DL; Parker et al.'s major chiral discharge products were racemic within error; Bernstein et al.'s UV-ice products were racemic; and natural-pyrite photocatalysis produced a measurable **D** excess. An origin-of-life network that directly labels these nodes “L-Ala” would therefore encode a conclusion that the experiments do not support.

A chemically faithful network should instead use something like

\[
\text{achiral feedstocks} \rightarrow \mathrm{DL\!-\!Ala}
\]

followed by a distinct, presently uncertain stage,

\[
\mathrm{DL\!-\!Ala} \rightarrow \mathrm{L\!-\!Ala\ enriched}.
\]

Circularly polarized radiation, chiral mineral faces, asymmetric adsorption, selective crystallization and amplification processes are candidate symmetry-breaking frameworks, but the alanine synthesis experiments summarized here do not yet justify treating any of them as an established general L-alanine-producing endpoint. Bernstein et al.'s ice study proposed a possible role for circular polarization but itself obtained racemic amino acids, while Li et al.'s mineral experiment selected D-alanine.

### Precursor availability is often more problematic than alanine formation

Once **acetaldehyde + HCN + NH₃** or **pyruvate + NH₃ + reducing power** is supplied at laboratory concentrations, alanine is comparatively easy to make. The deeper origin-of-life question is how those reagents are generated together. Miller chemistry addresses this for acetaldehyde and HCN by producing them in the same discharge; the acetylene/NiS experiment generates acetaldehyde but still requires added cyanide and ammonia; hydrothermal reductive amination uses pyruvate directly; and Ni–Fe CO₂ fixation generates pyruvate but at much lower concentrations than subsequent amination experiments use.

The concentration mismatch is quantitatively important. The Beyazay Ni₃Fe CO₂/H₂ experiment produced about **0.02 mM pyruvate**, whereas the Kaur reductive-amination experiment began at **30 mM pyruvate**—a nominal factor of about **1,500**. Because the experiments use different reactors and conditions, that factor is not a thermodynamic prohibition or even a direct comparison of attainable steady states. It is, however, a clear experimental target: demonstrate concentration, continuous production, mineral adsorption or a coupled flux sufficient for the upstream CO₂-fixation chemistry to feed the downstream alanine reaction without externally adding tens of millimolar pyruvate.

### Reduced nitrogen remains a cross-cutting bottleneck

Many of the highest-yield routes require NH₃/NH₄⁺ in substantial excess. Huber & Wächtershäuser used roughly **15 mmol ammonium salt per 0.1 mmol keto acid**; Novikov & Copley used **70 mM NH₄Cl**; the optimized natural-pyrite experiment used **4 mmol NH₃ for 40 μmol keto acid**, or 100 equivalents. Barge et al. explicitly cautioned that natural vent ammonia may not have been present in the experimental excess used in their system.

Kaur et al.'s ability to form alanine with **6 mM NH₄⁺** therefore represents a significant improvement in nitrogen economy. Conversely, the Miller and proton-irradiation experiments are valuable because they demonstrate routes that can begin with **N₂**, although energetic activation is required and yields are lower than when ammonia is supplied directly.

### “Mineral catalysis” needs to be chemically disaggregated

The experiments demonstrate at least four chemically distinct meanings of mineral involvement. Fresh FeS can serve as a **consumable reductant/promoter**; mixed-valence iron hydroxides provide a redox-active reactive phase; ZnS and pyrite are **photocatalysts**; and Ni can activate externally supplied H₂ as a genuine heterogeneous catalyst. Treating all of these as a single generic “mineral-catalyzed reductive amination” edge would erase mechanistically important requirements for electron sources, illumination and mineral regeneration.

Likewise, mineral identity controls selectivity. Novikov & Copley's reaction-landscape measurements demonstrate that the same pyruvate can be diverted among alanine, lactate, propionate and C–C-coupled products depending on surface and fluid composition. A realistic synthesis network therefore needs **competitive sinks**, not only the desired alanine-forming edge.

### Batch yield can overstate environmental performance

Barge et al.'s approximately 60–70% alanine fractions are among the strongest quantitative results, but their flow-through chimney model did not reproduce the alanine synthesis. This is a direct warning that long batch residence times, high reactant/mineral contact, alkaline bulk pH and large NH₃ inventories can make a reaction look considerably more favorable than it is in an open geochemical system.

Similar cautions apply elsewhere: powdered meteorites provide far greater reactive area than intact rocks; high-pressure H₂ reactors maintain gas activity not necessarily present in an open pool; acid hydrolysis can turn bound precursors into analytically measured alanine; and high-temperature catalyst extraction can release surface-bound amino acids that may not have been freely dissolved during synthesis. Kaur et al., Kebukawa et al. and Pizzarello each provide examples where those distinctions materially affect how a “yield” should be interpreted.

### Congruence with extant biochemistry is suggestive, not proof

Pyruvate sits at the center of modern metabolism, and modern alanine biosynthesis commonly uses transamination. The Stubbs transamination chemistry and the Kaur/Muchowska metabolism-like networks therefore show notable structural overlap with contemporary biochemical organization. That congruence is scientifically interesting because it reduces the conceptual gap between prebiotic reactions and metabolic topology. It cannot, by itself, establish historical continuity: chemical networks may converge on pyruvate because pyruvate is intrinsically versatile, whether or not a particular laboratory network was ancestral to metabolism.

The converse critique applies to Strecker chemistry. It is exceptionally robust for making amino acids but does not resemble modern alanine biosynthesis closely. That biochemical incongruence does not make it prebiotically implausible; prebiotic synthesis need not reproduce evolved biosynthetic pathways. The most neutral interpretation is that **Strecker and pyruvate-centered chemistry solve different parts of the evidential problem**: Strecker chemistry has strong abiotic synthetic precedent from simple atmospheric/HCN feedstocks, whereas pyruvate chemistry has stronger topological similarity to metabolism and particularly rich mineral-redox chemistry. Miller (1957), Patel et al. (2015), Muchowska et al. (2019), Stubbs et al. (2020) and Kaur et al. (2024) exemplify those competing but potentially complementary frameworks.

### Overall assessment

No single route currently dominates on every criterion. The experimental literature supports the following balanced assessment:

**For robust direct production of alanine from very simple atmospheric feedstocks**, electrical-discharge/Strecker chemistry has the deepest experimental foundation. Its principal dispute is whether sufficiently reducing gases were globally or locally available; Hadean zircon redox constraints weigh against treating a CH₄/NH₃ Miller atmosphere as the uncontested global state. Miller (1955, *JACS*), Miller (1957, *BBA*), Johnson et al. (2008, *Science*) and Trail et al. (2011, *Nature*).

**For high-yield conversion of a plausible metabolic intermediate**, pyruvate reductive amination is the strongest family. FeS can give about 52 mol% alanine, mixed-valence Fe oxyhydroxides about 60–70% alanine fractions under favorable batch conditions, and H₂/Ni chemistry operates with millimolar ammonium at room temperature. Its major unresolved problem is generating pyruvate and reduced nitrogen at the required fluxes and concentrations in the same natural system. Huber & Wächtershäuser (2003, *Tetrahedron Letters*), Barge et al. (2019, *PNAS*) and Kaur et al. (2024, *Chem*).

**For an integrated surface-chemistry scenario**, cyanosulfidic chemistry is important because alanine's α-aminonitrile can arise within chemistry that also generates other biomolecular precursors. Its strongest criticism is not failure to produce the chemistry but the environmental problem of naturally arranging HCN, sulfur reductants, UV, metals and concentration/separation steps. Patel et al. (2015, *Nature Chemistry*).

**For endogenous hydrothermal networks from CO₂/H₂**, the most consequential progress is the experimental connection CO₂/H₂ → pyruvate and pyruvate/NH₃/H₂ → alanine. Those links are individually real, but present experiments do not yet close the network at natural concentrations. Beyazay et al. (2023, *Nature Communications*) and Kaur et al. (2024, *Chem*).

**For exogenous supply**, both UV-processed interstellar ice and aqueously altered planetesimal material can provide alanine or alanine-yielding precursors. These pathways are independent of the detailed terrestrial atmosphere, but they introduce delivery, survival and release requirements and do not solve the L-enantiomer problem. Bernstein et al. (2002, *Nature*) and Kebukawa et al. (2017, *Science Advances*).

Most importantly for a synthesis-network model, the evidence presently supports **multiple parallel inputs into a common alanine pool** rather than a uniquely established route. A defensible network would contain at least the independent hubs **acetaldehyde/HCN → alaninonitrile → alanine**, **pyruvate/NH₃ → alanine**, **pyruvate/glycine → alanine**, complex formaldehyde/N chemistry → hydrolysable alanine precursors, and exogenous alanine/precursors. It should explicitly represent pyruvate, acetaldehyde, HCN, 2-aminopropanenitrile, lactonitrile, glycine, glyoxylate, NH₃/NH₄⁺, and the relevant electron donors as intermediate or resource nodes, and it should keep **DL-alanine → L-alanine enrichment** separate from alanine synthesis itself. The present experimental record strongly supports the first problem; it does **not yet provide a consensus solution to the second**.
