# Prebiotic Synthesis of Threonine: Routes, Intermediates, Catalysts, Environments, and Unresolved Problems

## Scope and overall assessment

Threonine is a comparatively difficult proteinogenic amino acid to account for prebiotically because its four-carbon skeleton contains **two adjacent stereogenic centers and a β-hydroxyl group**. The literature nevertheless contains several genuinely distinct routes to threonine or to chemically immediate threonine precursors. They span aqueous HCN chemistry, Strecker chemistry, aldehyde–amino-acid condensation, atmospheric electrical discharge, ionizing-radiation chemistry, and extraterrestrial ice photochemistry. The evidentiary strength is very uneven: some experiments isolated or chromatographically identified threonine after hydrolysis, whereas others stopped at lactaldehyde or the threonine aminonitrile and therefore established a **precursor route rather than free threonine itself**.

The strongest threonine-specific conclusions from the primary literature are:

| Route class | What has actually been demonstrated? | Main feedstocks | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cyanosulfidic/Kiliani–Fischer–Strecker sequence | Lactaldehyde in **19% yield**, followed by conversion to the threonine/allo-threonine aminonitrile | HCN-derived nitriles, H₂S, NH₃, UV; Cu(I) in the original implementation | **Strong precursor-level demonstration**; free-threonine hydrolysis was not the central endpoint of the experiment. |
| Aminomalononitrile + acetaldehyde | D,L-threonine and allo-threonine after hydrolysis/decarboxylation | HCN trimer + CH₃CHO | **Direct experimental threonine route** under mild aqueous conditions. |
| Glycine/oligoglycine + acetaldehyde | Threonine among products | Glycine or glycine oligomers + CH₃CHO | **Direct experimental route**, catalyst-free, pH 3.3–3.7 and 60–80 °C, but poorly selective. |
| Concentrated HCN/NH₃ polymer chemistry | Threonine released from hydrolyzed HCN-derived material | HCN or NH₃/HCN systems | **Historical direct detection after strong hydrolysis**; chemical identity of the immediate precursor is poorly constrained. |
| γ-radiolysis of aqueous HCN/cyanide | Threonine after hydrolysis of irradiated material | HCN, or NH₄CN/NaCN + ionizing radiation | **Direct post-hydrolysis detection**, independently reproduced in the 1970s. |
| Electric-discharge chemistry | Threonine in the H₂S-rich 1958 Miller experiment | CH₄, NH₃, CO₂, H₂S, H₂O + spark | **Direct product-level detection**, but pathway and environmental realism are uncertain. |
| UV photolysis of interstellar ice analogues | Threonine and allothreonine after ice photochemistry/workup | H₂O, C₂H₅OH, HCN, NH₃ | **Direct laboratory production**, with Thr:Gly = 60:100 in one ethanol-ice experiment. |
| PAH-containing astrophysical ice | Threonine detected at relative abundance 13.6 versus alanine = 100; quantum yield \(1.1\times10^{-6}\) | Naphthalene, H₂O, NH₃ + UV/EUV | **Direct detection**, but extremely low absolute conversion. |
| Interstellar lactaldehyde synthesis | Lactaldehyde made from CO + ethanol ice | CO, C₂H₅OH + energetic electrons | **Upstream precursor only**; strongly relevant because lactaldehyde is the required C₃ aldehyde for Strecker threonine. |
| Aldehyde/ammonia “formose-like” parent-body chemistry | Many hydroxy amino acids, but **not threonine/allo-threonine** except trace L-Thr attributed to contamination | HCHO, CH₃CHO, NH₃, H₂O, ± glycolaldehyde | **Important negative result** for threonine. |
| “Complex glycine precursor” alkylation | Acetaldehyde proposed as the side-chain donor for Thr | Irradiation-derived glycine precursor + CH₃CHO | **Proposed, not demonstrated threonine synthesis**; currently speculative. |

Two distinctions are crucial for synthesis-network design. First, **“threonine detected after 6 M HCl hydrolysis” is not equivalent to “free threonine formed in the prebiotic reactor.”** Several HCN, radiation, and ice experiments instead generate nitriles, oligomers, or other bound precursors that release threonine analytically. Second, the best-defined carbon-skeleton chemistry converges repeatedly on **acetaldehyde, lactaldehyde, glycine, or HCN oligomers**, making these more useful nodes for a reaction-network model than “threonine formation” treated as a single black-box edge.

## Known synthesis routes and their mechanisms

### Cyanosulfidic photoredox chemistry through lactaldehyde

The most mechanistically explicit modern route was developed by **Dougal Ritson and John Sutherland (2013, *Angewandte Chemie International Edition*)** and incorporated into the broader cyanosulfidic network by **Patel, Percivalle, Ritson, Duffy and Sutherland (2015, *Nature Chemistry*)**. Its central idea is a photochemical, reductive version of Kiliani–Fischer homologation in which an HCN-derived nitrile is reduced to an aldehyde, the aldehyde adds another HCN unit, and the new cyanohydrin is reduced again.

For threonine, the chemically important branch is:

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CHO + HCN \rightarrow CH_3CH(OH)CN}
\]

acetaldehyde → **lactonitrile**, followed by photoreductive conversion of the nitrile carbon to an aldehyde:

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CN \rightarrow CH_3CH(OH)CHO}
\]

giving **lactaldehyde (2-hydroxypropanal)**. Lactaldehyde then enters ordinary Strecker chemistry:

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CHO + HCN + NH_3
\rightleftharpoons
CH_3CH(OH)CH(NH_2)CN}
\]

followed, in principle, by nitrile hydrolysis to:

\[
\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH(NH_2)COOH}
\]

which is threonine/allothreonine. Ritson and Sutherland explicitly obtained lactaldehyde from the acetaldehyde-cyanohydrin system in **19% yield after 2 h**, and subsequently showed that addition of HCN and excess NH₃ converted lactaldehyde through its cyanohydrin into the **(allo-)threonine Strecker aminonitrile**. Conversion of related aldehydes to aminonitriles proceeded over days to weeks.

The underlying photoreduction was demonstrated under approximately neutral aqueous conditions. In a representative first-stage experiment, Ritson and Sutherland used **10 mM glycolonitrile, 30 mM H₂S, 33 mM phosphate, 10 mol% CuCN, pH 7, and 254-nm irradiation for 2 h**, obtaining glycolaldehyde hydrate in **42% yield**. The same photochemical logic generated acetaldehyde and ultimately lactaldehyde. H₂S acts as a stoichiometric reductant while UV excitation produces the reducing chemistry; Cu(I) greatly facilitates the original photoredox implementation.

Patel et al. subsequently showed that this chemistry belongs to a larger network in which HCN-derived carbon can furnish precursors of ribonucleotides, amino acids and lipids. Importantly, in H₂S-containing versions, Cu(I) is not always indispensable to reduction, so copper should be regarded as a demonstrated accelerator/photoredox component rather than an invariant stoichiometric requirement of every version of the network.

A later alternative photoredox system was demonstrated by **Xu, Ritson, Ranjan, Todd, Sasselov and Sutherland (2018, *Chemical Communications*)**: UV irradiation of **ferrocyanide plus sulfite** efficiently drives HCN reductive homologation to simple sugars and amino-acid/hydroxy-acid precursors. This increases the geochemical flexibility of the reductive-homologation module, although Xu et al. did **not establish a new threonine endpoint comparable to Ritson and Sutherland's lactaldehyde/aminonitrile experiment**.

The strengths of this route are unusually clear intermediates, a measured lactaldehyde yield, aqueous conditions near neutral pH, and a common chemical network with other classes of prebiotic molecules. Its weaknesses are equally clear: the endpoint experimentally emphasized is the **aminonitrile precursor**, not an isolated yield of free threonine; the route requires coordinated supplies of cyanide, reductant, UV, ammonia and sometimes copper; and there are geochemical tensions between abundant sulfide and UV transmission, discussed below.

### Aminomalononitrile plus acetaldehyde

A chemically distinct HCN route was demonstrated by **John W. Thanassi (1975, *Journal of Molecular Evolution*)**. Aminomalononitrile, NH₂CH(CN)₂, is the **HCN trimer**. Thanassi found that it reacts with electrophiles, including aldehydes, under mild aqueous conditions; acid hydrolysis of the products yielded several amino acids, explicitly including **D,L-threonine and allo-threonine**.

For threonine the electrophile is **acetaldehyde**. The reaction can be represented schematically as nucleophilic C–C bond formation at the activated carbon of aminomalononitrile:

\[
\mathrm{NH_2CH(CN)_2 + CH_3CHO
\rightarrow
NH_2C(CN)_2CH(OH)CH_3}
\]

followed by conversion of both nitriles into carboxyl groups under acid hydrolysis and loss of one carboxyl group by decarboxylation:

\[
\mathrm{NH_2C(COOH)_2CH(OH)CH_3
\rightarrow
NH_2CH(COOH)CH(OH)CH_3 + CO_2}
\]

A later experimental paper by **Kuroda et al. (2020, *Natural Product Communications*)**, while extending the aminomalononitrile concept to other amino acids, describes Thanassi's threonine reaction as occurring by **alkylation with acetaldehyde under almost neutral aqueous conditions**, followed by acid hydrolysis and decarboxylation. Thanassi's primary abstract reports that the products were quantitatively measured but the numerical threonine yield is not given in the accessible abstract, so it would be unsafe to invent a percentage.

No mineral or metal catalyst is required for the demonstrated C–C-forming step. The route therefore has an attractive minimality: **HCN → HCN trimer; HCN trimer + acetaldehyde → threonine precursor; hydrolysis/decarboxylation → threonine**. Its main liabilities are upstream. It requires enough HCN to generate and retain aminomalononitrile, acetaldehyde must be available simultaneously, and the laboratory endpoint uses strong acid hydrolysis. It also produces threonine together with its allo stereoisomer rather than solving threonine's stereochemical-selection problem.

For network modeling, aminomalononitrile is particularly interesting because it provides a **C₂N₃ activated carbon synthon generated directly by HCN oligomerization**, rather than requiring lactaldehyde. It therefore constitutes a genuinely independent branch from the lactaldehyde/Strecker route even though both ultimately depend on cyanide chemistry.

### Condensation of glycine with acetaldehyde

A much less frequently discussed but experimentally important route was reported by **C. P. Ivanov and O. C. Ivanov (1983, *Origins of Life*)**. They reacted **glycine and glycine oligohomopeptides with formaldehyde and acetaldehyde** in homogeneous weakly acidic solution. The reactions were run at **pH 3.3–3.7 and 60–80 °C, explicitly in the absence of inorganic solids**. Among the products were the expected serine and **threonine**, together with alanine, glutamate, aspartate, norvaline, isoleucine and nonprotein hydroxy amino acids.

The simplest net threonine transformation is:

\[
\mathrm{glycine + acetaldehyde \rightarrow threonine/allothreonine}
\]

That C–C connectivity is the reverse of the familiar retro-aldol cleavage of threonine to glycine plus acetaldehyde. The prebiotic experiment is especially significant because it does **not require cyanide in the threonine-forming step, metal minerals, UV or ionizing radiation**. Glycine can in principle be supplied by many other prebiotic routes, so this reaction creates a useful point of convergence between otherwise unrelated origin-of-life networks.

The trade-off is selectivity. The Ivanovs obtained a fairly broad amino-acid inventory, indicating that aldehyde–amino-acid chemistry in this regime is not a selective threonine synthesis. The accessible primary abstract gives no isolated threonine percentage or threonine:allothreonine ratio. More fundamentally, the route moves rather than eliminates the precursor problem: it requires a pool containing **glycine or oligoglycine plus acetaldehyde at 60–80 °C**.

Nevertheless, among routes relevant to network design it deserves more attention than it commonly receives because it provides a direct edge:

\[
\boxed{\text{glycine}+\text{acetaldehyde}\rightarrow\text{threonine-family products}}
\]

under catalyst-free aqueous conditions.

### Bulk HCN and ammonia chemistry

Threonine also appears in the older literature on **HCN oligomerization/polymerization**, where the immediate molecular precursor is much less well defined.

**Lowe, Rees and Markham (1963, *Nature*)** demonstrated that ammonium cyanide produces a complex collection of amino acids, amino-acid polymers, fatty acids and purines. Closely related work by **Clifford Matthews and Robert Moser (1966–1967, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* and *Nature*)** showed that HCN-derived polymers and HCN/ammonia reaction products can, after hydrolysis, yield a broad suite of α-amino acids. Matthews and Moser's hydrolysates explicitly contained **threonine**, alongside glycine, serine, aspartate, histidine, lysine and other amino acids.

The key mechanistic point is that this is **not clean Strecker chemistry from a known carbonyl**. HCN produces dimers, trimers, tetramers and higher nitrogen-rich oligomers; water and ammonia then transform these networks into amide-, peptide- and amino-acid-like structures. Matthews and Moser emphasized peptide-like material that released amino acids only after strong hydrolysis. Thus the experiment proves that an HCN/NH₃ network can encode the threonine carbon skeleton, but does not identify a single dominant elementary pathway to it.

This ambiguity is important. A reactor that yields threonine only after **6 N HCl at approximately 100 °C for many hours** has demonstrated a hydrolyzable threonine precursor, not necessarily an environmental pool of free threonine. Moreover, classical HCN-polymer experiments commonly use very high cyanide concentrations and alkaline/ammoniacal conditions, making concentration a major prebiotic-plausibility issue. The chemistry is real; its geochemical deployment remains contested.

### Ionizing-radiation chemistry of HCN

**Sweeney, Toste and Ponnamperuma (1976, *Origins of Life*)** exposed **0.004–0.1 M aqueous HCN** to ^60Co γ-radiation. After irradiation and hydrolysis they identified seven protein amino acids by chromatographic/mass-spectrometric methods: glycine, alanine, valine, serine, **threonine**, aspartate and glutamate. The reported ^60Co dose rate was approximately **200,000 rad h⁻¹**, equivalent to about **2 kGy h⁻¹**. Control experiments were used to exclude contamination.

**Zorica Draganić, Ivan Draganić, Akira Shimoyama and Cyril Ponnamperuma (1977, *Origins of Life*)** subsequently irradiated oxygen-free aqueous HCN, NH₄CN and NaCN and concluded that the process is initiated by **free-radical chemistry producing oligomeric products that release amino acids on hydrolysis**. Chiral products were approximately racemic, and radiation-chemical amino-acid yields varied with radiation dose.

This route needs no mineral catalyst: the source of activation is ionizing radiation and water-derived/cyanide-derived radical chemistry. Its principal prebiotic uncertainty is therefore not chemical feasibility but **dosimetry**. A ^60Co source is a laboratory proxy, not a natural Hadean source; demonstrating that an equivalent dose can be supplied at useful rates by radionuclides, energetic particles, impact radiation or another natural process remains necessary before the laboratory route can be promoted to a quantitatively compelling terrestrial scenario. The analytical-hydrolysis caveat also applies: threonine was recovered after hydrolysis of irradiated products.

### Electric-discharge synthesis

The best direct atmospheric-discharge evidence comes from the archived 1958 experiment reanalyzed by **Eric Parker and colleagues (2011, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*)**. Miller had charged a 5-L apparatus with approximately **258 mmHg CH₄, 100 mmHg H₂S, 250 mmHg NH₃ and 87 mmHg CO₂**, then subjected the recirculating system to electrical discharge. Modern UPLC and time-of-flight MS analysis found **23 amino acids and four amines**. Threonine, leucine and isoleucine were detected in this H₂S-rich experiment but not in the comparison classic, volcanic or silent-discharge sample sets examined by the same group.

No intentional mineral catalyst was present. However, Parker et al. noted sulfur deposition near the **tungsten electrodes**, leaving open the possibility that electrode/sulfur surface chemistry modified product distributions. Consequently, it is safest to describe the experiment as **spark-driven rather than rigorously catalyst-free**. The detailed pathway to threonine was not established; plausible chemistry includes formation of carbonyls, HCN and nitriles followed by aqueous reactions, but assigning a particular threonine mechanism would go beyond the experiment.

The result is nevertheless important because it demonstrates that threonine can emerge from a complex gas-phase/aqueous reaction network without supplying lactaldehyde or aminomalononitrile by hand. The drawback is environmental. The experiment is strongly reducing and unusually rich in CH₄, NH₃ and H₂S compared with most models of the global Hadean atmosphere. Parker et al. themselves therefore emphasized possible **local or transient reducing environments**, including volcanic settings, rather than requiring such a composition globally.

A separate historical line of work by **Matthews and Moser (1966, *PNAS*)** subjected dry CH₄/NH₃ mixtures to electrical discharge and found threonine among amino acids released by hydrolysis of resulting peptide-like residues. This configuration differs chemically from the canonical Miller water-circulation experiment and again points to a complex hydrolyzable precursor network rather than a uniquely resolved threonine-forming elementary reaction.

### Extraterrestrial ice photochemistry

Astrophysical ice chemistry provides two separate direct threonine demonstrations.

**Jamie Elsila, Jason Dworkin, Max Bernstein, Mildred Martin and Scott Sandford (2007, *The Astrophysical Journal*)** irradiated interstellar-ice analogues at approximately **20 K**. In an experiment replacing methanol with ethanol, the starting ice had composition:

\[
\mathrm{H_2O:C_2H_5OH:HCN:NH_3=20:2:1:1}.
\]

The overall amino-acid yield was lower than in the corresponding methanol system: glycine was only about **10% of the glycine yield in the methanol-containing ice**. But the carbon-chain diversity increased substantially. In the hydrolyzed residue, relative abundances normalized to glycine = 100 included **D,L-threonine = 60 and D,L-allothreonine = 30**, along with alanine = 26, β-alanine = 5, serine = 14 and several other products.

This is one of the strongest quantitative threonine results in the entire literature. It is also mechanistically sobering. Isotope-labeling experiments in the same study showed that amino-acid formation in UV-processed ices proceeds through **multiple pathways**, and for the compounds investigated in detail the dominant routes did not conform simply to either a textbook Strecker mechanism or the simplest radical–radical scheme. Threonine's own detailed atom-by-atom pathway was not resolved.

A second experiment by **Y.-J. Chen, Michel Nuevo and colleagues (2008, *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*)** irradiated a **1:1:1 naphthalene:H₂O:NH₃ ice at 15 K** with **4–20 eV photons**. Thirteen amino acids were identified. Alanine was most abundant at an extrapolated **2.2 nmol** in the entire sample; threonine had a relative abundance of **13.6 when alanine = 100** and an estimated photochemical quantum yield of **\(1.1\times10^{-6}\)**.

The naphthalene experiment is relevant to broad surveys because it demonstrates a threonine route that does **not begin with HCN as the supplied carbon source**. Its limitation is efficiency: only a tiny fraction of the PAH carbon entered identified amino acids, and many other organic photoproducts dominated the residue. The authors therefore interpreted it as evidence for multiple possible astrochemical mechanisms, not a selective threonine factory.

### Interstellar production of the critical lactaldehyde precursor

A major advance relevant to the Strecker route came from **Wang et al. (2024, *Nature Communications*)**, who directly synthesized **lactaldehyde** in an interstellar-ice analogue. A CO/ethanol ice at **5 K** was exposed to energetic electrons as proxies for galactic cosmic rays. The experimentally supported elementary step is radical–radical combination:

\[
\mathrm{HCO^\bullet + CH_3CHOH^\bullet
\rightarrow CH_3CH(OH)CHO}
\]

that is, formyl + 1-hydroxyethyl → lactaldehyde. Because radical–radical recombination is barrierless once the partners meet, the chemistry is viable at extremely low temperature. Lactaldehyde was identified using isomer-selective photoionization mass spectrometry and isotopic substitution.

This experiment does **not make threonine**, but it solves an important upstream problem independently of terrestrial cyanosulfidic chemistry. Lactaldehyde made in molecular-cloud/protoplanetary ice could in principle be incorporated into asteroids or comets, undergo aqueous Strecker chemistry with HCN/NH₃ during parent-body alteration, or be delivered to an early planetary surface. Thus two conceptually different frameworks now converge on the same high-value network node, lactaldehyde:

\[
\text{surface HCN photochemistry} \rightarrow \text{lactaldehyde}
\]

and

\[
\text{CO/ethanol interstellar ice chemistry} \rightarrow \text{lactaldehyde}.
\]

### Proposed complex-glycine-precursor route

**Kuroda and Kobayashi (2024, *Molecules*)** proposed a broader “complex glycine precursor” or CGP framework in which energetic processing first creates a polymeric/complex glycine precursor and subsequent side-chain alkylation generates proteinogenic amino-acid skeletons. In their threonine scheme, **acetaldehyde is the proposed side-chain precursor**. The authors discuss hydrogen bonding as a possible source of preference for threonine over allothreonine.

This route must presently be kept separate from experimentally established threonine syntheses. The authors themselves identify kinetic and mechanistic uncertainties, and a complete CGP → threonine transformation with measured yield was not demonstrated. It is therefore useful as a **network hypothesis**, particularly because it again makes acetaldehyde a central C₂ precursor, but it should not be assigned the evidentiary weight of the Thanassi, Ivanov, Ritson–Sutherland, Parker or ice experiments.

## Intermediates, catalysts, and quantitative experimental evidence

Across otherwise very different frameworks, only a relatively small number of intermediates repeatedly matter.

**Acetaldehyde (CH₃CHO)** is the most conspicuous hub. It supplies the methyl-bearing carbon unit in the aminomalononitrile reaction, reacts with HCN to produce lactonitrile in the cyanosulfidic route, reacts directly with glycine/oligoglycine in the Ivanov route, and is the proposed side-chain reagent in the CGP hypothesis. It also occurs naturally among products of energetic prebiotic chemistry.

**Lactonitrile, CH₃CH(OH)CN**, is the key reduced-homologation intermediate linking acetaldehyde to lactaldehyde. **Lactaldehyde, CH₃CH(OH)CHO**, is then the direct carbonyl precursor required by a conventional Strecker construction of the threonine skeleton. The final immediate Strecker precursor is **2-amino-3-hydroxybutanenitrile**, whose nitrile carbon becomes threonine's carboxyl carbon upon hydrolysis.

**Aminomalononitrile, NH₂CH(CN)₂**, provides an alternative HCN-derived route in which the amino functionality and activated α-carbon are already present before acetaldehyde addition. This route bypasses lactaldehyde altogether.

**Glycine** is the minimal amino-acid precursor in the Ivanov chemistry. Since glycine itself is among the easiest amino acids to obtain from discharge, HCN, Strecker and extraterrestrial chemistry, the glycine + acetaldehyde reaction could potentially act as a downstream convergence step rather than requiring a unique dedicated route from inorganic gases to threonine.

Several conclusions follow from the catalyst literature.

There is **no universal catalytic requirement** for threonine chemistry. Aminomalononitrile + acetaldehyde and glycine/acetaldehyde reactions work without minerals; γ-radiolysis and astrophysical-ice routes are energy-driven; discharge chemistry likewise does not require an intentionally added mineral.

Copper has the clearest demonstrated catalytic role in the threonine-specific modern surface chemistry. CuCN/Cu(I) participates in photoredox cycling that accelerates nitrile reductions in the Ritson–Sutherland system. However, H₂S-driven versions can function without Cu(I), and **sulfite/ferrocyanide** offers a distinct photoredox implementation of HCN reductive homologation. It would therefore be incorrect to encode “CuCN required” as an absolute dependency for every HCN-to-hydroxyaldehyde network.

No clay, FeS, NiS or alkaline-hydrothermal mineral has yet emerged from the threonine-specific literature as an experimentally established catalyst comparable to copper in the photoredox route. Generic amino-acid syntheses on minerals or in hydrothermal systems exist, but that is not equivalent to demonstrating threonine formation; the target-specific evidence surveyed here does not justify adding a “hydrothermal mineral → threonine” edge merely by analogy.

A final analytical caution is essential. **Strong acid hydrolysis is itself a chemical transformation.** In HCN-polymer, radiolysis, and several ice experiments, it converts nitriles, amides or oligomer-bound precursors into measurable amino acids. Such experiments establish that the irradiation or polymerization network contained the appropriate carbon–nitrogen framework, but not necessarily that free threonine accumulated before laboratory hydrolysis. Draganić et al.'s finding that irradiated cyanide gives oligomers that release amino acids is particularly explicit on this point.

## Environmental contexts and geochemical plausibility

### Sunlit surface waters, ponds, and evaporative basins

Of the terrestrial environments, shallow surface waters are the closest fit to the Ritson–Sutherland chemistry because the reactions need UV radiation, liquid water, cyanide and a reducing sulfur species. The difficult issue is not whether the organic reactions work but whether all necessary reagents can coexist at useful concentrations.

There is meaningful geochemical support for concentrating cyanide. **Jonathan Toner and David Catling (2019, *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta*)** modeled closed-basin alkaline lakes and experimentally constrained precipitation of **sodium ferrocyanide salts** over plausible early-Earth conditions. Evaporation and mineral weathering can stockpile atmospheric HCN as ferrocyanide; subsequent heating of sediments can release concentrated cyanide salts. This provides a concrete geological mechanism for overcoming at least part of the “dilute HCN” problem.

The sulfide side is more contentious. **Sukrit Ranjan, Zoe Todd, John Sutherland and Dimitar Sasselov (2018, *Astrobiology*)** calculated early-Earth sulfide speciation and concluded that substantial aqueous HS⁻ concentrations are generally difficult to maintain because metal ions precipitate sulfides and because atmospheric sulfur species affect UV transmission. They explicitly identified a possible tension in cyanosulfidic scenarios: H₂S/SO₂ can provide sulfur-derived reducing species, but abundant sulfur gases/aerosols can also attenuate the UV needed to drive photochemistry. The authors did identify local geochemical circumstances—and sulfite/bisulfite chemistry—that can partly alleviate the problem, so this is a constraint rather than a disproof.

Accordingly, a **localized, periodically concentrated surface setting** is better supported than a homogeneous “primordial ocean” for this route. Evaporation, cyanide storage, water-level cycling and spatial separation could provide concentrations that open-ocean chemistry cannot. But no single field site or geochemical model has yet demonstrated simultaneous natural availability at laboratory-effective levels of HCN, ammonia, sulfur reductant, phosphate and photoredox catalyst.

### Reducing atmospheres and volcanic environments

The Miller H₂S experiment establishes excellent **chemical possibility** but weaker global environmental plausibility. Modern discussions generally favor an early atmosphere substantially richer in N₂ and CO₂ than the highly reducing CH₄/NH₃ atmosphere used in classic Miller chemistry. **Cleaves, Chalmers, Lazcano, Miller and Bada (2008, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*)** nevertheless showed that even neutral N₂/CO₂ discharge mixtures can make significant amino acids if nitrite/nitrate-mediated destruction during workup is suppressed, for example by ferrous iron. Their result weakens the simplistic claim that atmospheric amino-acid synthesis requires a permanently methane-rich planet, although it did not specifically establish threonine production from neutral gas mixtures.

For threonine itself, the strongest discharge result remains Parker et al.'s H₂S-rich experiment, whose CH₄/NH₃/H₂S inventory is more plausibly treated as **local or transient**—for example around reducing volcanic plumes—than as a global atmospheric composition.

Impact-generated reducing atmospheres provide another way to generate temporary H₂/CH₄/HCN-rich conditions. Modern atmospheric modeling finds that large impacts can produce reducing episodes lasting roughly **10⁵–10⁷ years**, with HCN production rising sharply when CH₄/CO₂ becomes sufficiently high. Such models do not synthesize threonine directly, but they provide a plausible upstream feedstock source for the HCN-dependent threonine routes.

Thus the atmospheric framework is best viewed as a **feedstock generator feeding aqueous chemistry**, rather than requiring threonine itself to be completed in the gas phase.

### Warm acidic waters

The Ivanov glycine–acetaldehyde reaction at pH 3.3–3.7 and 60–80 °C is chemically compatible with warm acidic pools, volcanic-lake margins or hydrothermally warmed surface waters. Unlike the cyanosulfidic pathway, it does not require UV or a metal catalyst during the threonine-forming step.

Its environmental weakness is quantitative rather than qualitative: no geochemical model accompanying the original experiment showed that glycine, glycine oligomers and acetaldehyde would naturally reach the concentrations and residence times needed together. Since the reaction generated numerous amino acids rather than selectively accumulating threonine, concentrating and preserving the product is another unresolved problem.

### Hydrothermal vents

The literature does **not** presently make high-temperature submarine hydrothermal vents the strongest threonine-specific setting. Although generic amino-acid syntheses have been investigated under hydrothermal conditions, target-specific evidence for efficient threonine synthesis is weak, while experimental degradation data point in the opposite direction.

A 2005 *Chemistry Letters* experimental study, **“Threonine Transformation under Hydrothermal Conditions,”** heated threonine solutions between **121 and 160 °C** and observed conversion to glycine together with other products, including peptide/polymeric material. Thus temperatures characteristic of many vent reactors can destroy or transform threonine on geologically relevant residence times.

This does not exclude cooler hydrothermal gradients, mineral pores or low-temperature vent margins. It does mean that a network model should not simply equate “hydrothermal amino-acid synthesis” with “threonine production”; **formation and destruction must be modeled simultaneously**, and high-temperature exposure may act as a sink rather than a source for threonine.

### Meteorite parent bodies and interstellar ices

Extraterrestrial chemistry has unusually strong direct experimental support for assembling the threonine skeleton. The Elsila ethanol-ice experiment actually produces substantial threonine relative to glycine in the hydrolyzed residue, while Chen et al. independently produce threonine from PAH/H₂O/NH₃ ice. Wang et al. add a mechanistically clean route to lactaldehyde at 5 K.

What is less secure is the transfer from laboratory ice residue to a meaningful early-Earth inventory. Irradiation doses, survival during ice warming and planetesimal processing, conversion of bound precursor into free amino acid, delivery rates and post-delivery destruction all have to be considered. Elsila et al.'s isotope experiments also caution against assigning simple textbook mechanisms to these residues: several pathways operate simultaneously.

Meteorite observations do not currently provide unambiguous corroboration specifically for indigenous threonine. **Koga and Naraoka (2017, *Scientific Reports*)** detected many hydroxy amino acids in Murchison, but the observed L-enrichment of proteinogenic compounds—including threonine—coincided with proteinogenic amino acids in procedural blanks and was judged potentially attributable to terrestrial contamination. In their complementary aldehyde/NH₃ experiment, **threonine and allothreonine were not produced**, apart from a small amount of L-threonine interpreted as likely contamination.

That is an important negative constraint. The experiment used H₂O/NH₃/HCHO/CH₃CHO at a **1000:100:10:1 molar ratio**, with or without glycolaldehyde, heated at **60 °C for six days** under N₂. It successfully generated many other hydroxy amino acids, showing that “aldehydes + ammonia + aqueous parent-body alteration” is not automatically sufficient to make threonine.

Consequently, extraterrestrial threonine is chemically plausible and experimentally producible, but **meteorite occurrence by itself is presently poorer evidence for threonine than for many nonproteinogenic amino acids**, because terrestrial contamination is particularly difficult to exclude for a common biological amino acid.

## Main unresolved questions and published critiques

### The free-threonine versus precursor problem

This is probably the most important interpretive issue. The cyanosulfidic route very convincingly reaches the **threonine aminonitrile**, but a network designer must still assign kinetics to aminonitrile hydrolysis under realistic environmental pH and temperature. HCN-radiolysis and many ice experiments similarly reveal threonine mainly after laboratory hydrolysis.

The distinction matters because nitrile hydrolysis is irreversible enough to pull Strecker equilibria toward amino acids, but its uncatalyzed rate can be slow under mild neutral conditions. Strong analytical hydrolysis therefore cannot simply be represented as an instantaneous environmental reaction. A useful synthesis network should distinguish at least:

\[
\text{lactaldehyde}
\rightleftharpoons
\text{cyanohydrin}
\rightleftharpoons
\text{aminonitrile}
\rightarrow
\text{threonine}
\]

rather than collapsing all four species into one “Strecker synthesis” node. Ritson and Sutherland's observation that aminonitrile formation itself can require **days to weeks** reinforces the kinetic importance of intermediate persistence.

### Cyanide concentration

HCN is versatile but difficult to concentrate in bulk water. The strongest classic HCN-polymer pathways often operate at concentrations far above plausible unconcentrated rainfall or oceanic HCN. Toner and Catling's alkaline-lake/ferrocyanide model is therefore valuable because it provides a physically motivated concentration and storage mechanism, but it introduces its own mineralogical and thermal requirements.

The cyanosulfidic route is less extreme in substrate concentration than old bulk-HCN polymerization, but it still requires a setting able to supply cyanide repeatedly while preventing excessive loss through volatilization, hydrolysis, metal complexation or competing oligomerization.

### Lactaldehyde availability

For a clean Strecker route, lactaldehyde is the central bottleneck. This was historically a weak point because hydroxy aldehydes are reactive and not obviously abundant geochemical species. The situation is now substantially better than it was a decade ago: Ritson and Sutherland produced lactaldehyde at **19% yield** by aqueous photoreduction, and Wang et al. independently demonstrated lactaldehyde production in irradiated CO/ethanol ices at 5 K.

What remains unknown is whether either source creates environmentally persistent, sufficiently concentrated lactaldehyde before it undergoes oxidation, reduction, aldol chemistry, polymerization or other side reactions. This makes lactaldehyde an especially high-value target for future geochemical lifetime measurements.

### Stereochemical selectivity

None of the experimentally mature abiotic routes solves the biological stereochemistry problem. Thanassi explicitly obtained **D,L-threonine and allo-threonine**; the Elsila ice experiment produced both D,L-threonine and D,L-allothreonine, at relative abundances 60 and 30 respectively; radiolysis experiments gave approximately racemic chiral amino acids.

Thus the problem is actually two-dimensional: enrichment of **threonine versus allothreonine** and enrichment of **L versus D** within the threonine pair. No demonstrated prebiotic synthesis surveyed here simultaneously delivers strong threo diastereoselectivity and L enantioselectivity. Claims that one route is especially “biological” because it makes proteinogenic threonine should therefore be treated cautiously unless the stereoisomer distribution is reported.

### Product complexity and selectivity

The cleanest-looking reaction schemes do not necessarily yield clean mixtures. Electrical discharge gives dozens of amino acids and amines; the glycine/aldehyde route gives many protein and nonprotein amino acids; ice irradiation gives a chemically complex organic residue; HCN polymerization generates heterogeneous nitrogen-rich solids and oligomers.

This matters for origin-of-life network design because **absolute yield is not enough**. Competition for acetaldehyde, HCN, ammonia and reducing equivalents may suppress threonine under a fully connected reaction network even when a binary or ternary model reaction works. Conversely, promiscuous networks may be beneficial if the objective is simultaneous access to many building blocks rather than selective threonine manufacture.

### Compatibility of UV and sulfur chemistry

This is the most explicit geochemical critique of the cyanosulfidic framework. Ranjan et al. found that aqueous hydrosulfide is difficult to sustain generically because Fe and Cu remove sulfide as minerals, while sulfur-rich atmospheres and aerosols can shield the short-wave UV required by photochemistry. They also showed that sulfite and bisulfite can be more readily supplied in some surface waters, and Xu et al. experimentally demonstrated useful photoredox chemistry with sulfite/ferrocyanide. The route is therefore **contested in environmental implementation, not in laboratory chemistry**.

An important consequence is that “sulfidic surface pond” should not be modeled as a single unconstrained reservoir. Metal sulfide precipitation, sulfur atmospheric opacity, cyanide-metal complexation and water depth all alter the simultaneous availability of light and reductant.

### Atmospheric composition

Parker et al.'s H₂S experiment is analytically convincing but geochemically nonunique. A global atmosphere with the experiment's very high CH₄/NH₃/H₂S partial pressures is not required—and is difficult to reconcile with many atmosphere models. Neutral-atmosphere discharge experiments demonstrate that organics can still be generated, but the threonine-specific result has not been reproduced at comparable strength under a fully CO₂/N₂-dominated mixture.

The defensible interpretation is therefore that discharge chemistry remains viable especially in **local reducing plumes, transient impact-generated atmospheres, or other disequilibrium settings**, while a globally persistent Miller-type atmosphere is not necessary and should not be assumed.

### High-temperature survival

A proposed pathway must produce threonine faster than the environment destroys it. Direct hydrothermal experiments show threonine transforming at **121–160 °C**, including formation of glycine and other products. This makes hot hydrothermal residence a potentially serious sink.

Consequently, a hydrothermal scenario would be more credible if threonine forms in cooler peripheral regions or is rapidly exported down a thermal gradient. Direct synthesis and decomposition kinetics need to be measured in the same mineral/fluid compositions before high-temperature vents can be ranked alongside surface or extraterrestrial routes.

### The formose/ammonia result argues against assuming “generic aldehydes make Thr”

Koga and Naraoka's 2017 experiment is particularly valuable because it falsifies an overly broad assumption. Their aldehyde + NH₃ system generated an impressive spectrum of C₃/C₄ hydroxy amino acids, yet **not threonine or allothreonine** at convincing indigenous levels.

This means that carbon-counting alone is insufficient. Threonine requires the correct positional relationship:

\[
\mathrm{HO{-}CH(CH_3){-}CH(NH_2){-}COOH}.
\]

Many formose/aldol pathways favor alternative α-, β- and γ-hydroxy amino-acid skeletons instead. In network terms, the route from generic HCHO/CH₃CHO/NH₃ chemistry to C₄H₉NO₃ is **not** equivalent to a route to threonine.

### Congruence with biology is currently weak

The experimentally demonstrated prebiotic routes surveyed here use HCN oligomerization, Strecker aminonitriles, aldehyde–glycine condensation, photoredox homologation, discharge chemistry or radical ice chemistry. None gains strong evidentiary support merely by resembling modern cellular threonine synthesis. The absence of biochemical congruence is not a disproof—modern enzymatic metabolism need not preserve the primordial synthetic route—but it means “continuity with extant metabolism” currently provides little leverage for choosing among these threonine scenarios.

A more productive comparison is at the **network-node level**: acetaldehyde, glycine, cyanide, hydroxy aldehydes and amino nitriles are products or substrates shared across several prebiotic frameworks. That convergence is experimentally better supported than any claim that one complete pathway is uniquely ancestral.

## Comparative judgment for synthesis-network design

For a reaction-network model, the literature supports treating the following edges as experimentally grounded:

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{acetaldehyde + HCN \rightarrow lactonitrile}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{lactonitrile \xrightarrow[H_2S]{UV,\;Cu(I)} lactaldehyde}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{lactaldehyde + HCN + NH_3 \rightarrow Thr/alloThr\ aminonitrile}}
\]

with the lactaldehyde-forming step reported at **19%** under the Ritson–Sutherland photoredox regime.

A second independently grounded branch is:

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{HCN \rightarrow aminomalononitrile}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{aminomalononitrile + acetaldehyde
\rightarrow hydrolyzable\ Thr/alloThr\ precursor}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{hydrolysis + decarboxylation\rightarrow Thr/alloThr}}
\]

with no mineral catalyst needed for the acetaldehyde addition.

A third is:

\[
\boxed{\mathrm{glycine/oligoglycine + acetaldehyde
\rightarrow Thr + other\ amino\ acids}}
\]

at pH 3.3–3.7 and 60–80 °C without inorganic solids.

The radiation, discharge and ice experiments should initially be represented as **network-generating reaction ensembles** rather than single elementary reactions because the threonine-forming sequence has not been resolved. Their experimental output constraints are nevertheless useful: threonine is definitely accessible under those conditions.

On current evidence, the most defensible environmental ranking is not a single winner:

**For mechanistic definition**, the aqueous cyanosulfidic/lactaldehyde route is strongest because individual intermediates and a lactaldehyde yield are known.

**For chemical simplicity of the target-specific C–C-forming step**, aminomalononitrile + acetaldehyde and glycine + acetaldehyde are particularly attractive because they do not require a mineral catalyst.

**For direct experimental production from very simple energetic starting mixtures**, the H₂S electrical-discharge experiment is compelling, but the environmental gas composition is contested.

**For exogenous synthesis**, the ethanol/HCN/NH₃ ice experiment provides unusually strong relative threonine production, and the independent 2024 lactaldehyde experiment supplies a plausible upstream Strecker precursor. The main uncertainties shift from synthesis to hydrolysis, survival and delivery.

**For hot hydrothermal synthesis**, the threonine-specific case is currently weakest: direct synthesis evidence is limited and measured thermal transformation provides a clear degradation constraint.

The single most important experimental gaps are therefore quantitative rather than conceptual: threonine/allo-threonine yields for the aminomalononitrile and glycine–acetaldehyde routes under rigorously prebiotic workups; direct aminonitrile-to-free-threonine kinetics at environmentally realistic pH, temperature and ionic strength; lactaldehyde lifetimes in mixed geochemical solutions; effects of minerals on both formation and destruction; absolute rather than relative yields in ice experiments; and full stereoisomer-resolved product distributions. These measurements would determine which of the many chemically possible pathways can carry appreciable flux in an integrated prebiotic reaction network.
