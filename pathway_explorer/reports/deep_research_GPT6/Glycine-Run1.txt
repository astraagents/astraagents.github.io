# Prebiotic Synthesis of Glycine: Routes, Intermediates, Catalysts, Environments, and Unresolved Problems

## Scope and principal conclusions

Glycine is unusually important for origin-of-life chemistry because it is both the simplest proteinogenic α-amino acid and one of the most reproducibly generated amino acids in abiotic experiments. The literature does **not** point to a single uniquely compelling prebiotic route. Instead, glycine sits at the intersection of several chemically distinct networks: aldehyde–cyanide chemistry, HCN polymer chemistry, glyoxylate reduction and disproportionation, high-energy atmospheric chemistry, impact chemistry, mineral-mediated hydrothermal chemistry, photocatalysis, amino-alcohol oxidation, and extraterrestrial ice chemistry. Many experiments that are colloquially described as “glycine synthesis” actually produce **acid-hydrolyzable glycine precursors** rather than free glycine in the primary reaction mixture; this distinction is particularly important for high-energy particle, hydrothermal, HCN-polymer, and some early atmospheric experiments.

The most experimentally mature route at the level of elementary organic chemistry is the **Strecker network**:

\[
\mathrm{H_2CO + NH_3 \rightleftharpoons CH_2{=}NH + H_2O}
\]

\[
\mathrm{CH_2{=}NH + HCN/CN^- \rightarrow NH_2CH_2CN}
\]

\[
\mathrm{NH_2CH_2CN \xrightarrow{H_2O} NH_2CH_2CONH_2 \rightarrow NH_2CH_2COOH}
\]

with **formaldehyde**, **HCN/cyanide**, **ammonia**, **aminoacetonitrile** and **glycinamide** as the central feedstocks/intermediates. An equivalent branch begins with HCN addition to formaldehyde to form **glycolonitrile, HOCH₂CN**, followed by ammoniation to aminoacetonitrile. This chemistry requires no mineral catalyst in principle. Miller's discharge experiments supplied the carbonyl, cyanide and ammonia chemistry upstream; hydrothermal experiments have instead added those compounds directly.

No environmental setting, however, simultaneously solves all of the supply, concentration, kinetics, and compatibility problems. Classical CH₄-rich atmospheric synthesis has high laboratory productivity but is sensitive to atmospheric redox state; aqueous Strecker synthesis needs concentrated HCN and carbonyl compounds; high-temperature hydrothermal synthesis competes with amino-acid degradation; recent glyoxylate/mineral systems use more metabolism-like carbon skeletons but require a credible source and concentration mechanism for glyoxylate and fixed nitrogen; surface photochemistry requires appropriate UV, solutes, and photocatalysts; impact and extraterrestrial routes are demonstrably productive but episodic or external. Geological constraints on the atmosphere remain debated: Hadean zircons have been interpreted as indicating mantle oxygen fugacities broadly comparable to later Earth, while impact models permit transient strongly reducing atmospheres and a recent 2026 study has argued for a much more reduced late-Hadean atmosphere. Those are competing models rather than a settled environmental boundary condition.

## Cyanide, atmospheric, photochemical, and high-energy routes

### Electric-discharge production and the Strecker network

Stanley L. Miller's 1953 *Science* experiment established the foundational result: electrical discharge through an aqueous/gas system containing CH₄, NH₃, H₂ and water vapor generated glycine and other amino acids. Miller's later mechanistic work showed why the experiment works chemically: electrical discharge first generates **HCN and aldehydes**, including formaldehyde, after which amino-acid formation is largely accounted for by Strecker-type chemistry rather than by direct assembly of an amino acid in the gas phase.

This distinction matters for network design. For glycine, the important elementary inventory is therefore not merely CH₄ + NH₃ + H₂O, but:

\[
\mathrm{CH_4/N_2/NH_3/H_2O \xrightarrow{discharge} HCN + H_2CO + reduced\ N}
\]

followed by

\[
\mathrm{H_2CO + NH_3 + HCN \rightarrow NH_2CH_2CN}
\]

and aqueous hydrolysis to glycine. Formaldehyde can also trap HCN as glycolonitrile, which is then converted to aminoacetonitrile. This route does **not require a mineral catalyst** once formaldehyde, cyanide and ammonia coexist; the energetic burden is predominantly upstream feedstock generation and, downstream, nitrile hydrolysis.

Schlesinger and Miller's 1983 *Journal of Molecular Evolution* systematic atmosphere experiments showed the strong dependence on redox state. CH₄-containing mixtures gave total amino-acid carbon yields of roughly 1.2–4.7%, whereas glycine was almost the only amino acid produced efficiently when CO or CO₂ replaced methane. Their companion work found HCN and formaldehyde yields as high as approximately 10–13% of carbon under sufficiently reducing CH₄ or H₂-rich CO/CO₂ conditions, but synthesis declined sharply when H₂/CO or H₂/CO₂ became too small.

The archival reanalysis by Adam P. Johnson, H. James Cleaves, Jason P. Dworkin, Daniel P. Glavin, Antonio Lazcano and Jeffrey L. Bada (2008, *Science*) is quantitatively useful. Miller's “volcanic” apparatus contained 200 torr CH₄, 200 torr NH₃ and 100 torr H₂ over 500 mL water and was sparked for about a week. Reanalysis found 22 amino acids and five amines in the volcanic experiment. Miller's original quantitative records corresponded to glycine yields of approximately **2.1%, 1.8% and 0.4% of carbon added as methane** in the three apparatus configurations examined. The water-mist volcanic apparatus was chemically richer than the iconic textbook configuration.

Eric T. Parker and colleagues (2011, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) subsequently analyzed Miller's 1958 H₂S-containing discharge samples. Glycine again occurred along with numerous other amino and sulfur-containing acids, supporting the narrower proposition that **localized H₂S-rich volcanic plumes plus lightning** could supply amino-acid chemistry even if the planet-wide atmosphere were less reducing.

The geological critique is substantial. Hadean zircon-based redox work by Trail and co-workers has been interpreted to indicate that mantle-derived Hadean melts had oxygen fugacities not radically different from later terrestrial mantle, disfavoring the assumption of a persistent global CH₄/NH₃ atmosphere. Neutral N₂–CO₂ atmospheres are much less productive in spark chemistry. However, the critique no longer implies that reduced atmospheric chemistry was impossible: impact-generated H₂/CH₄ atmospheres, reduced volcanic microenvironments, and transient post-impact atmospheres provide mechanisms for episodic Miller-like chemistry. Thus the contested point is **scale and persistence**, not whether the reactions themselves work.

### CO–N₂ atmospheres and energetic particles

A distinct atmospheric framework avoids the need for abundant methane. Kensei Kobayashi and colleagues irradiated **350 torr CO + 350 torr ¹⁵N₂ over 5 mL water** with 2.5–3.0 MeV protons at 297 K for 3 h. After 6 M HCl hydrolysis at 100°C for 24 h, glycine and other organics were recovered. The glycine energy yield was approximately **2 × 10⁻⁹ mol J⁻¹**, comparable to proton irradiation of CH₄/N₂/H₂O and about twice the cited energy-normalized glycine output of a CH₄/N₂/NH₃/H₂O spark experiment.

Earlier Kensei Kobayashi, Takeo Kaneko, Takeshi Saito and Tairo Oshima experiments likewise found glycine yields proportional to energy deposition, with a **G-value of about 0.02 molecules per 100 eV** near a CO:N₂ ratio of 1.

This pathway is mechanistically less resolved than Strecker synthesis. High-energy ionization dissociates the very strong CO and N₂ bonds, producing radical/ionic C–N–O networks and HCN among the products. Crucially, much of the analytical “amino acid yield” appears only **after acid hydrolysis**, so the primary products are likely macromolecular or nitrile/amide precursors rather than free glycine. That makes this route interesting for feedstock generation but prevents a simple one-step mapping “CO + N₂ → free glycine.”

Its environmental advantage is use of N₂ rather than pre-existing NH₃. Its weakness is energetic scaling: accelerator experiments use very high instantaneous proton fluxes, and estimates of globally integrated early-solar/cosmic-particle production depend strongly on young-Sun particle spectra, atmospheric shielding and atmospheric composition. Recent proton-irradiation experiments continue to find amino-acid precursors in CO₂/CH₄/N₂/H₂O atmospheres with only small amounts of methane, reinforcing the chemistry while leaving planetary production rates model-dependent.

### HCN concentration, polymerization and freezing

Juan Oró and S. S. Kamat's 1961 *Nature* experiments demonstrated that concentrated ammoniacal HCN can generate glycine, alanine and other amino-acid products. Related Oró–Kimball HCN-condensation work recovered glycinamide and glycine from the aqueous products of cyanide polymerization. This is not simply classical Strecker chemistry because polymerized HCN itself creates a network of C–N intermediates that hydrolyze into amino acids and other products.

The central geochemical problem is concentration. In kinetic treatments cited by experimental workers, HCN polymerization becomes competitive at roughly **≥0.01 M**, whereas at much lower concentration hydrolysis toward formamide/formate and ammonia dominates. One atmospheric-ocean calculation for a weakly reducing atmosphere estimated a steady-state oceanic HCN concentration of only about \(8\times10^{-10}\) M under its assumptions. Such global-ocean estimates do not apply directly to evaporating ponds, aerosols, ice eutectics or local impact deposits, but they demonstrate why essentially every HCN-based origin model requires a **concentration mechanism**.

Levy, Miller, Brinton and Bada (2000, *Icarus*) supplied one such mechanism experimentally: dilute NH₄CN frozen for years at −20 to −78°C continued reacting in the eutectic liquid between ice crystals, and simple amino acids dominated by glycine accumulated. Freezing therefore solves a real kinetic/concentration problem without evaporation, but it shifts the environmental requirement toward persistent cold traps and long reaction times.

### Cyanosulfidic photoredox chemistry

Bhavesh H. Patel, Claudia Percivalle, Dougal J. Ritson, Colm D. Duffy and John D. Sutherland (2015, *Nature Chemistry*) developed a broader **cyanosulfidic protometabolism** in which HCN supplies carbon and nitrogen, H₂S supplies reducing equivalents, UV light supplies energy, and copper cyanide photochemistry can accelerate electron transfer. The network gives glycolaldehyde/glyceraldehyde chemistry and **α-aminonitriles**, including the immediate Strecker-class precursors from which glycine and other amino acids arise by hydrolysis.

For glycine network design, this pathway is best regarded as an **upstream synthesis of glycolonitrile/aminoacetonitrile-class material**, rather than evidence that a pond irradiated with UV simply accumulates large amounts of isolated glycine. Its strength is systems compatibility: the same photochemical network can generate precursors associated with amino acids, nucleotides and lipids. Its contested aspect is geochemical co-location. Significant cyanide, reduced sulfur, appropriate copper speciation and UV transmission must overlap spatially and temporally; high sulfide can also strongly alter metal speciation. The laboratory's narrowband lamps are experimental proxies rather than exact reproductions of the Hadean solar spectrum, although follow-up photochemistry has shown that relevant cyanocuprate electron photodetachment overlaps wavelengths expected to have reached an anoxic early-Earth surface.

## Carbonyl, glyoxylate, nitrogen-oxide, and mineral routes

### Formaldehyde plus hydroxylamine

Juan Oró, A. P. Kimball, Stanley Fritz and Francis Master (1959, *Archives of Biochemistry and Biophysics*) showed that heating formaldehyde with hydroxylamine gives a suite of amino and hydroxy acids, with glycine prominent. Mechanistically this is especially interesting because externally added HCN is unnecessary: **formaldoxime** and related N–O intermediates generate HCN and NH₃ internally, after which Strecker/cyanohydrin chemistry can take over.

Kamaluddin, Hiroshi Yanagawa and F. Egami (1979, *Journal of Biochemistry*) monitored this chemistry in a “modified sea medium.” They found cyanide, **glycinenitrile/aminoacetonitrile** and **glycinamide** before amino acids accumulated, giving experimental support to the sequence:

\[
\mathrm{H_2CO + NH_2OH \rightarrow CH_2{=}NOH}
\]

\[
\mathrm{oxime\ chemistry \rightarrow HCN + NH_3}
\]

\[
\mathrm{HCN + H_2CO + NH_3 \rightarrow NH_2CH_2CN
\rightarrow glycinamide \rightarrow glycine}.
\]

Hiroshi Hatanaka and Egami's 1977 experiments heated formaldehyde/hydroxylamine media near **pH 5.5 and 105°C** with mixtures of Zn²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Co²⁺, Mn²⁺, molybdate and clay minerals. Glycine, alanine, serine and β-alanine were among the confirmed products. Molybdate changed amino-acid selectivity rather than simply increasing everything: increasing molybdate favored several other amino acids while **decreasing glycine and serine**. Thus this is an experimentally demonstrated example in which a mineral/metal component controls network branching rather than serving as a generic “catalyst.”

The major unresolved issue is upstream **hydroxylamine supply**. N–O compounds are chemically attractive fixed-nitrogen intermediates, but no consensus inventory demonstrates that hydroxylamine would have reached the concentrations used in these experiments in a particular Hadean environment. The metal-rich “modified seawater” recipes also should not automatically be identified with real Hadean seawater.

### Formaldehyde plus ammonia without externally supplied cyanide

Sidney W. Fox and C. R. Windsor (1970, *Science*) showed that heating formaldehyde with ammonia generates an organic product which, after hydrolysis, yields seven amino acids; **glycine was predominant**. This establishes an experimentally independent H₂CO/NH₃ route in which no cyanide is added.

Its interpretation must be conservative. The immediate reaction product is complex bound organic matter, and glycine appears after hydrolysis, so it is better represented as:

\[
\mathrm{H_2CO + NH_3 \xrightarrow{heat} N\!-\!organic\ polymer/precursors
\xrightarrow{hydrolysis} glycine}
\]

rather than as a selective one-step glycine synthesis. The route demonstrates that formaldehyde/ammonia chemistry has more than one path to a glycine skeleton, but its detailed reaction network and environmental concentration requirements are much less constrained than classical Strecker chemistry.

### The older glyoxylate–ammonia N-acyl route

Hiroshi Yanagawa, Y. Makino, K. Sato, M. Nishizawa and F. Egami (1984, *Origins of Life*) discovered that glyoxylic acid and ammonia form **N-oxalylglycine** in aqueous solution, with an optimum near **pH 3–4**. Subsequent hydrolysis with 6 N HCl released glycine in **5–39% yield**.

That route is mechanistically and prebiotically distinct from reductive amination. It establishes a C₂ glyoxylate/ammonia pathway but does **not** show facile accumulation of free glycine under the reaction conditions because strong-acid hydrolysis was used to liberate the amino acid. For a synthesis-network model, N-oxalylglycine should therefore be represented as a potentially stable reservoir/intermediate, with the hydrolysis step treated as a separate environmental problem.

### Catalyst-free aza-Cannizzaro glycine formation

Dean R. Jarois, Lars E. Schimmelpfennig and Samuel H. Gellman (2024, *Chemistry—A European Journal*) uncovered a fundamentally different glyoxylate mechanism. In concentrated ammonium solutions, glyoxylate forms imine/hemiaminal species that undergo intermolecular **hydride transfer** analogous to a Cannizzaro disproportionation. Glycine and oxamic acid are produced without an external reductant or metal catalyst.

At **0.5 M glyoxylic acid + 0.5 M ammonium phosphate, 50°C, 48 h**, glycine yield was about 11%. Reported yields were roughly 2.0% at pH 5, 2.4% at pH 6, 10.7% at pH 7, 12.7% at pH 8, 12.1% at pH 9 and 13.8% at pH 10; ammonium acetate and sulfate also supported the reaction. Isotopic experiments support transfer of glyoxylate-derived hydrogen to the glycine product.

The key negative result is equally important: little reaction occurred at glyoxylate concentrations below roughly **0.25 M** on the experimental timescale, and glyoxylic acid plus a stoichiometric equivalent of NH₄OH in unbuffered water did not give the same glycine production. Buffer anions appear to assist proton-transfer chemistry. This is therefore among the cleanest catalyst-free mechanisms known, but also among the routes most clearly dependent on **strong geochemical concentration**, suggesting evaporitic, freezing, aerosol, or pore-space settings rather than a dilute global ocean.

### FeS/Fe⁰ and alkaline-hydrothermal reductive amination

Norio Kitadai, Ryuhei Nakamura, Masahiro Yamamoto, Ken Takai, Naohiro Yoshida and Yoshi Oono (2019, *Science Advances*) showed that partially electroreduced FeS can be converted into an **FeS–Fe⁰ assemblage** at potentials ≤ approximately −0.7 V versus SHE. That material drove several protometabolic reductions. In glyoxylate experiments near **pH 9.6, 23–25°C for 48 h**, glycine yield reached **59%**; oxaloacetate gave aspartate at 64%.

The probable glycine mechanism is standard reductive amination:

\[
\mathrm{CHOCOO^- + NH_3 \rightleftharpoons HN{=}CHCOO^- + H_2O}
\]

followed by reduction of the imine:

\[
\mathrm{HN{=}CHCOO^- + 2H^+ + 2e^- \rightarrow NH_2CH_2COO^-}.
\]

Here the Fe-bearing phase is not merely a passive adsorption surface; its low-valence iron is the electron source. The geological argument is that H₂-rich alkaline hydrothermal systems can establish sufficiently reducing electrochemical potentials across conductive/mineral precipitates.

The main critique is experimental realization. The particularly active material was **electrochemically pre-reduced in the laboratory**; demonstrating that comparable FeS/Fe⁰ assemblages form and persist with adequate reactive area in a natural Hadean vent is a separate geochemical problem. The experiment also begins with millimolar glyoxylate and high ammonium, meaning the origin and concentration of those soluble substrates are not solved by the catalyst demonstration itself.

Earlier thermodynamic analysis of the FeS–H₂S/FeS₂ “iron–sulfur world” by Schoonen, Xu and Bebie (1999) found another important constraint: the reducing power available from the FeS/H₂S–pyrite couple declines as temperature rises, while the reducing demand of CO₂ fixation increases; direct electron transfer from pyrrhotite to CO₂ also has a substantial kinetic barrier. CO, CS₂ and formate are more kinetically accessible carbon substrates. This is a published reason not to equate “thermodynamically possible” with “kinetically plausible,” particularly for direct CO₂-to-amino-acid proposals.

### H₂/Ni reductive amination and protometabolic coupling

Harpreet Kaur, Sophia A. Rauscher, Emilie Werner, Youngdong Song, Jing Yi, Wahnyalo Kazöne, William F. Martin, Harun Tüysüz and Joseph Moran (2024, *Chem*) demonstrated a complementary system in which **H₂ is the reductant and Ni is the catalyst**. A representative condition was about **30 mM ketoacid, pH 8 water, 5 bar H₂, ammonium bicarbonate, 22°C for 72 h**. Ni/SiO₂–Al₂O₃, Pd/C, and Ni-bearing Aletai meteorite material all promoted reduction; controls without metal catalyst produced no reduction products.

Across the ketoacid set, Ni and meteorite catalysts gave combined amino-acid + hydroxy-acid yields of approximately **6–84%**, while Pd gave roughly 52–88%; Pd generally favored amino acid over hydroxy acid more strongly than Ni. Ammonium concentrations down into the millimolar range could still support chemistry. In coupled one-pot reaction networks containing glyoxylate and other ketoacids, glycine appeared together with aspartate and alanine.

The conceptual advantage is biochemical congruence: extant biosynthesis makes amino acids largely by reductive amination/transamination of ketoacid skeletons, not by Strecker addition of cyanide to aldehydes. The limitation is geochemical catalyst realism. Serpentinization readily supplies H₂, but the quantity, surface area, poisoning behavior and persistence of **native Ni/Ni-rich metal** accessible to aqueous organics are much less certain than the availability of silicates, sulfides or hydroxides. Pd/C is useful mechanistically but cannot reasonably be treated as an abundant primitive-Earth catalyst.

### Nitrate reduction and ferroan brucite

Lucy Chimiak, Emiko Hara, Alex L. Sessions and Alexis S. Templeton (2024, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) integrated fixed-nitrogen generation and glycine synthesis in a single mineral system. Synthetic **ferroan brucite, Fe₀.₃₃Mg₀.₆₇(OH)₂**, was incubated with glyoxylate and nitrate under alkaline, approximately room-temperature conditions. Fe(II) reduces nitrate toward ammonia; ammonia condenses with glyoxylate, and additional Fe(II) reducing equivalents convert the imine to glycine.

With initial concentrations of approximately **100 mM nitrate and 100 mM glyoxylate**, six-week nitrate experiments produced about **0.1–3.1% glycine** relative to initial organic substrate, whereas experiments supplied directly with ammonia yielded roughly **52–69%**. A striking 71–97% of the glycine generated in nitrate systems could be recovered only after digestion of the mineral fraction, showing strong adsorption or incorporation; corresponding mineral-bound fractions in NH₃ experiments were lower, about 17–27%.

This system addresses one objection to earlier reductive-amination routes—namely the assumption that ammonia was already available—but does so at the cost of introducing nitrate. Whether Hadean nitrate production and accumulation could sustain ~0.1 M local concentrations is therefore the key environmental question. The modest nitrate-to-glycine yield and six-week timescale also matter for network flux calculations, while mineral sequestration could be either a liability or a useful concentration/protection mechanism.

### Bucherer–Bergs conversion of glyoxylate and related ketoacids

Sunil Pulletikurti, Mahipal Yadav, Greg Springsteen and Ramanarayanan Krishnamurthy (2022, *Nature Chemistry*) showed that α-ketoacid skeletons can be converted to amino acids through **Bucherer–Bergs chemistry** using cyanide and ammonia sources. For glyoxylate, hydantoin-family intermediates are generated and then hydrolyzed to glycine; heated reactions around **80°C** produced glycine at reported tens-of-percent levels, alongside N-carbamoylglycine.

This route occupies an intermediate conceptual position between Strecker chemistry and metabolism-first chemistry. The carbon skeleton is a biologically recognizable ketoacid rather than an aldehyde, but cyanide is still a key reagent. The study was explicitly motivated by the discontinuity between classical prebiotic Strecker chemistry and extant transamination chemistry and demonstrated that cyanide chemistry can act directly on metabolic-type ketoacids.

Its contested features are therefore different from those of Ni/H₂ reductive amination: it avoids specialized metallic catalysts but retains the cyanide-concentration problem, and some implementations employ **diamidophosphate** as an ammonia/phosphoryl source whose prebiotic inventory is itself debated. Hydantoin hydrolysis also requires thermal processing.

### TiO₂ photocatalysis from methanol and nitrate

Peifeng Li, Wanghui Zhao, Kaixuan Wang, Tao Wang and Biaobiao Zhang (2024, *Angewandte Chemie International Edition*) reported a striking modern demonstration of a fully integrated carbon/nitrogen photochemical route:

\[
\mathrm{CH_3OH \rightarrow HOCH_2CH_2OH \rightarrow OHC{-}COOH}
\]

while simultaneously

\[
\mathrm{NO_3^- \rightarrow NH_3}
\]

and then

\[
\mathrm{glyoxylate + NH_3 \rightarrow imine \rightarrow glycine}.
\]

With anatase TiO₂ modified by Ba²⁺, the optimized glycine formation rate reached **870 μmol gcat⁻¹ h⁻¹**. Representative experiments used 5 mg photocatalyst, 4 mmol methanol and 1 mmol nitrate in 10 mL water at about **50°C, 1 bar Ar, 365-nm illumination**. Glycol/ethylene glycol, glyoxylate and ammonia were experimentally detected as intermediates.

Chemically, this is significant because it joins two difficult tasks—C–C formation from a C₁ alcohol and nitrate reduction—before reductive amination. As a **prebiotic** route, however, it should presently be classed as proposed rather than established. The optimized system employs synthesized anatase nanoparticles and deliberate Ba²⁺ modification, ~0.4 M methanol and ~0.1 M nitrate, and monochromatic 365-nm irradiation. Natural TiO₂ minerals certainly exist, but equivalence between a laboratory nanocatalyst and a Hadean mineral surface has not yet been demonstrated.

## Hydrothermal, amino-alcohol, formose, and impact chemistry

### Hydrothermal Strecker synthesis

Remy J.-C. Hennet, Nils G. Holm and Michael H. Engel (1992, *Naturwissenschaften*) tested amino-acid formation under hydrothermal conditions. A representative experiment heated an acidified aqueous solution containing roughly **0.19 M formaldehyde, 0.19 M KCN and 0.23 M NH₄Cl at 150°C and about 10 bar**, with Fe sulfide/oxide mineral assemblages and a CO₂/H₂ gas phase. Glycine was the dominant amino-acid product and occurred at millimolar scale.

This is good evidence that Strecker chemistry **survives hydrothermal pressure and temperature**; it is not evidence that hydrothermal vents themselves spontaneously produce 0.2 M formaldehyde and cyanide. That distinction is the principal published criticism of many early vent simulations: the organic feedstocks needed by Strecker synthesis were inserted experimentally instead of generated from CO₂, N₂ and H₂ in the same reactor.

Hiroshi Yanagawa and Kensei Kobayashi also subjected CH₄/N₂-bearing “modified hydrothermal vent medium” to approximately **260–325°C** and high pressure, recovering glycine, alanine and other amino acids after hydrolysis. Later comparisons concluded that the carbon yields were orders of magnitude below atmospheric-discharge yields. Detection after hydrolysis again indicates that at these temperatures the primary reservoir may be refractory/condensed amino-acid precursors rather than stable dissolved free glycine.

High temperature is intrinsically double-edged. Cox and Seward's experimental work on glycine and alanine at roughly 120–165°C and 20 bar showed dimerization and cyclization competing with persistence of free amino acid, while still hotter sub-/supercritical water promotes both oligomerization and decomposition. Consequently, the most plausible hydrothermal versions of glycine synthesis are generally cooler mixing zones, porous mineral interfaces or circulation systems rather than continuous residence in a 300–400°C vent fluid.

### Ethanolamine oxidation

Xianlong Zhang, Ge Tian, Jing Gao, Mei Han, Rui Su, Yanxiang Wang and Shouhua Feng (2017, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*) demonstrated a chemically different hydrothermal route in which **ethanolamine** is oxidized to glycine. Their proposed sequence is metal-assisted dehydrogenation to 2-aminoacetaldehyde followed by alkaline disproportionation/oxidation to glycine.

Using 0.1 M ethanolamine, pH 9.7 and 160°C for 6 h, reported glycine yields included approximately **3.1% with Ni, 5.0% with Fe, 5.0% with Cu and 6.8% with Co**; no glycine was detected in the metal-free control. Raising the pH to 12 increased the Co-assisted 160°C yield to approximately **23.5%**. Pressure increased the yield, and extending reaction time increased conversion.

The concentration dependence is a serious qualification. At 160°C under the study's pressure conditions, 0.1 M ethanolamine gave roughly 7% glycine in one condition, whereas 0.01 and 0.005 M gave only traces and 0.001 M gave none. Thus, before this can be treated as a viable origin pathway, an upstream prebiotic route must generate and locally concentrate ethanolamine to tens-of-millimolar or higher levels. The requirement for reactive native Co/Fe/Ni/Cu surfaces under strongly alkaline, hot conditions is another environmental assumption.

### Thiol-assisted formose chemistry

Arthur L. Weber (1998, *Origins of Life and Evolution of the Biosphere*) developed a sulfur-containing alternative in which formose-family carbonyls react with ammonia in the presence of thiols. The broader chemistry primarily generated alanine and homoserine, but glycine appeared from particular substrates: **glycolaldehyde + glyoxylate gave about 0.18% glycine**, while **glyoxal gave ~0.41% glycine** under the reported dilute carbonyl/thiol conditions.

The proposed intermediates are α-hydroxy/α-keto aldehydes and **amino-acid thioesters**, making the route conceptually attractive because a product of amino-acid synthesis is already activated toward condensation chemistry. Reactions were conducted under relatively mild aqueous conditions; the related alanine system, for example, operated at 40°C with 20 mM-scale formaldehyde, glycolaldehyde, NH₄Cl and a mercaptocarboxylic acid.

For glycine specifically, however, yields are low and the route does not solve the origin of glyoxal/glyoxylate or thiols. It is best represented as a **secondary network branch** that could transform a pre-existing formose/carbonyl inventory rather than a leading bottom-up route from CO₂, CO or CH₄.

### Shock chemistry in oceans and impact environments

Chizuka Suzuki, Yoshihiro Furukawa, Takamichi Kobayashi, Toshimori Sekine, Hiromoto Nakazawa and Takeshi Kakegawa (2015, *Geochemistry, Geophysics, Geosystems*) shocked aqueous ammonium formate and ammonium bicarbonate at projectile velocities of 0.51–0.92 km s⁻¹, corresponding to approximately **10–18 GPa** in the relevant experiments. Glycine, β-alanine, sarcosine and trace alanine formed from ammonium formate; smaller glycine amounts formed from ammonium bicarbonate.

The maximum glycine yield from ammonium formate was only about **0.00085 mol% of formate**, and the bicarbonate system was lower still. That low yield is nevertheless mechanistically notable because the feedstocks—formate/bicarbonate and ammonium—are simpler and potentially more geochemically accessible than aminonitriles. No specialized catalyst was intentionally required; mechanical shock supplies the activation.

Cometary-ice impact experiments provide a related but distinct scenario. Zita Martins and collaborators showed that hypervelocity shock of mixtures containing H₂O ice, CO₂, NH₃ and CH₃OH produces several amino acids after hydrolysis at pressures around tens of GPa. In those experiments methanol and sufficiently high shock pressure were required.

The difficulty is extrapolation. Natural impacts reach higher temperatures, larger spatial gradients and different cooling histories than millimeter-scale recovery cells. Strong shocks can synthesize and destroy organics simultaneously. Impact chemistry is therefore well supported as an **episodic source**, but present data do not establish that it dominated Earth's integrated glycine budget.

## Extraterrestrial and cosmochemical production

### Ultraviolet irradiation of interstellar ice

Max P. Bernstein, Jason P. Dworkin, Scott A. Sandford, George W. Cooper and Louis J. Allamandola (2002, *Nature*) irradiated interstellar-ice analogues at approximately **15 K**. Ice mixtures were dominated by water with smaller CH₃OH, NH₃ and HCN fractions; an example composition was approximately H₂O:CH₃OH:NH₃:HCN = 20:2:1:1. After UV irradiation and warm-up, the organic residue contained glycine, alanine and serine.

In an independent 2002 *Nature* study, Guillermo M. Muñoz Caro, Uwe J. Meierhenrich, W. A. Schutte, Barbier, Arcones Segovia, Rosenbauer, Thiemann, Brack and Greenberg irradiated analogous ice at about **12 K** and recovered 16 amino acids after warm-up/work-up, again including glycine. Independent replication across ice compositions is one of the strongest aspects of the extraterrestrial pathway.

Chang-Woo Lee, Joon-Ki Kim, Eui-Seong Moon, Y. C. Minh and Heon Kang (2009, *The Astrophysical Journal*) went further by irradiating H₂O/CH₃NH₂/CO₂ ice and detecting glycine-related products in situ, supporting radical mechanisms in which photofragmentation of methylamine creates C/N radical partners that combine with CO₂-derived functionality.

This route has a different relevance from an early-Earth pond experiment: it can contribute **preformed extraterrestrial glycine and precursor organics** through dust, comets and carbonaceous asteroids. It does not identify where Earth's first terrestrial glycine had to form. Laboratory ice compositions, radiation doses, warm-up rates and analytical hydrolysis must also be mapped onto actual molecular-cloud, protoplanetary-disk and asteroid histories before production rates can be extrapolated.

### Asteroidal aqueous Strecker chemistry

Carbonaceous meteorites provide independent evidence that Strecker chemistry occurred somewhere in the early Solar System. E. T. Peltzer, Jeffrey L. Bada, G. Schlesinger and Stanley L. Miller (1984, *Advances in Space Research*) found that Murchison's amino/hydroxy-acid distributions were consistent with aqueous parent-body Strecker chemistry and estimated parent-body ammonia and cyanide concentrations in roughly millimolar ranges.

N. R. Lerner, E. Peterson and S. Chang (1993, *Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta*) performed isotope-labeled Strecker experiments using deuterated carbonyl compounds and found substantial retention of carbonyl-derived deuterium in amino acids. The experiments support the feasibility of asteroid-parent-body synthesis from inherited interstellar aldehydes/ketones, NH₃ and HCN; they also found products attributable to HCN polymer chemistry.

This is important environmental evidence because it demonstrates that “prebiotic” chemistry need not all occur on Earth. A plausible glycine inventory could have multiple sources: interstellar radical chemistry, aqueous asteroidal Strecker chemistry, impact processing during delivery, and endogenous Earth synthesis.

### Formamide and meteoritic minerals

Raffaele Saladino and co-workers' 2015 *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* study irradiated **liquid formamide with high-energy protons in the presence of powdered meteorites**. A broad suite of organics formed; glycine and N-formylglycine were particularly prominent amino-acid products. For chondrite-catalyzed experiments, glycine plus its formyl derivative averaged about **1.12 μg mL⁻¹**.

The mineral catalyst is the meteorite itself, with different metal/silicate phases enabling radical and redox chemistry under particle irradiation. The route is chemically impressive but its environmental bottleneck is solvent inventory: producing and accumulating large volumes or films of nearly neat formamide on the early Earth is considerably harder to demonstrate than producing trace atmospheric formamide. Irradiation of nitriles near radioactive mineral deposits has been proposed as one source, but estimated production rates are strongly locality-dependent.

## Intermediates, catalysts, and conditions that matter most

Across otherwise competing frameworks, a surprisingly small set of intermediates recurs. This convergence is useful for synthesis-network design because environmental models can be compared at the level of **how effectively they generate the same nodes**.

| Network node | Role in glycine synthesis | Principal routes feeding it |
|---|---|---|
| **Formaldehyde, H₂CO** | C₁ aldehyde for Strecker glycine; precursor to oximes, formose carbonyls and methanol-derived chemistry | Discharge of CH₄/CO/CO₂ atmospheres; photochemistry; formaldehyde feedstock experiments. |
| **HCN/CN⁻** | Nucleophile/carbon donor in Strecker; polymer precursor; cyanosulfidic feedstock | Atmospheric discharge/photochemistry, energetic particles, impact chemistry. |
| **Glycolonitrile, HOCH₂CN** | Stable-ish formaldehyde–cyanide adduct; immediate precursor to aminoacetonitrile chemistry | Classical and cyanosulfidic Strecker networks. |
| **Aminoacetonitrile, NH₂CH₂CN** | Canonical nitrile immediately upstream of glycinamide/glycine | Strecker, hydroxylamine/formaldehyde, cyanosulfidic routes. |
| **Glycinamide** | Hydrolysis intermediate between aminoacetonitrile and glycine | Strecker and hydroxylamine systems. |
| **Glyoxylate, OHC–COO⁻** | C₂ metabolic-type carbon skeleton; condenses with NH₃ to glycine imine | FeS/Fe⁰, H₂/Ni, brucite/nitrate, aza-Cannizzaro, Bucherer–Bergs and TiO₂ routes. |
| **Glyoxylate imine/hemiaminal** | Central C–N intermediate; reduction gives glycine or disproportionation gives glycine + oxamate | Metal reductive amination and aza-Cannizzaro. |
| **N-oxalylglycine** | Bound glycine precursor/reservoir | Acidic glyoxylate–NH₃ chemistry. |
| **Hydantoin / N-carbamoylglycine** | Protected glycine skeleton preceding hydrolysis/decarboxylation | Bucherer–Bergs ketoacid/cyanide chemistry. |
| **Hydroxylamine/formaldoxime** | Alternative fixed-N entry; generates HCN/NH₃ and nitrile chemistry | H₂CO/NH₂OH route. |
| **Aminoacetaldehyde** | Oxidation intermediate | Ethanolamine route. |
| **Ethylene glycol** | C₂ precursor oxidized photocatalytically to glyoxylate | Methanol/nitrate/TiO₂ route. |
| **Formate/formic acid** | Impact precursor; proton shuttle/reductant in some theoretical networks | Shock chemistry, CO reduction, Cannizzaro chemistry. |

The corresponding catalyst map is equally heterogeneous. **No mineral catalyst is intrinsically needed** for classical Strecker formation once H₂CO/HCN/NH₃ are present, HCN condensation, formaldehyde/ammonia thermal chemistry, or concentrated glyoxylate aza-Cannizzaro chemistry.

**Fe-containing minerals/metals** have the broadest experimental coverage: FeS/Fe⁰ promotes reductive amination; Fe-bearing brucite couples nitrate reduction to glycine synthesis; Fe metal is active in ethanolamine oxidation; and hydrothermal sulfide/oxide assemblages can buffer redox conditions during Strecker chemistry. Whether these solids act catalytically or stoichiometrically must be specified—Fe(II) oxidation in brucite and FeS/Fe⁰ systems consumes reducing equivalents and therefore is not simply heterogeneous catalysis in the conventional sense.

**Ni** is particularly effective when molecular H₂ is the terminal reductant, including supported Ni and Ni-rich meteoritic material. **Co, Fe, Cu and Ni metal** facilitate ethanolamine-to-glycine oxidation, with Co strongest in the published alkaline system. **Anatase TiO₂**, especially with Ba²⁺ surface modification, enables the methanol/nitrate photochemical cascade. **Cu cyanide complexes** enable visible/UV photoredox electron chemistry in cyanosulfidic networks. **Molybdate and transition-metal ions** modify product selectivity in hydroxylamine/formaldehyde seawater simulations rather than acting as uniquely required glycine catalysts.

## Environmental plausibility and comparative assessment

There is no evidence-based basis for assigning glycine synthesis uniquely to “hydrothermal vents,” “warm little ponds,” “the atmosphere,” or “meteorites.” The experimentally supported picture is pluralistic.

**Surface atmosphere–water systems have the strongest evidence for generating the immediate Strecker feedstocks from genuinely simple gases.** Electric discharge converts CH₄/N₂/NH₃/H₂O or sufficiently H₂-rich CO/CO₂ mixtures into HCN, formaldehyde and amino-acid products without the experimenter first supplying an activated C₂ substrate. Their weakness is atmospheric redox. Neutral CO₂/N₂ chemistry is inefficient, while sufficiently reducing global atmospheres remain geologically disputed. Local volcanic plumes and transient post-impact H₂/CH₄ atmospheres substantially weaken—but do not eliminate—that criticism.

**Concentrating surface environments are especially attractive for subsequent aqueous chemistry.** Classical Strecker, HCN polymerization, aza-Cannizzaro and much cyanosulfidic chemistry all improve when reactants reach millimolar-to-molar concentrations. Evaporation, freezing and adsorption are therefore not incidental details but likely determine whether an atmospheric feedstock flux becomes chemically consequential. The Levy–Miller freezing experiments establish eutectic ice as one experimentally demonstrated way to concentrate cyanide chemistry; the Jarois aza-Cannizzaro data independently show why concentration matters for glyoxylate.

**Alkaline hydrothermal systems now have stronger glycine-specific experimental support than they did in the 1990s.** The important progress is not high-temperature Strecker synthesis but mild-temperature glyoxylate reductive amination using FeS/Fe⁰, ferroan brucite or H₂/Ni. These reactions operate near room temperature to modest temperature and make direct use of reducing power characteristic of serpentinizing systems. Their common unresolved variable is upstream substrate supply: neither glyoxylate nor tens-of-millimolar fixed nitrogen should simply be assumed.

**High-temperature vent interiors are less favorable for maintaining a free-glycine inventory.** They can synthesize precursors and support rapid reaction chemistry, but amino acids also dimerize, cyclize and decompose as temperature increases. Thus vent models are most chemically plausible when they invoke spatial gradients—hot synthesis regions coupled to cooler mixing zones, porous mineral walls or circulation—rather than treating a homogeneous 300°C reactor as the origin environment.

**Impact chemistry is plausible but probably episodic.** It can reduce N₂, generate reduced gases and HCN in impact plumes, or produce glycine directly from ammonium formate/bicarbonate and shocked ices. Direct recovered glycine yields in simple aqueous shock experiments are low, and the balance between synthesis and thermal destruction is sensitive to the pressure–temperature–time trajectory.

**Extraterrestrial synthesis is experimentally very strong as a source term**, because independent UV-ice experiments and meteorite-parent-body studies converge on glycine formation. It does not compete logically with terrestrial routes: delivered glycine, HCN, aldehydes and related precursors could supplement atmospheric or hydrothermal inventories. The central uncertainties are the delivery flux of intact material, atmospheric-entry survival, impact destruction, and dilution after deposition.

A useful overall assessment is therefore:

| Question | Route with strongest present experimental answer | Main unresolved issue |
|---|---|---|
| Can glycine be made from very simple atmospheric feedstocks? | Spark discharge and energetic-particle chemistry | Planetary redox state and natural energy flux. |
| Is there a robust, chemically understood solution route? | Classical Strecker | HCN/H₂CO concentrations and nitrile hydrolysis. |
| Can glycine form without cyanide? | Glyoxylate aza-Cannizzaro; Fe/Ni reductive amination; nitrate–TiO₂; ethanolamine oxidation | Production/concentration of glyoxylate or other immediate precursor. |
| Can fixed nitrogen be generated in the same system? | Ferroan brucite NO₃⁻ reduction; TiO₂ NO₃⁻ photoreduction; high-energy N₂ chemistry | Plausible nitrate/N₂ inventory and conversion rate. |
| Can H₂ drive metabolism-like amino-acid synthesis? | Ni/H₂ and reduced Fe mineral systems | Natural catalyst state and ketoacid availability. |
| Can concentration occur without evaporation? | Freeze-concentrated cyanide chemistry | Requires suitable cold regions and long residence times. |
| Is exogenous glycine formation demonstrable? | UV-irradiated interstellar ice and parent-body Strecker chemistry | Quantitative transfer to usable terrestrial inventories. |

## Open questions, critiques, and priorities for synthesis-network design

The first unresolved issue is **feedstock closure**. Many high-yield glycine experiments begin one or two network levels too late. A 59% conversion of supplied glyoxylate to glycine is chemically impressive, but an origin scenario must also show where the glyoxylate came from. A hydrothermal Strecker experiment with ~0.2 M HCN and formaldehyde demonstrates reaction compatibility at 150°C, not endogenous production of those concentrations. A 23% ethanolamine conversion similarly transfers the upstream problem to ethanolamine. For network evaluation, the relevant metric should therefore be flux from geochemically defensible starting materials—CO₂, CO, CH₄, N₂, NH₃, H₂O, H₂, H₂S, nitrate/nitrite and mineral reductants—not merely isolated-step yield.

The second is **concentration**. Classical cyanide chemistry can become effective near 10 mM or above; aza-Cannizzaro glycine production becomes weak below roughly 0.25 M glyoxylate on short timescales; the nitrate/brucite work used ~0.1 M reactants; TiO₂ photocatalysis used ~0.4 M methanol and ~0.1 M nitrate; the ethanolamine route strongly deteriorated below 0.1 M. These are not fatal concentrations in an evaporating or freezing microenvironment, but they are inconsistent with an unconcentrated global ocean. A credible model therefore needs a hydrological concentrating step.

The third is **analytical definition of “glycine synthesis.”** Some of the most frequently cited atmospheric, particle-irradiation, hydrothermal and impact experiments quantify amino acids only after 6 M HCl hydrolysis or comparable work-up. Those experiments demonstrate formation of a **glycine-generating precursor inventory**, which may actually be advantageous because bound material can be more persistent than free amino acids, but it should not be entered into a chemical network as direct free-glycine production.

The fourth is **reaction compatibility**. Cyanide is beneficial for Strecker/Bucherer–Bergs chemistry but binds transition metals and can interfere with redox chemistry. Sulfide is a reductant and photochemical electron donor but precipitates many transition metals. Strongly alkaline conditions help certain reductive-amination or Cannizzaro processes but alter HCN/CN⁻ partitioning and metal solubility. High temperature accelerates nitrile hydrolysis and carbonyl reactions but also destroys amino acids. Thus, assembling individually successful reactions into one “one-pot” pathway can be chemically misleading; spatially or temporally separated environments may be required. The demonstrated interference of cyanide with Fe(II)-based nitrogen reduction and the temperature sensitivity of FeS reduction chemistry exemplify this general problem.

The fifth is **catalyst geological realism**. Fresh FeS, ferroan brucite and common oxides are easier to motivate geochemically than Pd/C or engineered Ba²⁺-TiO₂ nanoparticles, but even a plausible mineral identity does not guarantee the oxidation state, crystal defects, surface area or lifetime used experimentally. The 2019 FeS/Fe⁰ system required electrochemical pretreatment to achieve especially active material; the 2024 TiO₂ system used a deliberately optimized photocatalyst; the Ni/H₂ work shows excellent chemistry but still needs a natural route to sufficiently reactive Ni surfaces. Catalyst availability must therefore be represented as a mineral-formation subnetwork, not a binary “mineral present/mineral absent” variable.

The sixth is the unresolved **cyanide-versus-metabolic-skeleton debate**. Classical Strecker chemistry has the deepest experimental pedigree and naturally explains why glycine is so readily formed, but extant amino-acid biosynthesis uses ketoacid reductive amination/transamination instead. Pulletikurti and colleagues explicitly targeted this discontinuity with ketoacid/Bucherer–Bergs chemistry, while Kaur and colleagues showed that H₂/Ni can perform genuinely cyanide-free, metabolism-like reductive amination. No evolutionary argument yet shows that one must exclude the other: early cyanide chemistry could have supplied amino acids before later network takeover by ketoacid chemistry, or ketoacid chemistry may have operated contemporaneously in different environments.

The seventh is **atmospheric uncertainty**. The traditional criticism of Miller chemistry—that an enduring CH₄/NH₃ atmosphere conflicts with an oxidized mantle—is supported by zircon/mantle-redox interpretations, but impact models predict transient H₂/CH₄-rich intervals, and a 2026 thermodynamic analysis has revived the stronger claim that late-Hadean conditions could have supported an H₂/CH₄/NH₃-rich atmosphere. These models invoke different assumptions about mantle redox evolution, atmosphere–ocean–crust exchange and impact history. Consequently, network calculations should test several atmospheric boundary conditions instead of hard-coding either a permanently reducing or permanently CO₂/N₂ atmosphere.

Finally, there remains a **kinetics-versus-thermodynamics problem**. Computational reaction-network searches can formally connect CO, H₂O and NH₃ to glycine through formic acid, formamide, aminomethanol and formaldimine, but calculated free-energy barriers in one recent study are commonly **~40–60+ kcal mol⁻¹**, including a ~51 kcal mol⁻¹ effective bottleneck for one glycine pathway and ~63 kcal mol⁻¹ for another CO/H₂/H₂O/NH₃ path. Such networks are useful for discovering possible connectivity but, absent a catalyst or high-energy event that radically lowers those barriers, they should not be treated as experimentally viable ambient-temperature prebiotic syntheses.

Taken together, the literature supports a network-level conclusion rather than a single-route conclusion: **glycine itself is not a particularly difficult molecule to make abiotically once either (a) formaldehyde + cyanide + reduced nitrogen or (b) glyoxylate + a nitrogen source + reducing equivalents are available. The difficult prebiotic questions lie upstream—fixed nitrogen, C₁/C₂ carbonyl production, concentration, mineral redox state—and downstream—hydrolysis, preservation, transport, and integration with peptide/nucleotide chemistry.** The highest-priority discriminating experiments are therefore not additional demonstrations that concentrated laboratory feedstocks can yield glycine, but closed-system experiments starting from geochemically constrained C/N sources, using natural mineral assemblages, realistic concentration cycles and analytically resolving free glycine from its hydrolyzable precursor pool.
