# Prebiotic Synthesis of Valine: Routes, Precursors, Catalysts, Environmental Context, and Open Problems

## Executive assessment

Valine is a useful stress test for prebiotic synthesis models because it is not merely an α-amino acid: it requires construction of a **branched C5 carbon skeleton**, HOOC–CH(NH₂)–CH(CH₃)₂. The literature contains several direct demonstrations of abiotic valine formation, but the evidence is uneven. The strongest direct demonstrations fall into five chemically distinct families: **Strecker-type atmospheric/electric-discharge chemistry; ammonium-cyanide/ketone chemistry; high-temperature formaldehyde–ammonia chemistry; shock chemistry; and reductive amination of α-ketoisovalerate with H₂ over metal catalysts**. More recently, **γ-radiolysis of formaldehyde/ammonia solutions in meteorite-parent-body analogues** has also generated valine as a minor product.

The central conclusion is that **no published route yet closes the entire synthesis from the simplest geochemical feedstocks to valine under one experimentally demonstrated, geochemically well-constrained environment**. The classical Strecker family can, in principle, build the C5 skeleton by adding the carbon of HCN to the C4 aldehyde isobutyraldehyde, and electric-discharge experiments genuinely produce valine, but the strongly reducing atmospheric mixtures that produce aldehydes and HCN most efficiently are difficult to reconcile with evidence that Hadean mantle outgassing was comparatively oxidized. Conversely, the 2024 H₂/Ni chemistry of Kaur and co-workers converts the biologically congruent precursor **α-ketoisovalerate directly to valine under remarkably mild conditions**, but α-ketoisovalerate was supplied at 30 mM: its abiotic synthesis from CO₂, CO, pyruvate, or other simpler feedstocks under the same conditions remains the major missing step.

A useful evidence hierarchy is therefore:

| Route family | Direct valine demonstrated? | Simplest feedstocks experimentally connected to the route | Main bottleneck |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Electric discharge → aldehydes + HCN → Strecker chemistry | **Yes** | CH₄, NH₃ or N₂, H₂O, H₂ | Reduced atmosphere; production of enough C4 isobutyraldehyde; low selectivity |
| NH₄CN + ketone network | **Yes** | HCN/NH₃ plus acetone | Mechanism of C5 branching is poorly resolved; NH₄CN alone does **not** reliably give valine |
| Heated HCHO + NH₃ | **Yes, historically** | Formaldehyde, ammonia, water | Harsh heating; poorly defined bound intermediates; weak valine-specific quantitation/reproducibility |
| Shock-heated reduced gases | **Yes** | CH₄, C₂H₆, NH₃ | Highly reducing gas composition not representative of favored global Hadean atmosphere |
| CO₂/N₂ impact chemistry | **Not yet** | CO₂/HCO₃⁻, N₂, H₂O, Fe, Ni, silicates | Experiments stop at simpler amino acids; no demonstrated valine |
| α-Ketoisovalerate + NH₃ + H₂, Ni/Pd/meteorite | **Yes** | H₂, NH₃ plus preformed C5 α-ketoacid | Origin and concentration of α-ketoisovalerate |
| α-Ketoacid + CN⁻/NH₃ Bucherer–Bergs chemistry | **Mechanistically plausible; valine not directly shown in the 2022 substrate set** | α-Ketoacid, CN⁻, ammonia source, bicarbonate | Requires preformed α-ketoisovalerate and concentrated cyanide/ammonia; long processing |
| CO/H₂/NH₃ Fischer–Tropsch-type mineral chemistry | **No in the principal 2012 study** | CO, H₂, NH₃ | Produces other amino acids, notably AIB; valine was not reported |
| γ-Radiolysis of HCHO/NH₃ parent-body solutions | **Yes, minor product** | HCHO, NH₃, H₂O, sometimes CH₃OH/glycolaldehyde | Product complexity, dose/concentration assumptions, bound precursors, extraterrestrial rather than terrestrial setting |

The route classifications above follow the direct experiments of Miller (1957, *Biochimica et Biophysica Acta*), Friedmann & Miller (1969, *Nature*), Bar-Nun, Bar-Nun, Bauer & Sagan (1970, *Science*), Fox & Windsor (1970, *Science*), Ring, Wolman, Friedmann & Miller (1972, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*), Pizzarello (2012, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*), Pulletikurti, Yadav, Springsteen & Krishnamurthy (2022, *Nature Chemistry*), Kaur et al. (2024, *Chem*), and Ishikawa, Kebukawa, Kobayashi & Yoda (2024, *Life*).

## Known synthesis routes and mechanisms

### Electric-discharge chemistry and the Strecker route

The most chemically explicit classical route is the one established mechanistically by **Stanley Miller (1957, *Biochimica et Biophysica Acta*)**. Electric discharge through CH₄–NH₃–H₂O–H₂ mixtures produced **HCN and aldehydes**, which then entered Strecker chemistry. Miller showed that aminonitrile hydrolysis proceeded quickly enough to account for the amino acids accumulated after roughly a week of sparking. Replacing NH₃ with N₂ still generated the same general product types, but at lower yield. Addition of ferrous ammonium sulfate did not substantially change product yields, arguing that no Fe/sulfate mineral catalysis was necessary in this experiment.

For valine specifically, the chemically straightforward Strecker sequence is:

\[
(CH_3)_2CHCHO + NH_3 \rightleftharpoons (CH_3)_2CHCH{=}NH + H_2O
\]

\[
(CH_3)_2CHCH{=}NH + HCN \rightarrow (CH_3)_2CHCH(NH_2)CN
\]

followed by nitrile hydrolysis:

\[
(CH_3)_2CHCH(NH_2)CN + 2H_2O \rightarrow
(CH_3)_2CHCH(NH_2)CO_2H + NH_3
\]

Thus the crucial carbon precursor is **2-methylpropanal, or isobutyraldehyde**. Its four-carbon branched skeleton supplies valine C2–C5, while cyanide supplies the carboxyl carbon. This is an important point for network design: making HCN is not enough; an efficient prebiotic valine network also has to generate a significant pool of **branched C4 aldehyde**.

Direct confirmation that such discharge chemistry reaches valine came from **Ring, Wolman, Friedmann & Miller (1972, *PNAS*)**. They discharged a mixture of methane, nitrogen and water containing traces of ammonia and identified, using ion-exchange chromatography together with GC–MS, glycine, alanine, α-aminobutyric acid, α-aminoisobutyric acid, **valine**, norvaline, isovaline, leucine, isoleucine, alloisoleucine, norleucine and several polar amino acids. The broad isomer distribution is significant: the experiment demonstrates chemical accessibility but **poor structural selectivity** for the biological amino-acid set.

The main environmental objection is now substantial. **Trail, Watson & Tailby (2011, *Nature*)** used Ce redox systematics in Hadean zircons to infer mantle-derived magmas near the fayalite–magnetite–quartz redox buffer by about 4.35 Ga. Such outgassing is predicted to favor H₂O, CO₂, SO₂ and N₂ rather than the CH₄-, NH₃- and H₂-rich mixture most favorable to Miller-type amino-acid synthesis. This does not prove that strongly reducing local or transient environments never existed, but it makes a globally persistent Miller-style atmosphere difficult to defend geochemically.

Attempts to extend spark chemistry to more neutral CO₂/N₂ atmospheres illustrate the problem. **Cleaves, Chalmers, Lazcano, Miller & Bada (2008, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*)** found that amino-acid recovery from CO₂/N₂ discharge mixtures could be raised considerably by controlling pH and suppressing oxidative destruction during work-up. In experiments with approximately 0.13 bar CO₂ and 0.13 bar N₂, CaCO₃ buffering raised total amino-acid recovery relative to unbuffered experiments. However, the published product set emphasized glycine, alanine, β-alanine, AIB, aspartate, glutamate and serine rather than valine. Moreover, **Kuwahara, Eto, Kawamoto, Kurihara, Kaneko, Obayashi & Kobayashi (2012, *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres*)** used isotopic tests to show that ascorbate, which had been used as an oxidation inhibitor in some such experiments, could itself contribute carbon to apparent amino-acid products. Thus neutral-atmosphere spark chemistry remains relevant for simpler precursors, but **it is not currently a strong direct experimental route to valine**.

### Ammonium cyanide plus ketones

**Nadav Friedmann & Stanley Miller (1969, *Nature*)** directly revisited reports that polymerized ammonium cyanide produced the branched protein amino acids. Their result is unusually important because it contains both a positive result and a published refutation: they **could not detect valine, isoleucine or leucine from NH₄CN polymerization alone** when more stringent separation was used. Earlier identifications had relied primarily on amino-acid-analyzer elution positions, where non-protein amino acids can co-elute with valine or isoleucine.

When **acetone or methyl ethyl ketone was added to NH₄CN**, however, Friedmann and Miller obtained significant quantities of valine and, depending on the ketone, alloisoleucine and isoleucine. This therefore constitutes a direct prebiotic valine synthesis route from a cyanide/ammonia pool plus simple ketones.

Mechanistically, this route is more problematic than the ordinary aldehyde Strecker pathway. A simple Strecker reaction of acetone, NH₃ and HCN gives **α-aminoisobutyric acid**, a C4 amino acid, not C5 valine. Consequently, the reported appearance of valine from acetone/NH₄CN must involve additional carbon–carbon chemistry in the cyanide/ketone reaction network. Friedmann and Miller established the product much more securely than earlier NH₄CN work, but they did not establish a unique elementary mechanism for the C5 branch. This makes the route experimentally real but **mechanistically underconstrained for network reconstruction**.

The study also supplies one of the clearest historical warnings for the valine literature: **NH₄CN polymerization by itself should not be counted as an established valine synthesis**. The most careful direct re-investigation found that earlier assignments were probably chromatographic false positives.

### Heated formaldehyde–ammonia chemistry

A chemically very different route was reported by **Sidney Fox & Charles Windsor (1970, *Science*)**. Heating formaldehyde with ammonia generated a complex product which, after hydrolysis, yielded aspartate, glutamate, serine, proline, **valine**, glycine and alanine; glycine was the dominant amino acid. Later experimental literature describing the original procedure reports heating at approximately **185 °C**, illustrating that this was a substantially hotter process than ordinary aqueous surface chemistry.

This is not best understood as an ordinary Strecker reaction because HCN and a discrete valine aldehyde were not supplied. The experimentally observed amino acids appeared importantly after hydrolysis, pointing to **nitrogen-containing oligomers or other hydrolysable bound precursors** rather than to a clean solution of free amino acids. The detailed route from C1 formaldehyde to a branched C5 valine skeleton was never resolved at an elementary-mechanism level. Modern work generally treats related HCHO/NH₃ chemistry as a nitrogen-containing, formose-like condensation network rather than a single selective synthesis.

Modern parent-body simulations expose a major reproducibility problem for valine in this chemistry. **Yoko Kebukawa and co-workers (2017, *Science Advances*)** heated formaldehyde, glycolaldehyde, ammonia and water at **90–250 °C for 72 h**, producing complex insoluble organic matter and numerous amino-acid precursors. Their analytical standard included valine, but the valine recovered from experiments was detected only as the **L enantiomer**, unlike genuinely abiotic products that were racemic; the authors therefore regarded the apparent valine as probable biological contamination rather than evidence of synthesis.

That result does not disprove Fox & Windsor's 1970 observation, because the recipes differ substantially, but it means that **thermal HCHO/NH₃ chemistry should not be treated as a robust, reproducibly quantified modern valine pathway**. It does robustly generate simpler amino acids and hydrolysable organic material; the C5 branched products remain much less secure.

### Radiation-driven formaldehyde–ammonia chemistry in small bodies

A more recent variant uses ionizing radiation rather than heat alone. **Yoko Kebukawa (2022, *ACS Central Science*)** showed that γ irradiation of aqueous formaldehyde and ammonia mixtures produced multiple amino acids after hydrolysis. Amino-acid yield scaled approximately linearly with accumulated γ dose and was comparatively insensitive to dose rate, which matters because the proposed natural energy source is γ radiation associated with radionuclides such as ^26Al in aqueously altered asteroid parent bodies. The estimated natural radiation production was argued to be chemically significant on parent-body timescales.

The expanded experiments of **Akari Ishikawa, Yoko Kebukawa, Kensei Kobayashi & Isao Yoda (2024, *Life*)** varied the formaldehyde/ammonia/methanol composition and reported **valine among the lower-abundance amino-acid products**, behind alanine, glycine, β-alanine, α-aminobutyric acid and several other species. Representative experiments used large integrated γ doses on the order of tens to ~90 kGy.

Mechanistically, this chemistry is more plausibly a **radical-driven reaction network** than a single Strecker event. Radiolysis generates reactive carbon-, oxygen- and nitrogen-centered species; formaldehyde can also form glycolaldehyde and higher carbonyl compounds, which then feed carbon-chain growth. A discrete, experimentally traced sequence from HCHO to the branched C5 valine skeleton has not yet been established, so the route is direct at the product level but not mechanistically closed.

Its environmental relevance is strongest for **aqueously altered carbonaceous-asteroid parent bodies**, not for a particular early-Earth surface environment. It therefore represents a plausible contribution through extraterrestrial synthesis and delivery rather than a demonstrated endogenous terrestrial source.

### Shock synthesis

**A. Bar-Nun, N. Bar-Nun, S. H. Bauer & Carl Sagan (1970, *Science*)** subjected a reduced gas mixture containing approximately **3.3% CH₄, 11% C₂H₆ and 5.6% NH₃, with the balance Ar**, to single-pulse shock heating followed by rapid quenching. They directly observed **glycine, alanine, valine and leucine**. The reported overall efficiency for α-amino-acid production was about **5 × 10¹⁰ molecules per erg of shock energy**. The authors argued that rapid quenching could kinetically trap amino acids that would not be favored at the peak equilibrium temperature.

The mechanism was not resolved into a valine-specific sequence. At shock temperatures, extensive radical fragmentation and recombination occur, followed by chemistry during rapid cooling. In principle, aldehydes, HCN/nitriles and related intermediates can emerge during such processing, but the study established valine production rather than a unique pathway to it. No mineral catalyst was required.

The principal critique is environmental. The experiment requires substantial reduced carbon and ammonia. The Hadean zircon constraints of Trail, Watson & Tailby favor substantially more oxidized volcanic outgassing, making this particular bulk-gas composition difficult to justify as the persistent global atmosphere.

Modern impact experiments have substantially improved the geochemical starting composition, but they have **not yet closed the gap to valine**. **Takeuchi, Furukawa, Kobayashi, Sekine, Terada & Kakegawa (2020, *Scientific Reports*)** shocked mixtures of Fe, Ni, forsterite, water, bicarbonate/CO₂ and N₂, designed to represent an Fe-bearing impactor striking an ocean beneath a CO₂/N₂ atmosphere. Their experiments used an impact velocity of about **0.9 km s⁻¹**, pressure of about **7 GPa for 0.7 μs**, a shock-compression temperature near **300 °C**, and estimated post-shock temperatures around **1500 °C**. Isotopic labeling confirmed abiotic amino-acid formation. Glycine and alanine were prominent; additional low-molecular-weight amino acids appeared when ammonia was supplied, but **valine was not demonstrated**.

This creates an instructive contrast: **the old reduced-gas shock experiment reaches valine; the newer geochemically more conservative CO₂/N₂/Fe impact experiment does not**. Impact chemistry is therefore a credible source of valine precursors and simpler amino acids, but the direct valine evidence still depends on the less geochemically favored reduced gas mixture.

### Reductive amination of α-ketoisovalerate with hydrogen

The clearest modern direct conversion of a specifically valine-like metabolic precursor is the work of **Harpreet Kaur, Sophia Rauscher, Emilie Werner, Youngdong Song, Jing Yi, Wahnyalo Kazöne, William Martin, Harun Tüysüz & Joseph Moran (2024, *Chem*)**. They examined six biological α-ketoacids, including **α-ketoisovalerate**, under H₂ with ammonia and heterogeneous metal catalysts. α-Ketoisovalerate gave valine together with its competing carbonyl-reduction product, 2-hydroxyisovalerate.

The net valine reaction is simple:

\[
(CH_3)_2CHCOCO_2H + NH_3 + H_2
\rightarrow
(CH_3)_2CHCH(NH_2)CO_2H + H_2O
\]

Mechanistically, ammonia reacts with the α-keto carbonyl to form an imine/iminium-type intermediate, followed by surface-mediated hydrogenation. The competing reaction is direct hydrogenation of the carbonyl to the corresponding α-hydroxy acid.

The standard valine experiment used **30 mM α-ketoisovalerate**, five equivalents NH₄HCO₃, **5 bar H₂**, water at **pH 8**, **22 °C**, and **72 h**. Three catalysts worked: Pd/C containing 1 mol% Pd relative to substrate, Ni/SiO₂–Al₂O₃ containing 10 mol% Ni, and 3 mg of ground **Aletai iron meteorite** containing substantial Ni. No reduction products were seen in catalyst-free controls.

For α-ketoisovalerate specifically, Figure 2 of Kaur et al. gives unusually useful quantitative data. The reported combined amino-acid plus hydroxy-acid yields and amino:hydroxy ratios were:

| Catalyst | Combined valine + 2-hydroxyisovalerate yield | Valine:hydroxy-acid ratio | Approximate valine yield inferred from the figure |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Ni/SiO₂–Al₂O₃ | 20% | 34:66 | **~6.8%** |
| Pd/C | 77% | 83:17 | **~64%** |
| Aletai meteorite | 74% | 18:82 | **~13%** |

The last column is an arithmetic inference from the published combined yield and product ratio, not a separately reported value. The striking point is the trade-off in plausibility versus selectivity: **Pd is much more amino-acid selective, whereas Ni and meteoritic metal are geochemically easier to motivate but favor substantial hydroxy-acid formation**.

The same study found that the chemistry can function at ammonium concentrations from **6 to 150 mM**. It also linked reductive amination to H₂-driven reductive-aldol chemistry involving glyoxylate, oxaloacetate and other α-ketoacids. Some aldol steps occur even without catalyst, whereas the critical hydrogenation steps require the metal catalyst. The paper notes that hydrothermal fluids can contain approximately **1–15 mmol kg⁻¹ dissolved H₂**, making the reductant geochemically attractive in serpentinization-associated systems.

This is therefore one of the strongest **final-step** valine syntheses in the literature, but it is not yet a de novo valine synthesis from CO₂/H₂. The experiment **supplied α-ketoisovalerate**. The coupled reaction network generated α-ketoglutarate and other α-ketoacids from simpler precursors but did not establish a pathway from CO₂, pyruvate or glyoxylate all the way to α-ketoisovalerate. In modern biochemistry, construction of the valine skeleton from pyruvate involves a highly specific branched-chain pathway; an experimentally validated nonenzymatic equivalent remains missing.

### Bucherer–Bergs chemistry from α-ketoacids

**Sunil Pulletikurti, Mahipal Yadav, Greg Springsteen & Ramanarayanan Krishnamurthy (2022, *Nature Chemistry*)** demonstrated a general alternative in which α-ketoacids react with cyanide and ammonia sources through **Bucherer–Bergs-type chemistry** to produce the corresponding α-amino acids. Their mechanistic sequence goes through cyanide addition/amino-nitrile chemistry, cyclization to a **hydantoin**, hydrolysis to an N-carbamoyl amino acid, and finally amino-acid formation.

Under their optimized general procedure, an α-ketoacid was used at roughly **0.35 M**, with 2 equivalents of NH₄Cl or diamidophosphate, 1.2 equivalents NaCN, and **0.5 M bicarbonate at pH 8.5**. The mixture was held at room temperature for **21 days** and then heated at **80 °C for 14 days** to complete amino-acid formation. Their supporting information shows explicitly that hydantoin hydrolysis can proceed through an N-carbamoyl amino acid and that ammonia-source chemistry affects the hydrolysis.

For valine-network design, the obvious substrate is **α-ketoisovalerate**, which would be expected to give an isopropyl-substituted hydantoin and ultimately valine. However, the experimental substrate set documented in the 2022 paper concentrates on pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, oxaloacetate and glyoxylate; **the paper does not provide a direct α-ketoisovalerate-to-valine experiment**. The pathway is therefore a well-supported reaction class that is mechanistically transferable to valine, but it should not be counted as a direct valine demonstration from that study.

Its principal prebiotic criticisms are the comparatively high substrate/buffer concentrations, long reaction periods, requirement for cyanide plus a concentrated ammonia source, and—again—the prior availability of α-ketoisovalerate. Kaur et al. explicitly contrasted their H₂ chemistry with Bucherer–Bergs chemistry, noting both its use of cyanide and its two-stage temperature protocol.

## Key intermediates, precursors, catalysts, and kinetic constraints

For network-design purposes, the literature reduces to two particularly important **valine-specific carbon skeletons**.

The first is **isobutyraldehyde (2-methylpropanal)**. A Strecker route needs this C4 branched aldehyde plus HCN and ammonia. The aldehyde carbon becomes valine's α carbon and the cyanide carbon becomes the carboxyl carbon. The immediate intermediate is **2-amino-3-methylbutanenitrile**, whose hydrolysis yields valine. HCN and the imine/aminonitrile intermediates therefore matter kinetically as much as the aldehyde itself. Miller's 1957 experiments showed that aminonitrile hydrolysis rates were sufficient to account for amino-acid accumulation on approximately week-long discharge timescales.

The second is **α-ketoisovalerate, 3-methyl-2-oxobutanoate**. It already contains all five carbons in the correct topology; only the α-keto group must be converted into an α-amino group. It is the direct precursor in the Kaur H₂/Ni/Pd route and the obvious substrate for a valine-specific Bucherer–Bergs pathway. This makes α-ketoisovalerate particularly attractive for metabolism-congruent models, but also shifts nearly the entire carbon-skeleton problem upstream.

The major catalytic evidence can be summarized as follows.

| Reaction | Catalyst/surface experimentally tested | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Spark generation of HCN/aldehydes and subsequent Strecker chemistry | No mineral catalyst required; Fe ammonium sulfate gave no important enhancement in Miller's experiment | Electrical energy; reducing feedstock mixture |
| NH₄CN + ketone valine network | No mineral catalyst reported as required | Cyanide/ammonia chemistry plus ketone |
| Heated HCHO/NH₃ route | No mineral catalyst required in original Fox–Windsor experiment | Strong heating, followed by hydrolysis |
| Reduced-gas shock route | No heterogeneous catalyst required | Shock heating and rapid quench |
| α-Ketoisovalerate + H₂/NH₃ | Ni/SiO₂–Al₂O₃, Pd/C, Aletai meteorite | **Catalyst essential** under tested conditions |
| General α-ketoacid reductive amination | Fresh FeS or Fe(OH)₂ in Huber–Wächtershäuser experiments | Strongly surface/state dependent |
| Photochemical α-ketoacid amination | ZnS/sphalerite in Wang et al. | UV illumination and electron donor/hole-scavenging chemistry |
| FTT-type CO/H₂/NH₃ chemistry | Fe/Ni meteorite powder, magnetite, Murchison material; bentonite much less active | 370 °C catalytic surface chemistry |

The first four rows follow Miller (1957), Friedmann & Miller (1969), Fox & Windsor (1970), and Bar-Nun et al. (1970); the H₂ metal-catalysis result is from Kaur et al. (2024).

Two older mineral studies are important mechanistically even though they did **not directly make valine**. **Claudia Huber & Günter Wächtershäuser (2003, *Tetrahedron Letters*)** showed that freshly precipitated FeS and Fe(OH)₂ can promote reductive amination of several α-ketoacids with ammonium salts. Their experiments produced alanine, glutamate, phenylalanine and tyrosine, with maximum yields for several systems of tens of mol%; the reaction was highly sensitive to the physical state of FeS, pH and ionic strength. Optimal pH with ammonia was around **8.8–9.3**, close to the NH₄⁺/NH₃ pKₐ; elevated ionic strength suppressed the reaction. At 100 °C the tested tyrosine-forming system became fast on approximately hourly timescales, whereas reaction could still occur slowly at 4 °C. No α-ketoisovalerate/valine experiment was reported, so this work is evidence for a *reaction class*, not for valine itself.

Similarly, **Wei Wang, Qiliang Li, Bin Yang, Xiaoyang Liu, Yanqiang Yang & Wenhui Su (2012, *Chemical Communications*)** demonstrated reversible photochemical amination of four reverse-TCA-related α-ketoacids on irradiated ZnS. Their experiments used an oxygen-free ZnS suspension, pH around 9 and a Hg–Xe source; glutamate yield was only about 0.5% after four hours without a sulfite hole scavenger and increased when the surface and photoredox conditions were optimized. α-Ketoisovalerate was not one of the substrates, so ZnS is a plausible catalyst family to test for valine chemistry rather than an existing direct demonstration.

The **Fischer–Tropsch-type framework** deserves the same caution. **Sandra Pizzarello (2012, *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*)** reacted CO:H₂:NH₃ in a 1:1:1 gas mixture over meteorite/mineral powders at about **370 °C for 24 h**. Fe/Ni meteorite powder produced glycine, alanine, α-aminobutyric acid and smaller amounts of higher homologues; magnetite strongly favored the branched non-protein amino acid **α-aminoisobutyric acid**, reaching about 540 nmol g⁻¹ catalyst, and also yielded ~1 nmol g⁻¹ isovaline. **Valine itself was not reported in the product table.** Bentonite was a poor catalyst. FTT chemistry therefore provides experimentally important CO/H₂/NH₃ carbon–nitrogen chemistry but should not currently be listed as a demonstrated valine route.

The valine literature contains surprisingly little **valine-specific equilibrium thermodynamics** under proposed prebiotic conditions. Most constraints are kinetic/empirical: week-scale aminonitrile hydrolysis in Miller chemistry; ultrafast high-temperature formation followed by quenching in shock chemistry; 72-h hydrogenation at 22 °C for Kaur's route; weeks-long formation/hydrolysis in the Bucherer–Bergs system; and approximately linear accumulated-dose dependence in γ-radiolysis. Thus, for synthesis-network modeling, experimentally determined rate constants, activation parameters and competing decomposition rates for **isobutyraldehyde, valine aminonitrile and α-ketoisovalerate under realistic saline/mineral conditions are major missing datasets**.

## Environmental context and geochemical plausibility

### Atmosphere and surface waters

Classical Miller–Strecker chemistry has the strongest historical evidence for direct synthesis from very small molecules, but its environmental support is weakest at the level of a **globally reducing atmosphere**. Trail, Watson & Tailby's Hadean zircon measurements imply mantle oxidation states similar to later terrestrial mantle by ~4.35 Ga, which favors outgassing of H₂O, CO₂, SO₂ and N₂ rather than large steady-state CH₄ and NH₃ abundances. The key environmental question is therefore not whether electrical discharge can make valine—it can—but whether early Earth could repeatedly create **local mixtures rich enough in reduced C and N to produce the necessary HCN and branched C4 aldehydes**.

Neutral CO₂/N₂ spark experiments demonstrate that some amino-acid chemistry remains possible under more oxidized atmospheric assumptions, particularly when solution pH and oxidative work-up chemistry are controlled, but they have not supplied comparable direct evidence for valine. The later ascorbate-contamination result of Kuwahara et al. further weakens claims of very high amino-acid yields in some neutral-atmosphere experiments.

### Hydrothermal and serpentinization systems

The **Kaur et al. H₂/Ni chemistry** is especially attractive environmentally because it works at 22 °C and pH ~8 rather than requiring hydrothermal temperatures. H₂ is continuously generated by water–rock reactions such as serpentinization, and the authors note measured hydrothermal-fluid H₂ values of roughly 1–15 mmol kg⁻¹. Ni-bearing metal/mineral phases and meteoritic material offer plausible heterogeneous catalysts.

The difficulty is upstream carbon chemistry. Hydrothermal plausibility of H₂ and Ni does **not** establish a hydrothermal source of 30 mM α-ketoisovalerate. Kaur et al.'s coupled H₂ network makes several ketoacids from oxaloacetate/glyoxylate chemistry, but the valine-specific branched ketoacid was externally supplied. Thus the route currently has strong environmental support for the **reductive-amination step**, not for the complete valine synthesis.

FeS/Fe(OH)₂ reductive-amination chemistry is often associated with hydrothermal/mineral-surface models, but Huber & Wächtershäuser's own results caution against treating “FeS” as a generic catalytic label: freshly precipitated material worked substantially differently from other preparations, excess sulfide could suppress activity, increased ionic strength reduced yields, and several alternative metal sulfides failed. Those dependencies need to be confronted explicitly when extrapolating to seawater or vent fluids.

### Impact environments

Impact chemistry is geochemically attractive because it supplies both extreme energy and reducing power from Fe/Ni projectiles without demanding a globally reducing atmosphere. Takeuchi et al.'s 2020 ^13C-labeled experiment demonstrates this principle directly with CO₂/HCO₃⁻, N₂, water and meteoritic metals. But the realistic-feedstock experiment does **not yet reach valine**, whereas Bar-Nun et al.'s direct valine synthesis starts with methane, ethane and ammonia already in the gas phase.

Thus “impact synthesis of valine” currently joins two pieces of evidence that have not been experimentally unified: old direct valine synthesis under a strongly reduced gas mixture, and modern geochemically motivated synthesis of simpler amino acids from CO₂/N₂/Fe/Ni. Demonstrating valine in a ^13C/^15N-labeled CO₂/N₂ impact experiment would materially strengthen this framework.

### Meteorite parent bodies

Radiolytic and hydrothermal formaldehyde/ammonia chemistry has perhaps the clearest environmental case for an **extraterrestrial** contribution. Carbonaceous parent bodies experienced aqueous alteration, and ^26Al provides both heat and ionizing radiation. Kebukawa's γ-radiolysis experiments showed dose-dependent amino-acid synthesis, while Ishikawa et al. subsequently included valine among the minor products.

The caveat is that amino acids are generated as members of large, low-selectivity product suites, frequently after acid hydrolysis of complex precursor material. The concentration of formaldehyde, ammonia and related small organics in actual parent-body pore solutions, the extent of transport and destruction, and the quantitative contribution of γ radiation relative to ordinary thermal chemistry remain model-dependent. The 2017 Kebukawa hydrothermal experiment is particularly important as an analytical warning because its apparent valine was L-only and was judged likely contamination.

## Competing frameworks, evidence against them, and unresolved bottlenecks

The strongest general criticism that applies across the field is **precursor displacement**: a route can solve the final C–N bond formation while moving the harder carbon-skeleton problem upstream. The 2024 Ni/H₂ route is the clearest example. Once α-ketoisovalerate exists, valine formation at 22 °C is straightforward. But α-ketoisovalerate is already almost valine; the experimentally unresolved problem is the abiotic creation of its branched C5 skeleton in appreciable concentration from simpler C1–C3 compounds.

The converse problem affects Strecker chemistry. Its advantage is that it **actually constructs the extra carbon** of the amino-acid carboxyl group from HCN, and electric discharges can generate HCN and carbonyl compounds from small gases. But valine specifically demands isobutyraldehyde. Producing a broad soup of C1–C4 aldehydes is chemically easy compared with generating the branched aldehyde at a concentration high enough for valine to compete with glycine, alanine, AIB, norvaline and numerous non-protein isomers. Ring et al.'s broad product distribution illustrates that selectivity problem experimentally.

The **NH₄CN/acetone route** has a particularly unresolved carbon-accounting problem. Because ordinary Strecker chemistry of acetone should lead to AIB rather than valine, direct valine formation implies secondary C–C transformations in the cyanide polymerization network. Friedmann & Miller established the product, but the detailed sequence and its yield dependence on cyanide, ketone concentration, pH and temperature have never been developed into a modern isotope-resolved mechanism. Their failure to reproduce valine from NH₄CN alone also invalidates the simplest version of older cyanide-polymer claims.

The **formaldehyde/ammonia route** has an analogous mechanistic gap. It starts from admirably simple C1/N1 feedstocks but must execute several selective C–C couplings to reach a branched C5 skeleton. Fox & Windsor demonstrated valine among hydrolysis products, yet modern HCHO/NH₃ experiments yield complex polymeric material and broad amino-acid suites, and Kebukawa et al.'s hydrothermal analogue did not produce analytically secure abiotic valine. The exact branch-forming sequence remains unknown.

For **shock chemistry**, the principal controversy is geochemical rather than chemical. Valine is demonstrably made by shock-heating a CH₄/C₂H₆/NH₃ mixture, but Hadean mantle redox constraints do not support that mixture as a general background atmosphere. More realistic CO₂/N₂ impact chemistry works, but so far only for simpler amino acids.

The **Bucherer–Bergs α-ketoacid route** is chemically convincing but carries three distinct relevance questions: whether α-ketoisovalerate was available; whether cyanide and ammonia could coexist at useful concentrations; and whether the long room-temperature plus 80 °C processing sequence could occur without destroying other network components. Pulletikurti et al.'s optimized experiments used 0.5 M bicarbonate and comparatively concentrated substrates, making environmental concentration mechanisms an essential part of any proposed setting.

The **FTT framework** currently should not be promoted as a valine synthesis. Pizzarello's 2012 experiment is valuable because it uses exceptionally simple CO/H₂/NH₃ feedstocks and geologically relevant catalytic materials, but its 370 °C product suite contains AIB, norvaline and isovaline rather than valine. The strong production of AIB by magnetite shows that mineral surfaces can select branching patterns, but the selection is not the biological valine skeleton in that study.

There is also a broader **congruence-with-biochemistry debate**. Strecker chemistry is efficient and geochemically versatile but has no direct analogue in extant amino-acid biosynthesis. α-Ketoacid reductive amination is structurally congruent with extant metabolism, which is one reason Kaur et al. emphasize it, but metabolic resemblance by itself does not establish historical continuity. A reaction network is compelling only if the required α-ketoacid itself can arise from geochemically available starting materials under compatible conditions.

Finally, there is the **chirality problem**. None of the strongest direct routes discussed here establishes a robust mechanism for generating protein-level L-valine enantiomeric excess from achiral feedstocks. Indeed, Kebukawa et al.'s observation of exclusively L-valine in an otherwise abiotic experiment was interpreted as a warning sign for contamination rather than as spontaneous enantioselection. For synthesis-network design, valine production and valine homochirality should therefore be treated as separate problems unless an explicit symmetry-breaking mechanism is demonstrated.

## Implications for synthesis-network design

The most productive network representation is to treat valine as having **two convergent immediate-precursor nodes**:

**Aldehyde branch**
\[
\mathrm{CH_4/CO/CO_2 \rightarrow C_1-C_3\ carbonyls \rightarrow
isobutyraldehyde}
\]
\[
\mathrm{isobutyraldehyde + NH_3 + HCN \rightarrow
valine\ aminonitrile \rightarrow valine}
\]

and

**α-ketoacid branch**
\[
\mathrm{small\ carbonyls/pyruvate \rightarrow branched\ C_5\ chemistry
\rightarrow \alpha\!-\!ketoisovalerate}
\]
\[
\mathrm{\alpha\!-\!ketoisovalerate + NH_3 + H_2
\xrightarrow[22\,^\circ C,\ pH\sim8]{Ni/Pd} valine}
\]

The second arrow in each branch is reasonably well supported; the first arrow is the strategic bottleneck. The Strecker branch has abundant experimental evidence for the general **HCN + aldehyde → aminonitrile → amino acid** machinery, but insufficient quantitative work on prebiotic formation of isobutyraldehyde. The α-ketoacid branch has excellent modern evidence for **α-ketoisovalerate → valine**, but currently lacks a comparably convincing abiotic synthesis of α-ketoisovalerate from pyruvate or simple C1–C3 feedstocks.

For experimental prioritization, the highest-value missing experiment would therefore not simply be “make valine again.” It would be an isotope-resolved synthesis of the **branched skeleton**. Particularly discriminating experiments would test whether plausible atmospheric, photochemical, mineral, impact or H₂-driven networks can generate ^13C-labeled isobutyraldehyde or α-ketoisovalerate from CO₂/CO/HCHO/pyruvate, followed *in the same reaction system* by capture as valine. The need for this follows directly from the precursor gaps in both the Miller/Ring and Kaur pathways.

A second priority is to quantify competition. In Kaur's α-ketoisovalerate experiment, geochemically attractive Ni and meteoritic metal make substantial **2-hydroxyisovalerate**, whereas Pd strongly favors valine. A realistic network therefore needs to model carbon partitioning between amino-, hydroxy-, decomposition- and adsorption products, not just report the presence of valine. Likewise, electric-discharge chemistry produces many constitutional isomers and non-protein amino acids.

A third priority is to introduce realistic **salinity, mineral aging and concentration cycling**. Huber & Wächtershäuser showed that ionic strength, FeS preparation and sulfide loading can change reductive-amination behavior dramatically, while Pulletikurti et al.'s Bucherer–Bergs chemistry uses much more concentrated bicarbonate, cyanide and organic substrate than would be expected in an unconfined ocean. Evaporation, freezing, adsorption, pore confinement or repeated wet–dry cycling therefore cannot merely be environmental decoration; where high concentrations are required, a quantitative concentration mechanism is part of the chemical hypothesis.

Overall, the **best-supported direct valine-forming chemical step under mild, geochemically interpretable conditions is presently α-ketoisovalerate reductive amination with H₂ over Ni-bearing or meteoritic material**, especially because Kaur et al. directly quantified valine/hydroxy-acid competition at 22 °C. The **best-supported route that constructs valine from substantially smaller organic molecules remains Strecker/discharge chemistry**, but its strongest demonstrations use reducing atmospheres whose global Hadean relevance is contested. **Parent-body γ chemistry and reduced-gas shock chemistry independently show that valine can also emerge from much less pathway-specific, energy-rich reaction networks**, though with low selectivity and substantial environmental qualifications. None of these results yet establishes a uniquely favored historical route.

## Primary literature cited

**Miller, Stanley L. (1957), *Biochimica et Biophysica Acta* 23, 480–489, “The mechanism of synthesis of amino acids by electric discharges.”** Established HCN/aldehyde/aminonitrile Strecker chemistry as the mechanism underlying much of electric-discharge amino-acid synthesis and found no substantial catalytic effect from ferrous ammonium sulfate.

**Friedmann, Nadav & Stanley L. Miller (1969), *Nature* 221, 1152–1153, “Synthesis of Valine and Isoleucine in Primitive Earth Conditions.”** Failed to confirm valine from NH₄CN polymerization alone but directly obtained valine when acetone was added.

**Bar-Nun, A.; Bar-Nun, N.; Bauer, S. H.; & Sagan, C. (1970), *Science* 168, 470–472, “Shock Synthesis of Amino Acids in Simulated Primitive Environments.”** Directly produced glycine, alanine, valine and leucine in a reduced gas mixture exposed to shock heating and rapid quenching.

**Fox, Sidney W. & Charles R. Windsor (1970), *Science* 170, 984–986, “Synthesis of Amino Acids by the Heating of Formaldehyde and Ammonia.”** Reported valine among amino acids obtained after hydrolysis of heated formaldehyde/ammonia reaction products.

**Ring, D.; Wolman, Y.; Friedmann, N.; & Miller, S. L. (1972), *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA* 69, 765–768, “Prebiotic Synthesis of Hydrophobic and Protein Amino Acids.”** Confirmed valine and a broad suite of hydrophobic amino acids by chromatography and GC–MS after electric discharge through methane/nitrogen/water with traces of ammonia.

**Huber, Claudia & Günter Wächtershäuser (2003), *Tetrahedron Letters* 44, 1695–1697, “Primordial Reductive Amination Revisited.”** Demonstrated FeS/Fe(OH)₂-mediated reductive amination of several α-ketoacids; important mechanistic precedent, but not a direct valine experiment.

**Cleaves, H. James; Chalmers, John H.; Lazcano, Antonio; Miller, Stanley L.; & Bada, Jeffrey L. (2008), *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres* 38, 105–115, “A Reassessment of Prebiotic Organic Synthesis in Neutral Planetary Atmospheres.”** Re-evaluated CO₂/N₂ discharge chemistry; relevant to atmospheric plausibility but not a direct valine demonstration.

**Trail, Dustin; Watson, E. Bruce; & Tailby, Nicholas D. (2011), *Nature* 480, 79–82, “The Oxidation State of Hadean Magmas and Implications for Early Earth's Atmosphere.”** Provided zircon-based evidence against persistently strongly reducing volcanic outgassing in much of the Hadean.

**Wang, Wei; Li, Qiliang; Yang, Bin; Liu, Xiaoyang; Yang, Yanqiang; & Su, Wenhui (2012), *Chemical Communications* 48, 2146–2148, “Photocatalytic Reversible Amination of α-Keto Acids on a ZnS Surface: Implications for the Prebiotic Metabolism.”** Demonstrated mineral-photocatalytic α-ketoacid amination, although not α-ketoisovalerate/valine.

**Pizzarello, Sandra (2012), *Meteoritics & Planetary Science* 47, 1291–1296, “Catalytic Syntheses of Amino Acids and Their Significance for Nebular and Planetary Chemistry.”** Demonstrated CO/H₂/NH₃ FTT-type amino-acid synthesis on meteoritic and mineral catalysts; notably produced AIB and isovaline but did not report valine.

**Kuwahara, Hideharu; Eto, Midori; Kawamoto, Yukinori; Kurihara, Hironari; Kaneko, Takeo; Obayashi, Yumiko; & Kobayashi, Kensei (2012), *Origins of Life and Evolution of Biospheres* 42, 533–541, “The Use of Ascorbate as an Oxidation Inhibitor in Prebiotic Amino Acid Synthesis: A Cautionary Note.”** Demonstrated that ascorbate can produce misleading apparent amino-acid signals in neutral-atmosphere synthesis experiments.

**Kebukawa, Yoko et al. (2017), *Science Advances* 3, e1602093, “One-pot Synthesis of Amino Acid Precursors with Insoluble Organic Matter in Planetesimals with Aqueous Activity.”** Produced extensive amino-acid precursor chemistry from formaldehyde/glycolaldehyde/ammonia but treated observed L-only valine as likely contamination.

**Takeuchi, Y.; Furukawa, Y.; Kobayashi, T.; Sekine, T.; Terada, N.; & Kakegawa, T. (2020), *Scientific Reports* 10, 9220, “Impact-induced amino acid formation on Hadean Earth and Noachian Mars.”** Demonstrated isotopically verified amino-acid formation from CO₂/N₂/H₂O plus Fe/Ni-bearing impact materials, but not valine.

**Kebukawa, Yoko (2022), *ACS Central Science* 8, 1664–1671, “Gamma-Ray-Induced Amino Acid Formation in Aqueous Small Bodies in the Early Solar System.”** Established dose-dependent amino-acid production by γ irradiation of aqueous formaldehyde/ammonia mixtures.

**Pulletikurti, Sunil; Yadav, Mahipal; Springsteen, Greg; & Krishnamurthy, Ramanarayanan (2022), *Nature Chemistry* 14, 1142–1150, “Prebiotic Synthesis of α-Amino Acids and Orotate from α-Ketoacids Potentiates Transition to Extant Metabolic Pathways.”** Established Bucherer–Bergs conversion of several α-ketoacids to amino acids via cyanide/ammonia chemistry; directly relevant mechanistically to α-ketoisovalerate but did not itself provide a valine-specific substrate experiment.

**Kaur, Harpreet; Rauscher, Sophia A.; Werner, Emilie; Song, Youngdong; Yi, Jing; Kazöne, Wahnyalo; Martin, William F.; Tüysüz, Harun; & Moran, Joseph (2024), *Chem* 10, 1528–1540, “A Prebiotic Krebs Cycle Analog Generates Amino Acids with H₂ and NH₃ over Nickel.”** Provides the strongest modern quantitative direct experiment for α-ketoisovalerate → valine using H₂ and Ni, Pd or meteoritic catalysts under mild aqueous conditions.

**Ishikawa, Akari; Kebukawa, Yoko; Kobayashi, Kensei; & Yoda, Isao (2024), *Life* 14, 103, “Gamma-Ray-Induced Amino Acid Formation during Aqueous Alteration in Small Bodies: The Effects of Compositions of Starting Solutions.”** Extended parent-body radiolysis experiments and reported valine among minor amino-acid products.
